Finding a Supervisor for MPhil MS: Step 2

🗓️ Sep 21, 2026⏱️ 17 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

Your shortlist from Step 1 named departments. Steps that come later in this series will name documents, funding and visas. But right now, in this article, finding a supervisor for MPhil MS becomes the single most important job in your application, because for a research degree the supervisor is not a detail, the supervisor is the admission. When an admissions committee reads your file, the question they ask first is not whether you wrote a strong essay, it is whether any faculty member wants to spend two years working with you. Every research based MPhil or MS application in Europe, the UK, and the research universities of Asia flows through a supervisor conversation before it flows through a portal. If you get this chapter right, your proposal lands on a desk where it is expected. If you get it wrong, the rest of your documents barely get read.

This is Step 2 in the six part series that carries you from first search to student visa, and this article assumes you already completed the shortlist from Step 1, because your supervisor search burns on the fuel of that earlier work. You are not hunting for a random professor; you are hunting for the three or four specific people whose research already overlaps your topic, inside the departments your shortlist already approved. Finding a supervisor for MPhil MS is therefore a research task, not a social one, and this guide turns it into a repeatable procedure with an email that professionals answer.

Keep your place in the series. The roadmap below is the order the whole pipeline runs in, and this article is Step 2, the step that turns your shortlist into a real research relationship. Each step links forward to the next.

You should already know from the previous article that a shortlist built on research fit makes supervisor outreach dramatically easier, because you are only ever emailing people whose work you can honestly reference. That honesty is the entire engine of this step. Every effective supervisor email in the world shares one property: it shows, in the first few lines, that the sender has genuinely read the professor's work. Professors receive hundreds of requests calling them brilliant; they are almost starved for emails that demonstrate actual reading. Your advantage in finding a supervisor for MPhil MS is effort, and effort is free.

Why the supervisor decides your MPhil or MS admission

It helps to understand the machinery you are negotiating with. In research based masters and doctoral admissions, the department does not admit a file so much as a supervisor agrees to take a student, and then the paperwork catches up. The professor cares about three things: whether your topic lives close enough to their expertise that they can actually guide it, whether your evidence of skill, grades, research experience, technical ability, matches the demand of the work, and whether you are plausibly someone who will finish, because a dropout costs the supervisor time, money and reputation. Your email and your proposal are your only instruments for answering all three questions before the formal application.

This is why finding a supervisor for MPhil MS comes before writing documents or chasing references. A supervisor who has agreed to consider you will then shape everything you write later. They will tell you how your proposal should be positioned inside their group, whether your draft is too ambitious for two years, and sometimes they will point you to the exact funding source their project sits under. That is why the order of this series is not bureaucratic tidiness, it is strategy. Students who skip this step and write a generic proposal to five universities get five generic rejections. Students who do the supervisor work first get documents that answer questions their readers actually have.

Finding a supervisor for MPhil MS: where to look first

Your search starts where your shortlist left off. For each university on your list, open the department research page and the staff listing, and collect the names of every faculty member whose research area touches yours, even tangentially. You are casting a wider net here than in Step 1, because you want three to five candidates per department, then you will narrow sharply. The staff page is only the beginning. Move next to each professor's personal research page if one exists, and then to their publication trail on Google Scholar and, where relevant, the repositories that host university research outputs. The goal of this first pass is one sentence per professor: their current focus, their recent productivity, and the probability they supervise masters students at all, because some professors only take doctoral students and some sit on research committees that never directly mentor anyone.

You should also look at the research groups and centres attached to each department, because funding and supervision often live at the group level rather than the individual level. A professor whose name appears on a funded centre, a grant call, or a recently advertised PhD position, is a professor with resources to attach to a student. Group pages frequently list current students and their projects, and that list is gold: it tells you the group's typical student count, whether the topic space is already crowded, and whether students at your exact level, masters as opposed to doctoral, are being taken on. While you read, keep a working note per group, because Step 3's proposal will need to echo the group's actual vocabulary.

Finally, widen slightly into the databases that index funded research and open positions. The site FindAPhD aggregates postgraduate positions across much of Europe and beyond, and while it is famous for doctorates it also carries masters level research opportunities and studentships, making it a legitimate place to see which groups advertise for new researchers. For Germany and much of Europe, the DAAD portal is an official and dependable starting point for both funding and admission routes at the research masters level. Treat these two as structured sources you can trust, then verify every claim you act on at the university's own page before you ever email a supervisor. Databases point; universities decide.

How to evaluate a supervisor's real fit

Collecting names is cheap. Evaluating them is the skill. You are weighing three dimensions, and you should be ruthless on all three. The first is thematic fit: is the professor actively working, in the last three years, on something close enough to your topic that your next two years of work would plausibly sit inside their agenda? Read their recent abstracts, not their CV. A research agenda moves, and a professor whose current grants are in climate modelling cannot supervise your deep learning thesis whatever their long ago title says. Distance matters: close enough that they know the literature, not so close that your topic already belongs to their current student.

The second dimension is capacity. Count their current students, their recent publication output, their administrative load, and whether they advertise for students at all. You can infer much from the group page and alumni records: a group producing steady masters graduates every year has a working supervision pipeline; a group whose last masters student finished four years ago is not currently absorbing new people. Emailing a full professor who supervises no students is as pointless as emailing no one.

The third dimension is style fit, which is harder to read and worth reading anyway. Skim their published writing and any public talks: do they write with a long indirect register or a direct one, do they hand work to junior collaborators or keep it, do their students appear as co authors early or late? Style fit predicts the texture of your next two years with them, and for many students it predicts completion better than topic fit does. No database will score this for you, so the reading you did earlier, the actual papers, is the only instrument. This is also the point where you quietly discard candidates, and discarding is a success: your final outreach list should be smaller, sharper, and entirely defensible.

The first email: structure that gets replies

Now you write. The first email to a prospective supervisor is short, concrete and reads like you spent an hour on it, because you did. A good length is between 120 and 200 words; anything longer gets skimmed, anything shorter feels copied. The structure, in order, looks like this. First, a subject line that names your business instantly, something like Application interest: Masters research on reconstruction algorithms, not a greeting and not the word Hello alone. Second, one sentence identifying yourself: your name, your current degree and institution, and the programme you are applying for. Third, the sentence that does all the work, the demonstration of reading: one specific sentence about their recent paper, their funded project, or their group's current direction, phrased in their vocabulary, ending with a clear statement of why that connects to your question.

Fourth, one paragraph stating your research interest and its link to their agenda, plus two lines of evidence for your ability: relevant coursework, a thesis, a technical skill, a project, an award. Fifth, the practical ask, and keep it small: you would be grateful for ten minutes to ask whether they are taking students for the coming cycle, and whether they would consider your proposed direction. Sixth, one line on logistics: when you intend to apply, and a sentence confirming you have attached a CV or two page summary. Finally, a sign off with your full name, university email or a functional professional email, and a direct link to anything relevant you want them to open. Do not attach the full proposal yet; the first email asks for the conversation, not for admission.

The templates give you the wording; this article gives you the strategy around it, and strategy is what separates an email that gets a reply from an email that gets archived.

Handling replies, silence and polite noes

You will email eight to twelve supervisors from a well built shortlist and you will get a spread of outcomes. Some reply within days. Some reply after three weeks because they answered it on the calendar block between meetings, and some never reply at all, which for most European and British professors is a working style rather than a judgement of you. There is a correct playbook for each outcome. When a professor replies positively, reply within forty eight hours, confirm the concrete next step, whether that is a call, sending the full proposal, or naming the right portal deadline, and ask if there is a word about timing they want respected. Then open a file for that conversation, because you now have a named human in your chain, and that file feeds Step 4's references and Step 5's funding emails.

When a professor replies with a polite no, perhaps they are not taking students, or they say your direction fits a colleague better, treat that as information, not rejection. Ask, in your thank you reply, whether they might suggest one or two colleagues whose current work resembles yours; academics know their own research space better than anyone, and a single such pointer is often worth a dozen cold emails. When you hear silence, follow up exactly once, after ten working days, with a two sentence bump that adds one new concrete detail or closes a deadline. Do not follow up a second time. If the silence persists, move to the next name on your list; your effort budget is real, and the next candidate wants your attention just as much.

There is a subtlety worth naming here because it changes your odds. Weekday mornings in the professor's timezone are the strongest window, and a Tuesday is kinder than a Monday. Rule out Friday afternoons and entire weekends. Match the professor's language register: if they write tersely, match that; if they write long and considerate, match that too. And never send the same template to two professors without individualising it, because academics trade notes in corridors and the identical email is the one story nobody forgets, in the bad way. Finding a supervisor for MPhil MS is a relationship game played with patience and evidence, and every rule in this section protects that relationship.

Questions to ask once a supervisor engages

The moment a supervisor gives you a real signal, whether a call, a reply asking for your proposal, or a question about your background, you enter the negotiation phase, and this phase deserves its own etiquette. Ask questions in groups of three, at most four, so the conversation does not feel like an interrogation, and ask them in order of importance. The first group is capacity and structure: are you taking students this coming cycle, what is the typical timeline, and does the thesis run through one project or several smaller ones? The second group is funding and resources: is there a stipend, assistantship, or fee waiver attached to this group, and if I bring my own scholarship (this is where you join hands with Step 5 later), is that workable? Third is the practical environment: lab access, equipment, datasets, expected working hours in the group, and whether you can work remotely if a visa delay pushes your start.

Read the answers for three signals. Enthusiasm: does the professor keep writing longer and more specific replies? Specifics: concrete names of grants, courses, or past students, as opposed to flavourless politeness. And honesty: a professor who tells you the funding is scarce now is worth more than one who promises the world in week one and discovers it in week twelve. Capture every concrete detail in your conversation file, because admission committees and funding boards downstream will literally ask you to name the supervisor and the project. You are not having a chat; you are collecting evidence that will be quoted.

One more question belongs in the very first positive exchange, and it is the one most students forget to ask: what should your research proposal emphasise to be competitive in this group? Ask it directly. The answer will tell you whether to lean into methodology, existing datasets, a specific case study, or a pilot experiment, and it hands you the blueprint for Step 3 on a plate. A supervisor who has told you what they want to read has already started supervising you, and that is precisely how you want to feel at the end of finding a supervisor for MPhil MS: like the relationship has begun, even before the portal does.

Reading a paper the way a candidate should

Everything in this step depends on one recurring skill that deserves its own section, because it is the skill students rush most. When you open a professor latest paper, you are not reading for pleasure or even fully for understanding; you are reading for usable evidence. The usable evidence is four things. First, the precise question the paper answers, because you want to name it. Second, the method, the dataset, and the tools, because mentioning the method, the cohort, or the programming stack proves your reading was technical, not topical. Third, the unanswered questions the paper itself hints at in its conclusion, and this is the richest seam in the whole exercise: the future work paragraph is a standing invitation, and quoting it back to the author is the single most flattering sentence you can write. Fourth, the vocabulary, the terms of art the group uses, because Step 3 proposal will be read more kindly if it sounds like it belongs to this family of papers.

Two or three papers per candidate is enough for email purposes. You are not writing a literature review; you are preparing a conversation the recipient can feel, and that feeling comes from specificity. If a paper sits behind a paywall, use the title and abstract plus the group page description, and say what you could see; an honest mention of the abstract and how your topic extends it works almost as well as citing the full text. Some students keep a one page reading log per candidate with these four points, and that log later becomes your proposal input and, remarkably often, the exact sentence a supervisor quotes back to you in reply, which is the moment they have joined the relationship. The effort you spend reading is the effort they feel.

The supervisor shortlist ledger

Just as Step 1 gave you a university shortlist, this step gives you a supervisor shortlist, and it deserves the same kind of instrument: a simple table. For each candidate you track the name, the university, the research area, the recent publication with a short note, the current student load if visible, the funding signal, the email status, and the next action. Status moves through candidate, reading, emailed, replied, engaged, and dropped. You will end with roughly eight to twelve candidates in the table, three to five engaged in serious conversations, and one or two who become your real supervisors once the offer lands. Keep the ledger to one page of columns and update it in one sitting per week, because an unmaintained table becomes a rumour within a fortnight and you will forget who said what in reply.

The ledger also protects you from the most expensive mistake in this step, which is putting all your hopes into one professor and one reply. Even a brilliant supervisor takes a holiday, a sabbatical, or a funding hit between your email and your follow up. A portfolio of conversations means no single outcome can break your application. And when you do get an engagement, move the conversation forward in the ledger the same session you receive it, because momentum in supervisor outreach decays faster than almost any other part of the application, and the professor who replies today expects the student who really wants the position to reply today too.

Special cases that change the search

Three situations deserve separate advice within finding a supervisor for MPhil MS. The first is the very new professor. A lecturer or assistant professor in their first years has little reputation but real capacity, often eager students, and frequently freshly showered grant money from a start up package. They supervise intensely because their own tenure case depends on published student work. For a masters candidate these are some of the best hidden supervisors in the system, and they are usually the most responsive set of emails you will send. Do not avoid the new face; target it.

The second special case is the cold market, where your topic is genuinely rare and only one or two people anywhere do it. Then your search widens to closely adjacent fields and to method: a supervisor who does not share your exact topic but shares your method, your dataset family, or your theoretical frame can still supervise you well, because method fit supervises almost as cleanly as topic fit. Adjust your email, lead with the shared method and acknowledge the topic distance honestly, and ask how they would frame the project inside their agenda. The single best supervisor for a rare topic is often the person a department would tell you to find, so make that department a tiny bit of help too.

The third special case is the interdisciplinary idea, which increasingly attracts funding but never owns a department. Here the winning move is to pick a primary home and a secondary ally, to email the professor in the department that will host you, and to mention the second professor you intend to involve. Committees worry about interdisciplinary students chewing up two groups of time without clear governance; a student who shows they understand the hosting structure solves the worry before it forms. Whatever your special case, the ledger, the reading discipline and the short first email do not change, they adapt, and an adaptive student is exactly the student a supervisor wants to supervise.

The outreach schedule and your attachment pack

Finding a supervisor for MPhil MS is a campaign, so give it a calendar. Plan to send your eight to twelve first emails across two windows of about five days each, staggered by timezone, so replies do not all land on the same exhausted afternoon, and so you are not waiting on a single batch. Send on weekday mornings in the supervisor own timezone, which for a Pakistani applicant means timing matters more than you think: a message that lands at their 9 in the morning has a different fate from one that lands at their 6 in the evening. After the first window, set a follow up alarm ten working days out for any candidate who stayed silent. And keep a personal deadline in your calendar about three weeks before your university application window opens, at which point you need at least two engaged supervisors or you go fishing again with fresh candidates.

Your attachment pack should be exactly three things, built once and reused everywhere. The first is a one page CV, not a five page academic history, with your education, your relevant courses, your research or project experience, your skills and tools, one or two honours, and a single line on your language ability. The second is a two page research interest summary that is emphatically not a full proposal: it states your topic, your question, why it matters, how it connects to the supervisor work, and a rough method in three lines. The third is nothing, because you are attaching only what was asked, and starting with the CV plus offering the summary on request keeps the first email light and the professor in control. A heavy attachment behaves like a stone in the mailbox: it stops the reply before it starts.

Keep the whole pack in one clearly named folder with your name in every filename, because professors forward interesting files to colleagues, and a file named cv.pdf that is indistinguishable from forty others will never be forwarded. The name pattern should always include your surname and the word research, for example the file called yourname research summary 2026. Small professional details of this kind tell a supervisor instantly whether they are dealing with a serious applicant or a scattergun one, and in a campaign of nine emails these details are the difference between being remembered and being filed.

Before you send anything, run one final quality check on every message. Read it aloud, and if a sentence or a phrase is comfortable in any of your other nine emails, rewrite it, because students copy winners and supervisors compare. Check that every name, university and paper title is spelled exactly as it appears on the official page, because a misspelled supervisor name is the fastest single signature of a batch email there is, and check that your own details are accurate, your programme choice, your intake year, your degree title, because a supervisor who catches an error in the first sixty seconds will not believe the proposal later. One read aloud, one spelling pass, one accuracy sweep, and the campaign is ready to fly.

One final reassurance. A significant fraction of the most successful research applicants were turned down by their first two or three supervisors before landing the supervisor who shaped their career. The professor who says no is rarely judging you; they are protecting a pipeline, a project and a calendar. Every no is data, every silence is a door you can knock elsewhere, and the student who persists with the ledger, the reading and the honest first email is the student who eventually finds their fit. The search is not about winning every inbox; it is about winning the one who matches, and in staying this disciplined you have already done the hard part.

Common outreach mistakes that kill replies

Let us end the practical section with the errors that remove your email from consideration, so you can check your drafts against them. The first is the generic compliment, congratulating a professor on their work with nothing specific, which reads as spam to anyone with a full inbox. The second is the oversized ask, demanding a meeting, detailed feedback on a draft, or an admission decision in the opening email; supervisors grant megabytes of labour only in tiny increments, so move in small steps. The third is ignoring their research agenda, proposing a topic the professor has clearly left behind, which instantly signals that you did not read. The fourth is a wall of text, compound paragraphs with no structure; a busy supervisor stops after the first four lines, and your gold sentence must sit where they cannot miss it. The fifth is weak evidence, claiming enthusiasm without a single proof point, where a concrete detail about your thesis or skill would have done the work. And the sixth is the wrong format, an attachment that will not open, a reply-all over a group thread, or an email sent at a suspicious hour, all of which cost you points no follow-up can recover.

Notice that every one of these mistakes is a form of not showing effort, which is exactly why the strategy in this article beats every template in existence. The template proves you can write; only evidence proves you care. When you combine a genuinely read supervisor, a short structured email, and a concrete proof of your ability, you become rare in a professor's inbox, and rare is what gets answered. If you want the reference letters to arrive later in the process with real content, this is also where they are earned, because a professor who has personally engaged with you is a reference who can write a paragraph instead of a platitude, a point the dedicated reference letter guide on this site develops fully.

From the first reply to the next step

When you have two or three genuine supervisor relationships forming, you have fully completed Step 2, and you have accomplished something most applicants never reach: your application now moves with faces attached to it. Your next task is to turn the engagement into a proposal that the supervisor and their committee will recognise as theirs to judge, which is exactly the territory of Step 3. Take your conversation file, the notes on vocabulary, the funding hints, and the specific emphasis your supervisors asked for, and carry them into the research proposal article, which shows you how to structure a proposal that survives the committee. Read it with your notes open, and let the honesty of your supervisor conversations guide every section you write. The relationship you built in this step is the strongest asset in the rest of the series, and the series is only going to get smoother from here.

Frequently asked questions

How early should I contact a supervisor for an MPhil or MS?+

Six to nine months before your target start is typical, because supervisors plan their student intake around grant cycles and departmental budgets. Leaving the outreach to the last two months usually means the good supervisors are already committed or their funding is allocated.

What should I write in the first email to a professor?+

Keep it between 120 and 200 words: a clear subject line, one sentence identifying yourself, a specific proof that you have read their recent work, your research interest linked to their agenda, small evidence of your ability, one small ask, and a sign off with a CV link.

Why do professors not reply to my emails?+

Most silencing is about workload, not rejection. Follow up once after about ten working days with a short bump, then move on. Match their timezone weekday mornings and keep the message short, because a busy professor decides whether to reply in the first few lines.

Can I apply without speaking to a supervisor first?+

For truly taught and structured MS programmes, yes, and some universities say so explicitly. For research based MPhil and MS degrees, the supervisor conversation is normally part of admission, so skipping it sharply lowers your chances even where the portal technically allows submission.
