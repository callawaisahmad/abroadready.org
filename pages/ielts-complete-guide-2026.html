IELTS Complete Guide 2026: Modules, Scoring, Band Descriptions & Preparation

What Is IELTS?

IELTS stands for the International English Language Testing System. It is the world's most widely taken English proficiency test, used by millions of people every year to prove their English language ability for study, work and migration.

The test is jointly managed by three trusted organisations: the British Council, IDP: IELTS Australia and Cambridge Assessment English. Together, these three bodies develop, administer and oversee the test worldwide, ensuring consistent standards and fair assessment.

IELTS is accepted by more than 11,500 organisations across 140+ countries. These include universities, colleges, employers, immigration authorities and professional registration bodies. Whether you want to study at a UK university, migrate to Canada, work in Australia or register as a nurse in New Zealand, IELTS is one of the most recognised proof-of-English qualifications on the planet.

Over 3.5 million IELTS tests are taken each year, making it the most popular English language proficiency test globally. It is available in almost every country and is offered in two main formats: Academic and General Training. You also have the choice between a computer-delivered test and a paper-based test, depending on availability and your personal preference.

IELTS measures your ability across four core language skills: Listening, Reading, Writing and Speaking. You receive a band score for each module, and your overall score is the average of all four, reported on a scale from Band 0 to Band 9. This system gives universities and immigration authorities a detailed picture of your English ability, not just a single pass-or-fail result.

Whether you are a first-time test-taker or looking to improve a previous score, this guide covers everything you need to know about IELTS in 2026. We will walk through every module, explain how scoring works, list test fees for every major country, show you what scores you need for visas and university admissions, and give you a practical study plan.

IELTS Academic vs General Training

One of the first decisions you need to make is which version of IELTS to take. Both IELTS Academic and IELTS General Training test the same four skills — Listening, Reading, Writing and Speaking — but the content differs for two of those modules.

IELTS Academic

IELTS Academic is designed for people who want to study at university level or register as a professional in an English-speaking country. You should take Academic if:

  • You are applying for an undergraduate or postgraduate degree at an English-medium university.
  • You need to prove English proficiency for professional registration in fields like medicine, nursing, engineering or accounting.
  • You are applying for a research position or academic programme.
  • You need English for a medical or healthcare role in the UK, Australia, New Zealand or other English-speaking countries.

The Reading module in Academic uses three long passages taken from books, journals, newspapers and magazines. The texts are written for a general academic audience, and may include diagrams, graphs or charts that you need to interpret. The Writing Task 1 asks you to describe, summarise or explain visual information such as a chart, graph, table, diagram or map in at least 150 words. Writing Task 2 is an essay on a given topic.

IELTS General Training

IELTS General Training is designed for people who want to work, migrate or train in an English-speaking environment. You should take General Training if:

  • You are applying for a work visa (for example, UK Skilled Worker visa, Australia TSS visa).
  • You are applying for immigration or permanent residency (for example, Canada Express Entry, Australia General Skilled Migration).
  • You need to demonstrate basic English for a training programme or work placement.

The Reading module in General Training uses texts from everyday sources: advertisements, company handbooks, official documents, newspapers and books. These are the kinds of texts you would encounter in daily life and at work. Writing Task 1 asks you to write a letter — formal, semi-formal or informal — on a given topic in at least 150 words. Writing Task 2 is the same essay format as Academic.

What Stays the Same

The Listening module and the Speaking module are identical in both Academic and General Training. The difficulty level, number of questions and format are exactly the same. The only difference between the two versions of IELTS lies in the Reading and Writing modules.

This means that if you need to retake the test but want to try the other version, your Listening and Speaking scores should remain comparable. However, your Reading and Writing scores may differ because the content and task types are different.

Always check with your university or immigration authority which version they accept. Most universities require Academic, while most immigration authorities require General Training. Some accept both.

IELTS Test Format — Module by Module

Understanding the exact format of each IELTS module is critical for preparation. Here is a detailed breakdown of what to expect in each part of the test.

Listening Module (30 minutes + 10 minutes transfer time)

The Listening module is the same for both Academic and General Training. It lasts 30 minutes and consists of 4 sections with 40 questions total. You hear each recording only once.

Section 1 is a conversation between two speakers on an everyday topic — for example, a telephone booking, a hotel reservation or an enquiry about a service. This section tests your ability to understand factual information such as names, dates, numbers and addresses.

Section 2 is a monologue (one speaker) on an everyday topic — for example, a talk at a community centre, a tour guide's introduction or a workplace induction. You may be asked to match labels to a plan or map.

Section 3 is a conversation between two to four speakers in an academic or training context — for example, a tutor discussing an assignment with students or a group of students planning a project. This section tests your ability to follow opinions, attitudes and agreements.

Section 4 is a monologue on an academic subject — for example, a university lecture on climate change, history or science. This is the most challenging section and tests your ability to follow a complex academic argument.

Question types include: multiple choice, matching, plan/map/labelling, form completion, note completion, table completion, sentence completion and summary completion.

If you take the paper-based test, you get an additional 10 minutes at the end to transfer your answers to the answer sheet. If you take the computer-delivered test, your answers are entered directly into the computer and there is no extra transfer time.

Reading Module (60 minutes)

The Reading module lasts 60 minutes and consists of 3 passages with 40 questions total. There is no extra transfer time — you must write your answers on the answer sheet during the test.

In the Academic version, the three passages are taken from books, journals, magazines and newspapers. They are written for a general academic audience and cover a range of topics from science and technology to history and social issues. At least one passage contains a logical argument. Some passages include diagrams, graphs or charts.

In the General Training version, the three sections contain texts from everyday sources. Section 1 may include two or three short texts such as advertisements. Section 2 contains two shorter texts related to work — for example, a company handbook, job descriptions or training materials. Section 3 contains one long text on a topic of general interest, similar to a newspaper or magazine article.

Question types include: True/False/Not Given, Yes/No/Not Given, matching headings, matching information, matching features, matching sentence endings, sentence completion, summary completion, note completion, table completion, flow-chart completion, multiple choice, and diagram labelling.

Time management is crucial. Many test-takers spend too long on one passage and run out of time. A good strategy is to spend about 20 minutes per passage, and if a question is taking too long, mark your best guess and move on.

Writing Module (60 minutes)

The Writing module lasts 60 minutes and has two tasks. You should spend about 20 minutes on Task 1 and 40 minutes on Task 2. Task 2 carries more marks than Task 1, so many experts recommend doing Task 2 first.

Task 1 (Academic): You are given a chart, graph, table, diagram or map and asked to describe, summarise or explain the information in your own words. You must write at least 150 words. You should write an overview of the main trends, comparisons or features, then support this with specific details from the visual.

Task 1 (General Training): You are given a situation and asked to write a letter. The letter may be formal (to a manager or official), semi-formal (to a colleague or landlord) or informal (to a friend). You must write at least 150 words and cover all three bullet points provided.

Task 2 (both versions): You are given a topic and asked to write an essay of at least 250 words. The question may ask you to give your opinion, discuss both sides of an argument, explain causes and solutions, or analyse a statement. This task is worth approximately twice as much as Task 1.

Your Writing module is assessed on four criteria:

  • Task Achievement / Task Response — Did you answer the question fully and meet the word count?
  • Coherence and Cohesion — Is your essay logically structured with clear paragraphing and linking words?
  • Lexical Resource — How wide and accurate is your vocabulary?
  • Grammatical Range and Accuracy — How varied and correct are your sentences?

Key tips: always paraphrase the question in your introduction, write a clear thesis statement, support each body paragraph with examples, and conclude with a summary. Never copy words from the question — use synonyms and different sentence structures.

Speaking Module (11–14 minutes)

The Speaking module is a face-to-face interview with an examiner. It lasts 11 to 14 minutes and is divided into 3 parts. It is the same for both Academic and General Training.

Part 1 — Introduction and Interview (4–5 minutes): The examiner introduces themselves and asks you to introduce yourself. Then they ask you general questions on familiar topics such as your home, work, studies, hobbies, family and daily routine. This part is designed to help you relax and speak naturally.

Part 2 — Long Turn (3–4 minutes): You are given a cue card with a topic and three bullet points. You have 1 minute to prepare and make notes, then you must speak for 1 to 2 minutes without interruption. The examiner may ask one or two follow-up questions at the end. Common topics include a person you admire, a place you visited, a memorable event, a skill you learned or an object you own.

Part 3 — Discussion (4–5 minutes): The examiner asks you more abstract and complex questions related to the Part 2 topic. For example, if Part 2 was about a place you visited, Part 3 might ask about the impact of tourism on local communities or whether travel is essential for personal growth. This part tests your ability to express opinions, discuss ideas and give detailed, well-structured answers.

Your Speaking module is assessed on four criteria:

  • Fluency and Coherence — Can you speak at length without excessive pausing or self-correction?
  • Lexical Resource — How wide and accurate is your vocabulary?
  • Grammatical Range and Accuracy — How varied and correct are your sentences?
  • Pronunciation — Can the examiner understand you easily? Do you use appropriate stress and intonation?

Key tips: speak naturally and do not memorise scripted answers. Use a range of vocabulary and grammar structures. Give extended answers, not one-word responses. Ask the examiner to repeat a question if you do not understand it — this does not affect your score.

IELTS Band Scores — Complete Descriptions

IELTS uses a 0 to 9 band scale to report your score. Each of the four modules — Listening, Reading, Writing and Speaking — receives its own band score. Your overall score is the average of the four module scores, rounded to the nearest half band.

For example, if you score 6.5 in Listening, 7.0 in Reading, 6.0 in Writing and 7.0 in Speaking, your overall score would be (6.5 + 7.0 + 6.0 + 7.0) / 4 = 6.625, which rounds to 6.5.

Band 9 — Expert User

You have full operational command of the English language. Your use of English is appropriate, accurate and fluent, and you show complete understanding. Band 9 means you can function at the highest level in academic, professional and social settings without any difficulty. Very few test-takers achieve this band.

Band 8 — Very Good User

You have fully operational command of the language with only occasional unsystematic inaccuracies and inappropriacies. You may misunderstand some unfamiliar situations in complex language. Band 8 is excellent for competitive university admissions and professional registration.

Band 7 — Good User

You have operational command of the language, though with occasional inaccuracies, inappropriacies and misunderstandings in some situations. You generally handle complex language well and understand detailed reasoning. Band 7 is the most commonly required score for postgraduate programmes at top universities.

Band 6 — Competent User

You have an generally effective command of the language despite some inaccuracies, inappropriacies and misunderstandings. You can use and understand fairly complex language, particularly in familiar situations. Band 6 is the minimum accepted by many undergraduate programmes and immigration authorities.

Band 5 — Modest User

You have a partial command of the language, coping with overall meaning in most situations, though you are likely to make many mistakes. You should be able to handle basic communication in your own field. Band 5 may be accepted for some undergraduate programmes and basic immigration requirements.

Band 4 — Limited User

Your basic competence is limited to familiar situations. You frequently have problems with complex language. Band 4 is below the requirement for most university admissions and skilled immigration categories.

Band 3 — Extremely Limited User

You can only convey and understand general meaning in very familiar situations. There are frequent breakdowns in communication. Band 3 is not accepted for study or work purposes in most countries.

Band 2 — Intermittent User

You have great difficulty understanding spoken and written English. In very familiar situations, you can use individual words or short phrases to communicate basic needs. Band 2 is not useful for any official purpose.

Band 1 — Non-User

You have essentially no ability to use English beyond possibly a few isolated words. Band 1 is not accepted anywhere for study or immigration.

Band 0 — Did Not Attempt

You did not complete the test. Band 0 is assigned when a test-taker does not sit for one or more modules.

What Band Score Do You Need?

The band score you need depends entirely on your purpose:

  • Band 5.5 — Basic comprehension, acceptable for some foundation courses and basic visa requirements.
  • Band 6.0 — Adequate for most undergraduate programmes and many immigration pathways.
  • Band 6.5 — Minimum for most postgraduate courses and competitive immigration programmes.
  • Band 7.0 — Good for competitive university programmes, teacher training and healthcare roles.
  • Band 7.5–8.0 — Excellent for professional registration (medicine, law, nursing) and competitive scholarships.
  • Band 8.0–9.0 — Required for top-tier academic positions and the most competitive immigration points.

Remember that many institutions and immigration authorities require minimum scores per module, not just an overall score. For example, you might need an overall 6.5 with no band below 6.0. Always check the specific requirements for your programme or visa.

IELTS for UK Visa (UKVI)

If you are applying for a UK visa, you may need to take a specific version of IELTS called IELTS for UKVI (UK Visas and Immigration). This is different from the regular IELTS Academic or General Training test, though the test content is identical.

What Is IELTS for UKVI?

IELTS for UKVI is the only IELTS test accepted by UK Visas and Immigration for visa applications. It is a SELT (Secure English Language Test) approved by the UK government. The test is taken at a UKVI-approved test centre and includes additional security measures such as identity verification, finger-scanning and CCTV recording.

The test content is exactly the same as regular IELTS Academic or General Training. The difference is the test centre, security procedures and the way results are reported. Your TRF (Test Report Form) will indicate that you took the UKVI version.

When Do You Need IELTS for UKVI?

You need IELTS for UKVI if you are applying for:

  • Student Route Visa (formerly Tier 4) — to study at a UK university or college.
  • Skilled Worker Visa (formerly Tier 2) — to work for a UK employer.
  • Settlement (Indefinite Leave to Remain) — to permanently settle in the UK.
  • Family Visa — to join a partner or family member in the UK.

UKVI Band Requirements by Visa Type

  • Student Route Visa: You need CEFR level B2, which means a minimum of 5.5 in each band (Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking). Some universities may require higher scores — for example, 6.5 or 7.0 for postgraduate courses.
  • Skilled Worker Visa: You need CEFR level B1, which means a minimum of 4.0 in each band. Some roles, especially in healthcare and education, require higher scores.
  • Settlement: You need CEFR level B1 in Speaking and Listening. This can be proven with IELTS Life Skills B1.
  • Family Visa: You need CEFR level A1 (initial application) or A2 (extension). IELTS Life Skills A1 or A2 is accepted.

IELTS Life Skills

IELTS Life Skills is a separate test that only assesses Speaking and Listening. It is available at levels A1, A2 and B1. It is designed specifically for UK visa applicants who need to prove basic English for family, settlement or citizenship purposes. The test lasts 16–22 minutes and involves a face-to-face assessment with an examiner.

IELTS Life Skills is significantly shorter and simpler than full IELTS. It is not suitable for university admission or work visa applications that require full IELTS Academic or General Training.

If you are applying to study in the UK, you will almost certainly need IELTS for UKVI Academic. Always check with your university and the UKVI website for the latest requirements, as they can change.

IELTS Required Scores by Country

Different countries and institutions have different IELTS requirements. Here is a breakdown of what you need for the most popular study and immigration destinations in 2026.

United Kingdom

  • Student Route Visa: Minimum 5.5 per band (CEFR B2). This is the baseline for the visa itself, but universities often require higher.
  • University Admission: Undergraduate programmes typically require 6.0–6.5 overall. Postgraduate programmes typically require 6.5–7.0 overall. Competitive courses like medicine, law and teaching may require 7.0–7.5.
  • Skilled Worker Visa: Minimum 4.0 per band (CEFR B1) for most roles. Healthcare and teaching roles may require higher.

United States

  • F-1 Student Visa: There is no federal IELTS minimum for the F-1 visa itself. The requirement comes from the university you apply to.
  • University Admission: Most universities require 6.0–7.5 depending on the programme. Top universities may require 7.0+. Community colleges may accept 5.5+.
  • H-1B Work Visa and Green Card: IELTS is not required for H-1B or green card applications. Some employers may request English proficiency proof informally.

Canada

  • Student Direct Stream (SDS): Requires an overall score of 6.0 with no band below 6.0. SDS allows faster processing of study permit applications.
  • Express Entry: Requires Canadian Language Benchmark (CLB) levels. CLB 7 corresponds to IELTS 6.0 in each band. CLB 9 corresponds to IELTS 8.0 in each band. Higher CLB scores earn more Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) points.
  • University Admission: Undergraduate programmes typically require 6.0–6.5. Postgraduate programmes typically require 6.5–7.0.
  • Provincial Nominee Programmes (PNPs): Requirements vary by province, but most require CLB 4–7 depending on the stream.

Australia

  • Student Visa (Subclass 500): Requires an overall score of 6.0 with a minimum of 5.0 per band. Some universities require higher for specific programmes.
  • Graduate Visa (Subclass 485): Requires an overall score of 6.5 with a minimum of 6.0 per band (for the Post-Study Work stream).
  • General Skilled Migration: Requires a minimum of 6.0 in each band for most occupations. Some occupations require 7.0 or higher. IELTS scores contribute to your points test.
  • University Admission: Undergraduate programmes typically require 6.0–6.5. Postgraduate programmes typically require 6.5–7.0.

Germany

  • Student Visa: IELTS is not required for a German student visa itself. However, if your programme is taught in English, the university will require proof of English proficiency.
  • English-Taught Programmes: Most require an overall score of 6.0–6.5. Some top universities may require 7.0.
  • Work Visa: IELTS is not required for a German work visa. However, employers in English-speaking roles may request it.
  • Note: German-taught programmes require proof of German language proficiency (TestDaF or DSH), not IELTS.

New Zealand

  • Student Visa: Requires an overall score of 5.5–6.5 depending on the level of study.
  • Skilled Migrant Category: Requires an overall score of 6.5 or higher.
  • Accredited Employer Work Visa: Requires a minimum of 5.0 per band for most roles.

Ireland

  • Student Visa: Requires an overall score of 5.0–6.5 depending on the programme.
  • Work Permit: No official IELTS requirement, but employers may request it.

Singapore and Hong Kong

  • University Admission: Top universities typically require 6.5–7.5 overall.
  • Work Visa: Requirements vary by employer and role.

How to Prepare for IELTS

Good IELTS preparation is not about memorising answers — it is about building real English skills and understanding the test format. Here is a step-by-step guide to help you prepare effectively.

Step 1: Take a Diagnostic Practice Test

Before you start studying, take a full-length practice test under timed conditions. This gives you a baseline score and reveals which modules need the most work. You can download free practice tests from the official IELTS website, British Council or Cambridge.

Step 2: Identify Your Weakest Module

After the diagnostic test, look at your module scores. Most test-takers find Writing and Speaking the hardest to improve, so focus extra time on these. If Reading is your weakness, you likely need to build vocabulary and reading speed. If Listening is weak, you may need to practise with different English accents.

Step 3: Create a 6–8 Week Study Plan

A structured study plan is essential. Here is a sample weekly schedule:

  • Week 1–2: Learn the test format for all four modules. Do practice questions for each. Build vocabulary with topic-based word lists.
  • Week 3–4: Focus on your weakest modules. Do timed practice for Reading and Listening. Write Task 1 and Task 2 essays and get feedback.
  • Week 5–6: Do full mock tests under exam conditions. Practise Speaking with a partner or tutor. Review and learn from your mistakes.
  • Week 7–8: Do final mock tests. Focus on weak areas. Review vocabulary and grammar structures. Stay calm and confident.

Step 4: Use Official Materials

The best preparation materials come from the test makers themselves:

  • Cambridge IELTS books (volumes 14–19 for the latest practice tests)
  • British Council IELTS practice (free online resources and apps)
  • IDP IELTS practice (free practice tests and preparation tips)
  • IELTS.org official website (sample test questions and format guides)

Step 5: Build Daily English Exposure

Immerse yourself in English as much as possible. Listen to English podcasts (BBC Learning English, TED Talks, NPR). Read English newspapers and articles daily. Watch English movies and TV shows with subtitles. This builds listening comprehension, vocabulary and natural language patterns.

Step 6: Practise Speaking Regularly

Speaking is a skill that improves with practice. Find a study partner or hire a tutor for regular speaking practice. Record yourself answering Part 2 cue card topics and listen back. Focus on fluency, not perfection — natural conversation with occasional errors scores higher than robotic, memorised answers.

Step 7: Get Feedback on Writing

Writing is the module where self-study is hardest. Get your essays reviewed by a teacher, tutor or online IELTS writing feedback service. Focus on task response (answering the question), coherence (logical structure) and vocabulary variety. Practise writing under timed conditions — many test-takers fail to finish Task 2 because they spend too long on Task 1.

Step 8: Do Mock Tests Under Timed Conditions

In the final two weeks, do at least 3–4 full mock tests under strict timed conditions. Simulate the real test environment: no phone, no dictionary, no breaks. This builds stamina and helps you manage time effectively on test day.

IELTS One Skill Retake

IELTS One Skill Retake is a relatively new option that allows you to retake just one module — Listening, Reading, Writing or Speaking — without redoing the entire test. This is a cost-effective and time-saving option if you are happy with three of your module scores but want to improve one.

How It Works

After completing a full IELTS test, you can choose to retake one module within 60 days of your original test date. The new score for that module replaces your original score and is combined with your other three original module scores to give you a new overall band score.

Eligibility

You must have completed a full IELTS test (Academic or General Training) at an IELTS test centre that offers One Skill Retake. Not all test centres offer this option, so check availability before booking.

Cost

One Skill Retake typically costs 70–80% of the full test fee, making it significantly cheaper than retaking the entire test. This can save you both money and time.

When to Use One Skill Retake

One Skill Retake is most useful when:

  • Your three module scores meet the requirement but one module is below the minimum.
  • You ran out of time in Reading or Writing and want a second chance.
  • You were nervous during Speaking and did not perform at your best.
  • You want to improve your overall score by half a band without retaking everything.

Check the required scores for your target country to understand exactly what you need.

Common IELTS Mistakes to Avoid

Many test-takers lose marks not because of poor English but because of avoidable mistakes. Here are the top 10 mistakes and how to avoid them:

  1. Not managing time properly. Time pressure is one of the biggest challenges in IELTS. In Reading, many candidates spend too long on Passage 1 and run out of time for Passages 2 and 3. In Writing, some spend the entire hour on Task 1 and rush Task 2, which is worth more marks. Always practise with a timer and set strict time limits for each section.
  2. Not reading instructions carefully. The instructions specify exactly what form your answer should take — for example, "Write NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER." If you write three words, it is marked wrong even if your answer is correct. Read every instruction carefully.
  3. Writing Task 1 before Task 2. Task 2 is worth approximately twice as much as Task 1. If you are running low on time, it is better to spend the remaining time on Task 2. Many experts recommend doing Task 2 first to ensure you have enough time.
  4. Spending too long on one question. If a question is taking too long, mark your best guess and move on. You can always come back to it if you have time at the end. Getting stuck on one question can cost you marks on easier questions later.
  5. Not paraphrasing in Writing. Copying words directly from the question in your Writing response shows poor vocabulary. Always paraphrase using synonyms and different sentence structures. This demonstrates a wider vocabulary range.
  6. Using memorised answers in Speaking. Examiners are trained to detect memorised responses. If you sound robotic or recite prepared answers, your Fluency and Coherence score will suffer. Speak naturally and respond to the actual question asked.
  7. Not practising with a timer. Many test-takers practise without timing themselves and then struggle to finish on test day. Always time your practice sessions to build speed and accuracy.
  8. Ignoring weak modules. It is natural to practise what you are good at, but your overall score is the average of all four modules. Spending extra time on your weakest module can have the biggest impact on your overall score.
  9. Not reviewing mistakes. Doing practice tests without reviewing your answers is a wasted opportunity. Analyse every wrong answer and understand why you got it wrong. Keep a mistake log and review it regularly.
  10. Taking the wrong test. Applying for a university that requires Academic IELTS with a General Training score (or vice versa) means your result will not be accepted. Always check which version is required before booking.

Quick Reference: Key IELTS Facts at a Glance

Here is a summary of the most important facts about IELTS in 2026:

  • Test duration: 2 hours 45 minutes (plus Speaking, which may be on a different day).
  • Number of questions: 121 across all four modules (40 Listening + 40 Reading + 2 Writing tasks + 3 Speaking parts).
  • Score range: Band 0 to Band 9, in half-band increments.
  • Score validity: 2 years from the test date.
  • Test versions: Academic and General Training.
  • Test formats: Computer-delivered and paper-based.
  • Retake policy: No waiting period; unlimited retakes.
  • One Skill Retake: Available within 60 days of original test.
  • Results timeline: 3–5 working days (computer), 13 days (paper).
  • Test frequency: Multiple dates per month at most test centres.
  • Organisations accepting IELTS: 11,500+ in 140+ countries.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between IELTS Academic and General Training?+

IELTS Academic is for university study, professional registration and research positions. IELTS General Training is for work visas, immigration and PR applications. Listening and Speaking are identical in both versions. Reading and Writing differ: Academic uses academic texts and data interpretation, while General Training uses everyday texts and letter writing.

What is a good IELTS score?+

Band 7.0 is considered a good score for most university programmes. Band 6.5 is the minimum for many postgraduate courses. Band 6.0 is acceptable for undergraduate programmes. Band 8.0+ is required for competitive programmes in medicine, law and teaching. For immigration, scores vary by country: Canada Express Entry needs CLB 7-9 (IELTS 6.0-8.0).

How is IELTS scored?+

IELTS uses a 0-9 band scale. Each module (Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking) receives a band score. Your overall score is the average of the four module scores, rounded to the nearest half band. For example, if you score 6.5, 7.0, 6.0 and 7.0, your overall is 6.5.

How long does IELTS take?+

The total test duration is 2 hours 45 minutes: Listening 30 minutes + 10 minutes transfer time, Reading 60 minutes, Writing 60 minutes, Speaking 11-14 minutes (may be on the same day or up to 7 days before/after the other modules).

How much does IELTS cost in 2026?+

Fees vary by country: India INR 17,000-18,500, Pakistan PKR 53,000-62,000, UK GBP 215-235, USA US$235-270, Canada CAD 319-340, Australia AUD 485-525, Germany EUR 260. Computer-delivered tests may cost slightly more.

How long are IELTS scores valid?+

IELTS scores are valid for 2 years from the test date. Universities, immigration authorities and professional bodies almost always require results within the last 2 years. Some institutions accept results within 12-24 months depending on their policy.

Can I retake IELTS if I fail?+

Yes. You can retake IELTS as many times as you want. There is no waiting period between tests. You can also use One Skill Retake to resit a single module (Listening, Reading, Writing or Speaking) without retaking the full test. Results from One Skill Retake are combined with your original test.

What is IELTS One Skill Retake?+

IELTS One Skill Retake lets you retake just one module (Listening, Reading, Writing or Speaking) if you are not satisfied with your score in that module. You can retake within 60 days of your original test. The new score replaces the old one and combines with your other three original module scores.

What is the difference between IELTS for UKVI and regular IELTS?+

IELTS for UKVI (UK Visas and Immigration) is the only IELTS version accepted for UK visa applications. It is taken at a UKVI-approved test centre with additional security. Regular IELTS (Academic or General Training) is for university admissions and non-UK immigration. The test content is identical.

How do I prepare for IELTS?+

Start with a practice test to identify your weak modules. Use official IELTS preparation materials from Cambridge, British Council and IDP. Focus on timed practice, build daily English exposure through podcasts and reading, and practise speaking with a partner or tutor. A 6-8 week structured study plan is recommended.
