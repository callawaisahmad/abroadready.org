TOEFL iBT Complete Guide 2026: Sections, Scoring & Preparation

📅 Aug 17, 2026⏱️ 28 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

What Is TOEFL iBT?

The TOEFL iBT (Test of English as a Foreign Language, Internet-Based Test) is the world's most widely accepted English proficiency examination for academic purposes. Created and administered by ETS (Educational Testing Service), the TOEFL iBT measures your ability to use and understand English at the university level. It evaluates how well you combine your listening, reading, speaking and writing skills to perform academic tasks.

Unlike general English tests, the TOEFL iBT is designed specifically for academic English. The reading passages come from university textbooks. The lectures mirror real classroom discussions. The writing tasks require you to synthesise information from multiple sources. This means the test prepares you for the actual language demands of studying abroad.

The TOEFL iBT is accepted by over 12,000 institutions in more than 160 countries. It is the most widely accepted English test in the United States, where it is preferred by virtually every major university. It is also accepted by universities in the UK, Canada, Australia, Germany, and across Asia and the Middle East. Whether you are applying to MIT, the University of Toronto, or the University of Melbourne, a TOEFL score will be accepted.

You can take the TOEFL iBT at a test centre or from your own home through the TOEFL iBT Home Edition. Both versions are identical in content, format, difficulty and scoring. The Home Edition uses AI-based proctoring with human review to ensure test integrity. This gives you flexibility in how and where you take the test.

In 2023, ETS significantly shortened the test from over 3 hours to approximately 2 hours. They removed the unscored experimental section, reduced the number of questions in some sections, and streamlined the overall experience. The shorter test is less tiring and more focused, which helps test-takers perform at their best.

The TOEFL iBT is scored on a scale of 0 to 120, with each of the four sections receiving a score of 0 to 30. There is no pass or fail. Instead, each university sets its own minimum score requirement. A score of 80 is generally considered the minimum for most universities, while competitive programmes often require 100 or higher.

One of the biggest advantages of the TOEFL iBT is the MyBest Scores feature. This allows universities to see your highest section scores from all your test attempts within the last two years, giving you the best possible composite score. This means retaking the test can only help you, never hurt you.

If you are planning to study abroad in English, the TOEFL iBT is one of the strongest choices you can make. It is rigorous, it is respected, and it opens doors at thousands of universities worldwide. In this guide, we will walk you through every aspect of the test — from the format and scoring to fees, preparation strategies and country-specific requirements.

TOEFL Test Format: Complete Section-by-Section Breakdown

The TOEFL iBT consists of four sections: Reading, Listening, Speaking and Writing. The entire test takes approximately 2 hours to complete. There are no breaks between sections, so you need to pace yourself from start to finish. Here is a detailed look at each section.

Reading Section (20 minutes)

The Reading section is the first part of the test. It lasts 20 minutes and includes 2 academic passages with 10 questions each, giving you a total of 20 questions. The passages are taken from university-level textbooks across various disciplines — science, history, social science and the humanities.

Each passage is approximately 700 words long. You will not need any prior knowledge of the topic. All the information you need to answer the questions is contained within the passage itself. However, the passages are dense and use academic vocabulary, so strong reading skills are essential.

The question types in the Reading section include:

  • Factual Information: Identify specific details stated in the passage
  • Negative Factual Information: Find what is NOT stated or is incorrect
  • Inference: Determine what the author implies but does not directly state
  • Rhetorical Purpose: Understand why the author includes specific information
  • Vocabulary: Determine the meaning of words based on context
  • Reference: Identify what pronouns or phrases refer to
  • Sentence Insertion: Place a given sentence into the passage where it fits best
  • Prose Summary: Select the sentences that best summarise the passage

Time management is critical in this section. With 20 minutes for 20 questions across 2 passages, you have roughly 1 minute per question. Practise reading academic texts quickly and identifying key information without re-reading entire paragraphs.

Listening Section (28 minutes)

The Listening section lasts 28 minutes and tests your ability to understand spoken English in academic settings. It includes 3 lectures (each 3 to 5 minutes long with 6 questions each) and 2 conversations (each about 3 minutes long with 5 questions each). This gives you a total of 28 questions.

The lectures simulate university classroom presentations. A professor discusses a topic in detail, often with student participation. The conversations take place between two people — typically a student and a professor, or a student and a university staff member — discussing academic or campus-related topics.

You are allowed to take notes during this section. In fact, note-taking is strongly recommended. The audio is played only once, so you must listen carefully and jot down key points, examples, and the structure of the lecture or conversation. Your notes will be essential when answering the questions.

The question types include:

  • Gist-Content: What is the main idea of the lecture or conversation?
  • Gist-Purpose: Why is the speaker saying this?
  • Detail: What specific information is mentioned?
  • Function: What does the speaker mean by a particular statement?
  • Attitude: What is the speaker's opinion or feeling?
  • Organisation: How is the lecture or information structured?
  • Connecting Content: How do different ideas relate to each other?

Accent variety is important to note. You will hear North American, British and Australian accents in the Listening section. All speakers speak at a natural pace, so practising with a range of English accents will help you prepare effectively.

Speaking Section (16 minutes)

The Speaking section is the shortest part of the test, lasting just 16 minutes. It consists of 4 tasks that measure your ability to speak English in academic and campus situations. Your responses are recorded on computer and scored by a combination of AI scoring and human raters.

Task 1 — Independent (Personal Preference): You will read a question about a familiar topic and have 15 seconds to prepare and 45 seconds to speak. For example: "Do you prefer to study alone or in a group? Explain why." This task tests your ability to express and support a personal opinion.

Task 2 — Integrated (Read + Listen + Speak): You will read a short passage (45 seconds), listen to a conversation (about 1 minute), and then have 30 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to speak. You must summarise the relationship between the reading and the listening. This tests your ability to integrate information from two sources.

Task 3 — Integrated (Listen + Speak): You will listen to a short lecture (about 1 minute) and have 20 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to speak. You must summarise the main points and explain how they support a concept. This tests your academic listening and speaking skills.

Task 4 — Integrated (Listen + Speak): Similar to Task 3, but with a longer lecture (about 2 minutes). You have 20 seconds to prepare and 60 seconds to speak. This is generally considered the most challenging task because of the amount of information you must process and summarise quickly.

For the best results, practise speaking clearly and at a natural pace. Use structured templates for your responses — for example, start with a clear statement, provide supporting details, and end with a brief conclusion. This helps you stay organised and ensures you cover all the key points within the time limit.

Writing Section (29 minutes)

The Writing section lasts 29 minutes and includes 2 tasks. This section was significantly updated in 2023 when the independent essay was replaced with the new Academic Discussion task.

Task 1 — Integrated (Read + Listen + Write) — 20 minutes: You will read a passage (about 250 words) and listen to a lecture (about 2 minutes) on the same topic. You then have 20 minutes to write a response that summarises the points from the lecture and explains how they relate to the reading. Your response should be 150 to 225 words.

This task tests your ability to synthesise information from written and spoken sources. You do not need to express a personal opinion. Instead, you must accurately report the key points and show the connections between the reading and the lecture.

Task 2 — Academic Discussion (Respond to a Professor's Question) — 10 minutes: You will read a professor's question and two student responses in an online discussion forum. You then have 10 minutes to write a response that contributes to the discussion. Your response should be at least 120 words.

This task replaced the old independent essay in July 2023. It requires you to state and support an opinion with specific reasons and examples. You are graded on how well you develop your ideas, use grammar and vocabulary correctly, and follow academic writing conventions.

The Writing section is scored by both AI scoring and human raters. AI evaluates your grammar, vocabulary and organisation, while human raters assess the quality of your ideas and the overall effectiveness of your response.

TOEFL Scoring: How Your Score Is Calculated

TOEFL iBT scores range from 0 to 120. Each of the four sections — Reading, Listening, Speaking and Writing — is scored on a scale of 0 to 30. Your total score is the sum of all four section scores.

Here is how the score ranges are generally interpreted:

  • 0–55: Below Basic — limited ability to use English
  • 56–79: Basic — some ability but significant limitations
  • 80–100: Intermediate — functional academic English ability
  • 101–120: Advanced — strong academic English ability

Reading and Listening are scored based on the number of questions you answer correctly. There is no penalty for wrong answers, so you should always attempt every question. The raw score (number correct) is converted to a scaled score of 0 to 30 using a statistical process that accounts for differences in difficulty across test forms.

Speaking is scored on a scale of 0 to 4 for each of the 4 tasks. These raw scores are then converted to a scaled score of 0 to 30. Raters evaluate your responses based on delivery, language use and topic development. AI scoring is used as a first pass, with human raters reviewing scores that fall near the boundaries.

Writing is scored on a scale of 0 to 5 for each task. These raw scores are converted to a scaled score of 0 to 30. For the Integrated task, raters look at how accurately you report the lecture points and how well you connect them to the reading. For the Academic Discussion task, they assess your contribution quality, organisation and language use.

TOEFL vs IELTS Score Comparison

Many students want to know how TOEFL scores compare to IELTS. While the two tests are different in format, ETS provides an official comparison:

  • TOEFL 60–78 ≈ IELTS 5.5–6.0
  • TOEFL 79–93 ≈ IELTS 6.0–6.5
  • TOEFL 94–101 ≈ IELTS 7.0
  • TOEFL 102–110 ≈ IELTS 7.5
  • TOEFL 111–120 ≈ IELTS 8.0–9.0

These are approximate equivalents. Some universities accept both tests, while others may have different minimum requirements for each. Always check the specific requirements of your target universities before deciding which test to take. For a detailed comparison, see our guide on TOEFL vs IELTS.

It is important to note that there is no overall pass or fail for the TOEFL. Each university sets its own minimum score. Some require a minimum total score, while others also set minimum scores for individual sections. Always check both the total and section requirements for your target programmes.

TOEFL MyBest Scores: Your Superscore Explained

MyBest Scores is one of the most valuable features of the TOEFL iBT. Also known as a superscore, MyBest Scores combines your highest section scores from all TOEFL iBT attempts taken within the last 2 years into a single composite score.

Here is how it works. Suppose you take the TOEFL three times. On your first attempt, you score 24 in Reading, 22 in Listening, 23 in Speaking and 25 in Writing (total: 94). On your second attempt, you score 27 in Reading, 24 in Listening, 21 in Speaking and 23 in Writing (total: 95). On your third attempt, you score 25 in Reading, 26 in Listening, 25 in Speaking and 24 in Writing (total: 100).

Your MyBest Score would take the highest from each section: 27 (Reading) + 26 (Listening) + 25 (Speaking) + 25 (Writing) = 103. This is 9 points higher than your best single-test score of 100.

MyBest Scores are automatically included on your TOEFL score report. You do not need to request them separately. When you send your scores to universities, the report includes both your individual test scores and your MyBest composite score.

Many universities now accept MyBest Scores for admissions. However, some institutions still require scores from a single test sitting. Always check with your target universities to confirm whether they accept MyBest Scores.

The strategic implication is clear: retaking the TOEFL can only help you. Since MyBest Scores takes your best section from each attempt, there is no risk of a lower score on one section pulling down your overall result. If you have time and budget, taking the test two or three times can significantly improve your MyBest composite.

MyBest Scores also apply retroactively. If you took the TOEFL before the feature was introduced, your earlier scores (within the 2-year validity window) are included in your MyBest calculation. This means past test-takers benefit from the feature without needing to retake the test.

TOEFL Home Edition: Take the Test From Home

The TOEFL iBT Home Edition is the same test as the centre-based TOEFL, but you take it from your own home on your own computer. It was introduced during the COVID-19 pandemic and has become a permanent option due to its popularity and convenience.

The content, format, difficulty, and scoring are identical to the test centre version. Universities cannot distinguish between a Home Edition score and a centre-based score. Your score report does not indicate which version you took.

System requirements for the Home Edition include:

  • A desktop or laptop computer (tablets and mobile phones are not allowed)
  • A camera (built-in or external) that can be moved to show your full desk area
  • A microphone (built-in or external)
  • Speakers (built-in or external — headphones are not allowed)
  • A stable internet connection (at least 2 Mbps recommended)
  • A private, well-lit room with a clear desk and no other people

The test is proctored using a combination of AI-based monitoring and human review. Before the test begins, a proctor verifies your identity and inspects your room using your camera. During the test, AI monitors your eye movements, head position and background for any suspicious activity. Human proctors review flagged incidents after the test.

You cannot use any unauthorized materials during the test — no phones, notes, textbooks, or second monitors. The proctor may ask you to re-inspect your room at any point during the test. Violations can result in score cancellation.

Booking the Home Edition is straightforward. When you register for the TOEFL on the ETS website, you can choose between "TOEFL iBT" (test centre) and "TOEFL iBT Home Edition." Test dates and times are available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, giving you much more flexibility than test centres.

Tips for home testing: Test your equipment (camera, microphone, speakers and internet) at least a day before the test. Choose a quiet room where you will not be disturbed. Ensure good lighting so the proctor can see you clearly. Close all unnecessary applications on your computer to prevent technical issues. Have a valid passport or ID ready for identity verification.

The Home Edition is available in most countries. However, there are some restrictions in certain regions. Check the ETS website for availability in your country. For a detailed comparison of test options, see our PTE vs IELTS vs Duolingo comparison guide.

Remember that the TOEFL fee is an investment in your future. A strong TOEFL score can lead to university admissions, scholarships and career opportunities that far exceed the initial cost of the test. Budget for the test fee early in your study-abroad planning process.

TOEFL Required Scores by Country and University

TOEFL score requirements vary widely depending on the country, university and programme. Here is a comprehensive overview of what you can expect across major study destinations.

United States

The USA is the largest market for TOEFL, and the test is accepted by virtually every American university. Most universities require a total score of 80 to 100. Competitive programmes at top universities typically require 100 to 110.

Specific examples include:

  • Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT): Minimum 100 (no section minimums specified)
  • Stanford University: Minimum 100 for most programmes
  • Harvard University: Minimum 100, with some programmes requiring higher
  • University of California, Berkeley: Minimum 90 for most graduate programmes
  • University of Texas at Austin: Minimum 79 for most programmes
  • Arizona State University: Minimum 61 for undergrad, 80 for graduate

There is no federal visa requirement for a specific TOEFL score in the USA. However, the student visa (F-1) requires proof of English proficiency, and a TOEFL score is the most commonly accepted document for this purpose. Some universities accept a score of 80 for conditional admission with additional English support.

United Kingdom

The UK accepts TOEFL scores for student visa (Tier 4/Student Route) purposes, provided the test is taken at a test centre. The Home Edition is also accepted when taken from a test centre environment. The UK Visas and Immigration (UKVI) body recognises TOEFL iBT as a Secure English Language Test (SELT) for certain visa categories.

Most UK universities require a score of 80 to 100. Some universities also require minimum scores in individual sections, particularly Speaking and Writing.

  • University of Oxford: Standard: 100 (with minimum 22 in each section)
  • University of Cambridge: Minimum 110 (with minimum 25 in each section)
  • Imperial College London: Minimum 92 (with minimum 20 in each section)
  • University College London (UCL): Standard: 92 (with minimum 24 in each section)
  • University of Edinburgh: Minimum 92 (with minimum 20 in each section)

Always check whether your target university has section-specific requirements. A strong overall score does not guarantee admission if your individual section scores fall below the minimum.

Canada

TOEFL is accepted by most Canadian universities. Typical requirements range from 80 to 100. However, there is an important exception: the Student Direct Stream (SDS) programme, which provides faster processing for study permits, requires IELTS specifically. If you are applying through SDS, you will need IELTS rather than TOEFL.

  • University of Toronto: Minimum 89 (with minimum 21 in each section)
  • University of British Columbia (UBC): Minimum 90
  • McGill University: Minimum 86 (with minimum 20 in each section)
  • University of Alberta: Minimum 86
  • University of Waterloo: Minimum 90

Australia

Australian universities generally accept TOEFL scores in the range of 79 to 93 for undergraduate and postgraduate programmes. Australia also accepts TOEFL for student visa purposes, though IELTS is more commonly used.

  • University of Melbourne: Minimum 79 (with minimum 13 in Writing, 18 in Speaking, 13 in Reading, 13 in Listening)
  • University of Sydney: Minimum 85 (with minimum 19 in Writing, 17 in Speaking, 17 in Reading, 17 in Listening)
  • Australian National University (ANU): Minimum 80 (with minimum 20 in each section)
  • University of Queensland: Minimum 87

Germany

Germany accepts TOEFL scores for English-taught programmes at public and private universities. Requirements typically range from 80 to 100. German-taught programmes usually require TestDaF or DSH instead.

  • Technical University of Munich: Minimum 88
  • Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich: Minimum 80
  • Heidelberg University: Minimum 90
  • RWTH Aachen: Minimum 90

How to Prepare for TOEFL: A 6-8 Week Study Plan

Preparing for the TOEFL iBT requires a structured approach. Here is a proven 6-8 week study plan that covers all four sections and builds the skills you need for a high score.

Week 1: Diagnostic and Foundation

  • Take a full-length practice test under timed conditions. Use the free practice test on the ETS website or purchase TOEFL Practice Online (TPO).
  • Identify your weakest section. This is where you should focus most of your energy.
  • Start building your academic vocabulary. Use flashcard apps like Anki or Quizlet with TOEFL word lists.
  • Begin reading academic articles daily from sources like Scientific American, National Geographic or university websites.

Weeks 2-3: Section-Specific Practice

  • Reading: Practise reading academic passages under timed conditions. Focus on identifying main ideas, supporting details and inferences. Review unfamiliar vocabulary.
  • Listening: Listen to academic lectures on YouTube, TED Talks and Coursera. Take notes while listening and practise summarising what you heard.
  • Speaking: Record yourself answering TOEFL Speaking questions. Listen back and evaluate your clarity, fluency and organisation. Practise using templates.
  • Writing: Write practice responses for both the Integrated and Academic Discussion tasks. Time yourself strictly. Review grammar and vocabulary errors.

Weeks 4-5: Intensive Practice and Refinement

  • Take at least 2 full-length practice tests under strict timed conditions.
  • Review every wrong answer and understand why you got it wrong.
  • Focus on note-taking strategies for Listening and Speaking.
  • Practise the integrated tasks extensively. These are the most challenging because they combine multiple skills.
  • Build speed in reading. Aim to finish each passage in 8-9 minutes.

Weeks 6-7: Test Simulation and Final Review

  • Take 2 more full-length practice tests. Simulate real test conditions — no pauses, no phone, no distractions.
  • Review your scores and identify any remaining weak areas.
  • Practise time management. If you consistently run out of time in one section, work on pacing strategies.
  • Review your Speaking recordings and Writing samples. Compare them to high-scoring examples.

Week 8: Final Preparation

  • Do light review only. Do not try to learn new material.
  • Review your vocabulary flashcards and common grammar mistakes.
  • Take one final practice test early in the week to build confidence.
  • Prepare your test day logistics: passport/ID, equipment check (for Home Edition), test centre location, arrival time.
  • Get a good night's sleep before the test.

Use the TOEFL TestReady platform, which is ETS's free practice platform. It provides personalised feedback, practice questions and study recommendations based on your performance. This is one of the most underutilised resources available to TOEFL candidates.

Common TOEFL Mistakes to Avoid

Many test-takers lose points not because of poor English, but because of avoidable mistakes. Here are the most common errors and how to avoid them.

1. Not managing time effectively. The TOEFL is a timed test. Many students spend too long on difficult questions and run out of time for easier ones. If you do not know the answer after 30 seconds, make your best guess and move on. You can always return to flagged questions in the Reading section if time permits.

2. Not taking notes during Listening. The audio is played only once. Without notes, you will struggle to answer questions about details, speaker attitudes and lecture organisation. Develop a personal note-taking shorthand that is fast and clear.

3. Speaking too softly or too quickly. The microphone picks up your voice, but mumbling or rushing makes it hard for raters to understand you. Speak at a natural, conversational pace. Enunciate clearly. Practise speaking for 45 to 60 seconds without rushing.

4. Not using templates for Writing and Speaking. Templates give you a structure to follow, which saves time and keeps your response organised. For Writing, use a clear introduction, body paragraphs and conclusion. For Speaking, start with your main point, provide supporting details and end with a brief summary.

5. Reading too slowly in the Reading section. You do not need to understand every word. Focus on skimming for main ideas and scanning for specific details. Practise reading academic passages at a faster pace without sacrificing comprehension.

6. Not practising integrated tasks. The integrated tasks in Speaking and Writing are the most challenging because they require you to process information from multiple sources simultaneously. Practise these tasks regularly, not just the independent ones.

TOEFL Score Validity and Sending Scores

TOEFL scores are valid for 2 years from the test date. After 2 years, scores are removed from the ETS system and cannot be reported. If you need your scores after they expire, you must retake the test.

When you register for the TOEFL, you can send up to 4 score reports for free to universities of your choice. You select these institutions during registration or up to 24 hours after your test. Additional score reports cost USD 20 each.

Scores are typically available 6 to 10 days after your test date. You can view them online through your ETS account. Official score reports are sent to universities electronically, which is faster and more reliable than postal mail.

If you took the Home Edition, the process for viewing and sending scores is identical to the test centre version. There is no separate score report for the Home Edition.

TOEFL vs Other English Tests

The TOEFL iBT is not the only English proficiency test available. Here is a quick comparison with other popular options.

TOEFL vs IELTS: TOEFL is more widely accepted in the USA, while IELTS is more popular in the UK, Australia and Canada. TOEFL uses academic English exclusively, while IELTS uses a mix of academic and everyday English. TOEFL Speaking is recorded on computer; IELTS Speaking is face-to-face. See our detailed TOEFL vs IELTS comparison.

TOEFL vs Duolingo English Test: Duolingo is shorter (about 1 hour), cheaper (approximately USD 59), and can be taken entirely from home. However, it is accepted by fewer universities than TOEFL. Some universities accept Duolingo as an alternative, but TOEFL remains the gold standard for academic admissions.

TOEFL vs PTE Academic: PTE is fully computer-scored (no human raters) and provides results within 2 days. It is popular in Australia and the UK. TOEFL is more widely accepted globally, especially in the USA.

Test Day Tips and What to Expect

On test day, whether at a centre or at home, there are several things you should know to perform at your best.

At the test centre: Arrive at least 30 minutes early. You will need to present a valid passport or government-issued photo ID. Personal items like phones, bags, watches and notes are not allowed in the testing room. You will be given a locker or storage area for your belongings. The test centre provides scratch paper and pencils for note-taking (you cannot bring your own).

At home (Home Edition): Set up your testing area at least 30 minutes before your scheduled start time. Download and install the ETS Secure Test Browser. Ensure your room is clean, well-lit and free of distractions. The proctor will ask you to show your ID and scan your room with the camera. Keep your face visible on camera at all times during the test.

During the test, you will wear headphones (at test centres) or use speakers (at home) for the Listening section. The Speaking section uses your computer's microphone. Speak clearly and at a normal volume — do not whisper or shout.

You cannot pause or take breaks during the test. The entire 2-hour test is completed in one sitting. Use the bathroom before the test begins. If you need to use the restroom during the test, the timer does not stop, so you will lose valuable time.

After completing all four sections, your Reading and Listening scores appear on screen immediately (unofficial scores). Your Speaking and Writing scores are available within 6 to 10 days, as they require human and AI scoring.

TOEFL Test Dates and Registration

The TOEFL iBT is offered more than 60 times per year at test centres worldwide. Test dates are available throughout the year, with multiple sessions per week in most major cities. This frequency means you can almost always find a test date that fits your schedule and application deadlines.

To register, visit the ETS website (ets.org/toefl) and create an account. You will need a valid passport or government-issued ID, a credit or debit card for payment, and an email address. The registration process takes about 15 minutes. You can search for available test dates and centres by entering your city or postal code.

The regular registration deadline is typically 7 days before the test date. If you miss this deadline, you can register late (up to 3 days before the test) for an additional USD 40 late fee. However, late registration availability depends on remaining seats at your chosen test centre, so it is always better to register early.

For the Home Edition, test dates and times are available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. You can book a test slot with as little as 24 hours' notice, making it ideal for students who need flexibility or live far from a test centre. The Home Edition is particularly popular in regions where test centres are limited or fully booked.

If you need to change your test date, you can reschedule through your ETS account. A rescheduling fee of USD 60 applies. You must reschedule at least 4 full days before your original test date. If you miss your test without rescheduling, you will forfeit the full test fee and need to register again.

It is advisable to register 2 to 3 months before your university application deadline. This gives you time to receive your scores (6-10 days), retake the test if needed, and send scores to universities before the deadline. Many universities have strict application deadlines, and late score submissions are rarely accepted.

Keep in mind that test centres in popular cities can fill up quickly during peak application seasons (September to January for fall admissions). Register as early as possible to secure your preferred date and location.

Final Thoughts

The TOEFL iBT is a rigorous but fair test of academic English proficiency. With the right preparation, it is entirely possible to achieve a high score. The key is to start early, use official materials, practise under timed conditions, and focus on your weakest areas.

Remember that the TOEFL is not just a test — it is a preparation tool for studying abroad. The skills you build while preparing — reading academic texts, taking notes from lectures, speaking clearly about complex topics, and writing under time pressure — are the exact skills you will use in your university courses.

Take advantage of the MyBest Scores feature. If your first attempt does not give you the score you need, retake the test. There is no limit on how many times you can take the TOEFL, and your best section scores from each attempt are combined into your MyBest composite.

Whether you choose the test centre or the Home Edition, whether you are applying to universities in the USA, UK, Canada, Australia or Germany, the TOEFL iBT gives you a globally recognised credential that demonstrates your English proficiency to universities, employers and immigration authorities around the world.

Start your preparation today. Your future self will thank you.

Frequently asked questions

What is TOEFL iBT?+

TOEFL iBT (Test of English as a Foreign Language, Internet-Based Test) is a standardised English proficiency test by ETS. It tests Reading, Listening, Speaking and Writing skills in an academic context. It is accepted by over 12,000 institutions in 160+ countries and is the most widely accepted English test for US universities.

How is TOEFL scored?+

TOEFL iBT scores range from 0 to 120. Each section (Reading, Listening, Speaking, Writing) is scored 0-30. Your total score is the sum of all four section scores. How long is the TOEFL test?+

TOEFL iBT is approximately 2 hours long (reduced from 3+ hours in 2023). Reading: 20 minutes, Listening: 28 minutes, Speaking: 16 minutes, Writing: 29 minutes. There is no break between sections. The entire test is completed in one sitting.

How much does TOEFL cost in 2026?+

TOEFL fees vary by country: India INR 16,000-17,500, Pakistan PKR 42,000-50,000, USA USD 200-250, UK GBP 190-220, Canada CAD 250-300, Australia AUD 350-400, Germany EUR 240. Additional fees: late registration USD 40, rescheduling USD 60, extra score reports USD 20 each.

What is TOEFL MyBest Scores?+

MyBest Scores (superscores) combines your highest section scores from all TOEFL attempts within the last 2 years. For example, if you scored 25 in Reading on one test and 28 in Reading on another, your MyBest Reading score is 28. Many universities accept MyBest Scores, so retaking the test can improve your total.

What is TOEFL Home Edition?+

TOEFL iBT Home Edition is the same test as the centre-based TOEFL, but taken from home on your own computer. It uses AI proctoring and human review. You need a computer with camera, microphone, speakers, and stable internet. It is accepted by the same institutions as the centre-based test. Available in most countries.

What is a good TOEFL score?+

Most universities require TOEFL 80-100 overall. Competitive programmes require 100+. Top universities (MIT, Stanford, Oxford) often require 100-110. For comparison: TOEFL 80 is approximately equal to IELTS 6.0, TOEFL 90 to IELTS 6.5, TOEFL 100 to IELTS 7.0, TOEFL 110 to IELTS 8.0.

Which is better, TOEFL or IELTS?+

TOEFL is more widely accepted in the USA. IELTS is more widely accepted in the UK, Australia and Canada. TOEFL uses academic English exclusively. IELTS uses a mix of academic and everyday English. TOEFL Speaking is recorded on computer; IELTS Speaking is face-to-face. Both are valid for 2 years. Choose based on your target country and personal preference.

How do I prepare for TOEFL?+

Use official ETS preparation materials, take practice tests on the ETS website, focus on note-taking for Listening and Speaking, practise independent and integrated writing tasks, build academic vocabulary, and take timed practice tests. A 6-8 week structured study plan is recommended.

How long are TOEFL scores valid?+

TOEFL scores are valid for 2 years from the test date. You can send score reports to 4 universities for free at the time of testing. Additional reports cost USD 20 each. Many universities accept scores within 2 years of the test date.
