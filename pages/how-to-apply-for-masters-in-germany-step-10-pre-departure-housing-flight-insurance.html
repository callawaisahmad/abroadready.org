What apply for masters in germany step 10 departure actually covers

Apply for masters in germany step 10 departure is the fortnight of admin between an approved visa and a life that actually works. The admission letter is in your inbox, the APS certificate is done, the blocked account is funded, the consular interview is a formality you will pass. What remains is the part that quietly strands a large share of students: finding a bed, insuring yourself, booking a flight, and arriving with the right paperwork in your hand. Done properly, you are functional within ten days of landing. Done badly, you pay hotels, lose two weeks to registration problems, and miss enrolment while you fix them.

Searchers sometimes treat this as apply for masters in germany step 10 departure, as though it were a single event, and that framing is why people underestimate it. It is a chain of dependencies, each one gated by the last. No visa without an insurance certificate. No Anmeldung without a signed lease and a landlord's written confirmation. No Immatrikulation without the registration certificate. No residence permit without the blocked account and the insurance certificate, again. Any missing link delays all the others.

Almost everything here is knowable from Pakistan weeks before you fly, and most of it is purchasable online. The risk is that it competes with goodbyes, wedding season, and the last-minute panic that makes people book a flight to Munich without checking whether their university is in Cologne. Read this with a notebook rather than a scroll, and treat every figure as a starting point to confirm, not a fact to memorise.

When should I start on this, and when should I land?

Start the day your visa is approved, and start the housing search before that. The realistic sequence: apply to the relevant Studentenwerk the moment you accept your offer, because dorm allocation is a lottery weighted by need, not a first-come queue. Run a WG search in parallel. Take out health insurance as soon as you are eligible, since the visa file and the residence permit both require the certificate. Book the flight only after the visa sticker is in your passport, which is the point at which non-refundable tickets make sense.

How far ahead of the semester should I be on the ground?

Two to four weeks before lectures begin, and the upper end is better. Winter lectures typically start in mid-October, with orientation and registration events in the late-September to early-October window. Landing in early September gives you buffer for a flat falling through, a missed connection, an Anmeldung appointment that needs three documents you forgot, and the two or three weeks a first-term residence permit can take. Landing the week lectures start means any of those problems costs you attendance.

What is the order of the first three weeks in Germany?

Land, get a SIM or activate an eSIM, then go straight to the city registration office (Bürgeramt or Bürgerbüro) for the Anmeldung, because that is the item with a legal deadline and the longest queues. In parallel, register at the university, which needs the registration certificate plus the blocked account confirmation. Then apply at the Ausländerbehörde for your residence permit, which needs the registration certificate, the insurance certificate, the admission letter and proof of funding. Then the bank account, the semester ticket, and settling into the room. If you enter on a national visa, that visa expires 90 days after entry, so the residence permit application is not optional housekeeping.

Can I miss the enrolment deadline by arriving late?

You can, and the penalties are softer than students fear but real: a late-registration fee, or in the worst cases a place lost. Most international offices will accept a student who arrives with a housing crisis or a cancelled flight if you email before the deadline, explain plainly, and attach the flight booking. What they will not accept is silence, or a no-show discovered at the end of the month. Email from a working address, keep the sent mail.

apply for masters in germany step 10 departure: student housing options in Germany

Germany has four housing routes for a master's student, differing on price, waiting time, paperwork and social life. Most people use two in sequence: a Studentenwerk dormitory if you get one, or a WG room for the whole stay, with temporary housing as a bridge either side.

What are the four options?

Studentenwerk dormitories are university-affiliated residences run by the local student services body. WG rooms (Wohngemeinschaft, shared flat) are the dominant market option: a rented flat where each tenant has a private bedroom and the kitchen, bathroom and living room are shared. Private studios and one-room flats are the solo option, simplest and most expensive. Temporary housing means a hostel bed, a budget hotel, a night-train berth or a short let booked online to bridge the gap while a longer contract is arranged. The trade-off is arithmetic: dorms are cheapest and hardest to get, WGs are the value choice, studios cost the most and simplify your life, temporary housing is the most expensive per night and the lowest-risk way to start.

How do I get into a Studentenwerk dormitory?

Apply. There is no trick, only a queue and an allocation policy. The national network is at https://www.studentenwerke.de, which routes you to the body serving your university. Applications usually open several months before term, commonly in spring for a winter intake, and each body runs its own round. Typical ranking criteria are distance of the room to campus, financial need, waiting time, international status in some cases, disability, and family circumstances. Dorms are usually single rooms with a shared kitchen and bathroom, occasionally a shared bedroom, and rent normally includes utilities, internet and often a local transport pass, which is why dorms are frequently better value than the headline number suggests.

Should I expect a dorm rejection?

In high-demand cities, yes, and it is the most common housing failure point for international arrivals. Munich, Berlin, Frankfurt and Cologne fill long before term, and non-local applicants are often weaker on distance-based criteria. Expect a rejection rather than a queue position, and never build a plan that assumes a room. Apply anyway: it costs minutes, and the payoff is rent roughly 30 to 50 percent below market. Keep the WG search running throughout.

What is a WG room and how does a shared flat work?

Two to five people rent the whole apartment between them, each with a locked bedroom door. You normally sign one main lease (Hauptmietvertrag) with the landlord and either sublease your room under an additional contract, or the existing tenants are the main tenants and add you. Both structures exist. Rent is agreed per person, a deposit is taken, and utilities are usually billed separately on top of the advertised figure. The advantages are real: a shared kitchen cuts food costs, a flat full of people is a built-in social network in your first month in a new country, and the price-per-square-metre beats a studio in every German city.

What should I check before signing a WG contract?

The risk is in the process, not the format. Two hard rules: never transfer a deposit or rent to a landlord you have not seen in person, and never sign terms you have not read or had explained. Ask for a Mieterschutz (tenant protection certificate), ask whether the landlord uses the Kautionsregelung that spreads the deposit over equal monthly instalments instead of blocking it all on day one, and ask whether utilities are billed by the landlord or opened in your name. Check the platform's safety guidance, and be suspicious of listings priced far below the going rate, which usually means an illegal sublet, a bait advertisement, or a surprise at the viewing.

When is a private studio the right call?

Take a studio when your budget absorbs it, when your work needs quiet, when you are older and used to living alone, or when the studio-to-WG gap in your city is narrow. A studio removes the search for housemates, which is the most time-consuming part of arriving alone somewhere new. It also removes the built-in social network, which matters if you have no friends in the city. Studios are consistently the most expensive option, and in Munich the gap is wide enough to make this a budget decision rather than a preference. A common compromise: a WG room for the first semester, then move once you know the neighbourhoods and the people.

What does temporary housing cost, and how long should I book it?

Hostel beds in German student cities commonly run EUR 25 to 45 a night, budget hotels with shared bathrooms EUR 60 to 120, and city-centre hotels considerably more. Book four to seven nights, not weeks, take the first available viewing appointment you get, and use the days to view three to five flats. The failure mode is booking a fortnight as a fallback and then finding nothing, which becomes two expensive weeks. If your German is weak, do video tours of the long list and reserve your in-person days for the three flats you would actually take.

How much will rent and utilities cost me, and by city?

German rent is quoted cold (kalt), the base rent only, or warm (warm), base rent plus a service charge and usually heating. Advertisements almost always show the Kaltmihte, so the affordable-looking number is a floor, not a total. In a WG the headline is your share of the cold rent and the Nebenkosten are split with the others. Budget the running total, and assume your true monthly housing cost runs 20 to 35 percent above the cold-rent headline, more in older buildings with heating.

Which cities are genuinely cheapest, and should that decide where I study?

If cost is the binding constraint, the smaller cities are the honest answer, and the gap is dramatic rather than marginal. A WG room in Leipzig or a smaller Bavarian or Rhineland-Palatinate town costs roughly EUR 200 to 350 a month less than the same setup in Munich, which over 24 months is several thousand euros. The trade-offs are a thinner English-language job market, fewer international students, and sometimes limited housing near the campus. The calculation is straightforward: if the subject makes the target city a requirement for internships and jobs, do not move for rent. If the discipline is location-flexible, the same degree costs very different money in two cities.

Are utilities included in the advertised rent?

Often not, and this is the first question to ask. In a dorm, utilities and internet are usually bundled and a local transport pass may be included, which makes dorm rent look poor on comparison and excellent on value. In a private flat or WG, a warm rent includes a service charge covering maintenance, rubbish and sometimes heating, while electricity, gas, water and internet are frequently separate contracts you open yourself. Heating can run EUR 60 to 180 for a winter in a cold city, billed monthly or annually with a retrospective true-up. Assume 20 to 35 percent above the cold-rent figure and you will not be surprised.

What is the Kaution, and how do I get it back?

The Kaution is the security deposit, legally capped at two or three months of cold rent depending on the state. More landlords now use the Kautionsregelung: three months up front, then one further monthly instalment, which matters enormously to a student living on a blocked account. To get it back, agree at the start whether you want an individual or joint deposit, keep every receipt and the signed contract, take dated photos of the room's condition at handover, and submit a written request after moving out. Joint deposits must be reclaimed jointly, so individual deposits are safer. Unreturned deposits are a genuine and common dispute, and documentation is what resolves them.

How much cash should I hold for the first month in total?

Build a first-month figure, not a rent figure: accommodation first month plus deposit, utilities, insurance, food, local transport, a phone plan, university fees and the semester ticket. In an expensive city a realistic first-month outflow is EUR 1,200 to 2,000 including deposit; in a cheaper city EUR 700 to 1,200. If the blocked account does not cover that, add savings before you fly, because the first ten days generate more small unexpected costs than any settled week you will live. Our study abroad cost and budget guide builds the full monthly model, and the figure you proved to the consulate has to match the city you actually land in.

How do I book accommodation from Pakistan, and how does the Anmeldung work?

This is the part of apply for masters in germany step 10 departure with the hardest deadline attached, and it does not negotiate. German law expects you to register your address within 14 days of moving into a dwelling, and that registration is what unlocks your bank account, tax number, residence permit paperwork, enrolment certificate and in several cities your local services.

What does the Anmeldung actually require?

A Wohnungsgeberbestätigung, your landlord's written confirmation that you live there, plus your passport or national ID card, plus the registration form. In most states the landlord is also legally required to send this confirmation to the registration office electronically, so even if they tell you not to worry you still need a signed copy in your hand. The office issues a stamped Anmeldebestätigung listing your address and date, and this single sheet is the most-requested document in all of German student administration. Budget two to four weeks in a big city, longer at the start of term, because appointments are booked weeks out and offices are slow.

Can I book a room remotely from Pakistan?

Sometimes, and it is legal, but it carries real risk. Three approaches work. The safest: a live video tour of the room, street and kitchen, a signed contract, and a trusted colleague physically checking condition at handover. The middle path, which most students should take: arrive with five to seven nights of temporary housing booked and view in person. The third: a one-to-four-week short let on a major booking platform, a legal tourist stay that gives you an address for the Anmeldung and a normal if expensive way to start. The dorm route is the exception, because a Studentenwerk room is allocated by an institution rather than sold to the highest bidder, so booking one needs no trust at all.

Can I register an address before I physically live there?

No. The Anmeldung requires that you have moved in, and the Wohnungsgeberbestätigung is the landlord's statement that you have. What you can do from abroad is prepare everything: the landlord confirmation, the registration form, the appointment booking, and the simplified digital registration many Länder now offer. That last option solves paperwork delay, not the physical move, because it still needs the landlord's electronic confirmation.

What happens if I miss the 14-day window?

You get a reminder, then a fee, and in a serious case a legal notice. The bigger damage is indirect: without registration you cannot enrol, the university flags your file, and the residence permit stalls. If you have already missed it, do not wait to be noticed. Go to the Bürgeramt, say you are a newly arrived international student, bring passport, admission letter, landlord confirmation and visa, and ask for late registration. Staff handle this constantly, especially at the start of a semester. Use the same appointment to ask whether you can register the permanent address immediately once you move from temporary to a long-term contract, because that sequence decides whether you register once or twice.

Which platforms should I use?

Start with the free ones. WG-Gesucht is the dominant student-specific platform, with a large inventory and a built-in scam-awareness culture, and most university-owned listings appear there or on campus housing pages. Kleinanzeigen carries private listings, including direct-from-owner rooms. General portals such as https://www.immobilienscout24.de cover long-term rentals and are useful when you want a studio rather than a WG room. Paid student-housing agents and union flat-finding services can be worth it in Munich or Berlin when your schedule is impossible, but the fee is real and the inventory is not exclusive. Run at least two channels, and treat a paid service as a supplement, never a replacement for your own checks.

Flights: when to book, and when to land

When exactly should I book?

After the visa is approved, never before. A non-refundable ticket bought ahead of approval is a bet on a consulate, and the odds are not good enough. Once the sticker is in your passport, book a return or open ticket and keep the confirmation, because that document is itself a travel-evidence item. The window between approval and departure is usually six to twelve weeks: long enough for a sensible fare, short enough that prices have not already climbed through peak season.

One-way or return?

Check your own mission's list, because the requirement is stated by the mission and it varies. German missions generally ask for evidence of intended onward travel, which a confirmed return ticket satisfies, and which a one-way booking plus a declared onward plan may also satisfy depending on the case. A dummy reservation you do not intend to use is not acceptable. If you are uncertain, ask the visa officer at the interview rather than guessing, and be consistent: the date on your reservation, your insurance and your blocked account calculation should agree with each other.

Which airports and routes work from Pakistan?

There is no significant direct commercial service from Pakistan to Germany, so itineraries connect through Istanbul, Doha, Dubai, Abu Dhabi and sometimes Amsterdam or a UK hub. Frankfurt is the main hub, easiest to reach from a Gulf or Turkish connection, with onward ICE and regional trains covering most cities. Munich, Düsseldorf, Hamburg, Berlin Brandenburg and Stuttgart all have long-distance rail links from major hubs, and a night train from Hamburg or Berlin to a smaller destination saves a hotel night. For Berlin, Berlin Brandenburg is small and slow to the city, so an arrival at Frankfurt or Hamburg followed by a train can be faster door to door. For Munich, take the airport train rather than the bus and build in extra time.

What is a reasonable fare, and what about baggage and transit?

A few hundred euros is realistic for a typical round trip in shoulder season; peak-season departures at the start of a winter semester can multiply that. Track fares rather than guessing: set an alert on a metasearch engine, compare the flexible-date view, and price a connection through a hub hub against the marketed direct. Take a fare that allows changes over the cheapest that does not, and book with an airline or reputable agent rather than an anonymous broker. On mixed itineraries, a low-cost second leg often means cabin-bag-only with a hard weight limit, so check before packing. Prefer one through-ticket, because a self-transfer without one means collecting and rechecking bags, and a miss becomes a new problem with a second visa-relevant document. Finally, price the alternative honestly: a later arrival into Berlin is cheaper overall if the onward train costs less than a missed night's hotel.

Health insurance: the certificate the whole step depends on

Which health insurance do I need before arriving?

Any policy that is valid, accepted for the visa, and effective in Germany. The visa file requires a health insurance certificate (Krankenversicherungsbescheinigung) proving cover in Germany from your intended entry date, and the residence permit application requires a certificate of enrolment in a valid scheme. This is the item students most underestimate, because they treat it as a formality to produce on the morning of the consulate appointment. It is a real purchase, and in the public system you must be eligible before you can enrol.

How much is public insurance for students under 30?

Statutory health insurance for students under 30 costs roughly EUR 128 to 140 a month in 2026, with most people landing close to EUR 130. Students under 30 are not given a special reduced student rate. Instead, the contribution is calculated as a percentage of income against a minimum monthly base, and the university is deemed to pay the other half while you are enrolled. The figure you pay therefore depends on the base the insurer uses and its supplementary contribution, which differs between the roughly 90 competing Krankenkassen, but the result sits in a narrow band. That is why nearly every major provider, including https://www.tk.de, BARMER and AOK, will quote you something in the same range for a student with no income.

Why do some students choose private cover instead?

Public is the default and well regarded: accepted everywhere, no excess for most student care, psychiatric and dental treatment included without separate pre-approval, and the same price whether you see a consultant or a general practitioner. Some students look past it because of monthly payments and paperwork: you enrol online or in person, you pay monthly, you file a claim form for most treatment, and you wait for a card. If you are over 30 the university no longer pays half, so the full cost falls on you and the figure sits meaningfully above the student band. Private student tariff plans are priced individually rather than by statutory formula and can be cheaper for someone over 30, sometimes for a young student with a deductible. Check the deductible, the reimbursement ceiling, whether psychiatric treatment needs prior approval, whether dental and hospital admission are included, and whether the university and foreigners' authority accept the insurer.

Do I need travel insurance for the first weeks?

A Schengen short-stay visa issued outside your country of residence normally requires travel medical insurance with minimum cover of EUR 30,000, valid across the whole Schengen area, with a zero or low deductible, for the entire stay. Some applicants also buy cover as an extra layer for the first weeks in Germany, particularly if their public enrolment has not activated yet. Understand the limit: a travel policy is not a substitute for statutory health insurance, and where statutory cover is required, substituting one causes problems at the residence permit stage. A Studentenwerk travel insurance scheme is a good budget option because it is designed for student gap months. Buy it for the whole travel period plus the first weeks, verify Germany is named explicitly, and cancel it once your German policy is active.

What is the Electronic Health Card, and what if I arrive uninsured?

The eGK is the card you present in Germany, and it is functionally ordinary: treatment is generally not contingent on producing it, but receptionists ask for it and pharmacies scan it, so having it saves a conversation. New entrants receive it within a few weeks of enrolment, and some insurers now issue a usable digital version immediately. If you misjudge the timing, fix it within days rather than weeks. Enrol with a statutory insurer online, which takes minutes once you have your admission letter and address, and ask for the policy to be backdated to your entry date; the certificate is then issued immediately as an electronic document, and that certificate is what the residence permit office wants. The genuine risk is having no certificate at all at the residence permit appointment, so enrol online before you land if you can, and the morning after you land if you cannot.

Money, transport and daily life logistics

How should I bring money, and what do I need in cash?

Bring euros, not rupees, and do not arrive with your whole budget in cash. A reasonable opening position is EUR 300 to 500 in cash, mostly small denominations, enough for the first week plus a hotel night if something goes wrong, plus an international card with a sensible limit and a second card on a different network as backup. Notify your Pakistani bank of your travel dates so the card is not silently declined, and test it at a German terminal before you need it in a hurry. German ATMs inside bank branches do not run out of cash the way airport machines sometimes do, and EUR 20 and EUR 50 notes disappear fastest, so bring those.

Should I open a German bank account before I fly?

You can, and you should, but the workable order runs the other way: take the Anmeldung registration, then open a current account, usually free for students, at a branch offering a student plan, then receive a free or low-cost card. A blocked account is not a payment account; it is a held deposit, so plan to open a second ordinary account after registration and run daily life from that. For money arriving from Pakistan, transfer through your own bank rather than an exchange house, and keep receipts, because they are useful for the currency authorities and for any later visa renewal or scholarship audit.

How do I pay rent, and what is the semester ticket?

SEPA direct debit is the German default, and nearly every landlord will ask for a mandate authorising them to pull rent on a set date each month. A German current account is effectively required for a smooth rental relationship, and it is what the tax office and university expect. Before signing, ask for the exact monthly amount, the due date, the account details, and whether they want a mandate or a standing transfer, then get the annual Nebenkosten settlement in writing, because that is where overcharging surfaces. On transport, the semester ticket is a term-long pass sold by universities, usually covering all local and regional buses, trams, U-Bahn and S-Bahn for a fraction of the daily single fare. Costs range from roughly EUR 30 in some cities to above EUR 100 in others, and it is almost always worth buying because it removes one thing to think about in week one. Note what it excludes: long-distance Intercity and ICE, and often the Airport Express.

What about phones and the internet in the flat?

Three routes, all workable. Buy a prepaid or contract SIM in a shop at the station or in the city centre on day one; the big networks have storefronts in most major cities and student plans exist. Activate a German eSIM from your phone before landing, which works the moment you have data. Or keep roaming briefly while you shop for a local plan. For the flat itself, ask during the viewing whether internet is included, whether it is a shared line or a dedicated connection in your room, and how the bill is split. In a WG it is nearly always shared, and asking tells you a lot about how the flat is run.

What do I pack, and what must be physically with me?

Which documents must I carry rather than scan?

Carry the originals in your hand luggage, in one folder, in a waterproof sleeve, with a digital backup on two devices. The working set: passport with the visa sticker, university admission letter, blocked account confirmation, health insurance certificate, APS certificate, birth certificate, and the certified and translated degree and transcript documents you were asked to bring. Add the landlord's written confirmation for the flat you are moving into, and the university enrolment paperwork once you have it. The reason for hand luggage is not only that checked bags get lost, but that they arrive when you need identification and a visa, and the consequences are worse.

What clothes and equipment do I actually need?

German student cities are cool and wet in autumn and winter, which surprises people arriving from Lahore or Karachi in a light shirt. Landing in September or October, bring a genuinely warm waterproof coat, shoes that survive rain, layers and an umbrella. A week of clothes plus a coat plus the ability to wash is enough, because German second-hand shops and discount chains make filling out a wardrobe cheap. Bring your laptop, chargers, phone, headphones, glasses, and prescription medicines in original packaging with a doctor's letter. Do check that your devices handle 230 volts, and bring a Type F (Schuko) plug adapter, which is the standard here.

What should I buy after arrival instead of carrying?

Everything bulky: bedding, kitchen equipment, cleaning supplies, furniture, a desk, a bike and a helmet, textbooks. All cheaper or easier to source locally, and most student cities have cheap second-hand channels as well as nationwide delivery. Do not carry a full kitchen or any furniture; the volume will wreck your baggage allowance and the money is better spent once you know the flat. The exception is anything you cannot get in Germany: your preferred food, spices, tea, or a specific religious or medical item, packed carefully and declared honestly.

Are there customs and import rules that will surprise me?

Yes, and the surprise lands at the airport rather than on arrival. Germany and the EU apply strict rules to animal products, so meat, raw milk and some dairy from outside the EU are prohibited or restricted, and items are confiscated on the spot. Packaged vegetarian and plant-based goods are usually straightforward, and German cities have good, often cheaper, vegan, vegetarian and halal grocery supply, so test before you import. Also declare anything above the duty-free allowance in cash and keep receipts for large purchases, because customs do ask and the same documents help in a later visa renewal or scholarship audit.

What goes in the final two weeks, and the last 72 hours?

Week one: insurance sorted, any required travel medical cover bought, blocked account opened and funded. Week two: flight booked, document folder collected, and the shopping you cannot do in Germany done. Last 72 hours: re-read the folder, confirm the passport is valid well beyond the enrolment date, check that the visa sticker matches the passport number on the ticket, download every confirmation for offline access, tell your bank, and set the phone up for arrival.

What are the classic mistakes here?

Five, in order of frequency. Booking a flight before the visa is issued. Buying a non-refundable ticket on a self-transfer with no through-ticket. Arriving with no health insurance certificate. Assuming the dorm application guarantees a room and therefore arriving with no fallback. And leaving address registration until last, so the 14-day window is half gone before you discover you also needed the landlord's electronic confirmation. None of these need a different talent to avoid. They need this page eight weeks before departure rather than four days before.

Step 10 is the least academic part of the process and the one most likely to produce a bad month. It has no committee, no grade and no appeal. What are the six things that matter most?

Health insurance in place with the certificate in hand, because the visa and the residence permit both depend on it. A signed lease and landlord confirmation, because the Anmeldung and therefore enrolment depend on it. A dorm application filed in the first week after you accept the offer, because the market does not wait. A flight booked only after the visa, arriving two to four weeks before lectures. A document folder in hand luggage holding passport, admission letter, blocked account confirmation, insurance certificate, APS certificate and certified degree documents. Frequently asked questions

How do I find accommodation for my masters in Germany?+

Start 3 to 4 months early: apply to student dormitories (Studentenwerk), search WG-Gesucht for shared flats and check university housing boards. Rents run about EUR 350 to 600 monthly depending on the city.

Which health insurance do I need before arriving?+

You need a valid policy for the visa and arrival. Students under 30 typically take statutory (public) insurance such as TK or BARMER at roughly EUR 128 to 140 monthly; language or over-30 students often use private plans from about EUR 35 monthly.

What should I pack and bring for Germany?+

Bring certified copies of every academic document, your admission letter, the blocked account certificate, insurance confirmation, passport photos, and enough cash or cards for weeks one and two. Keep files backed up digitally too.

When should I book my flight to Germany?+

Book only after the visa is approved, and arrive 2 to 4 weeks before your semester starts so you have time for Anmeldung, enrollment and settling into your room.
