How to Immigrate to Italy (2026): EU Blue Card & Work Permits

🗓️ Aug 12, 2026⏱️ 12 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

Italy's immigration system has two very different doors. For qualified professionals, the EU Blue Card is fast, quota-free and available year-round, requiring a salary of around €35,500 and a valid job offer. For everyone else, the Decreto Flussi is an annual quota lottery that opens on a "click day" and typically fills within minutes. This guide explains both routes, plus the digital-nomad visa and the study-first path, so you know which door actually fits your profile before you start.

If you are a university-educated professional, or have five years of relevant experience, the Blue Card is almost always the smarter play than waiting for a quota window.

Key takeaways

  • The EU Blue Card is Italy's skilled route: quota-free, year-round, for a highly qualified job paying at least €35,000–35,500 gross a year (reduced to ~€28,200 in shortage sectors like ICT and healthcare).
  • The Decreto Flussi is the annual capped quota (around 150,000 slots in 2025) for seasonal, subordinate and trade work, applied for by employers on the click day.
  • Your employer secures a Nulla Osta (work authorisation) first; you then apply for a Type D work visa and convert it to a residence permit within eight working days of arrival.
  • The study-first route is often the most reliable: convert your student permit to a work permit any time, no quota window needed.
  • Permanent residence comes after 5 years of legal residence; citizenship after 10 (4 for EU citizens).

The EU Blue Card: Italy's skilled route

The Blue Card is for university-educated professionals or those with five years' professional experience equivalent to a higher-education qualification. You need a job offer for a highly qualified position with a contract of at least 12 months and a salary meeting the threshold: €35,000–35,500 gross per year (1.5x the national average), or around €28,200 in shortage sectors such as ICT and healthcare. The salary must be base salary, not bonuses, and must also meet the minimum set by the relevant collective agreement. Processing runs 60–120 days total (Nulla Osta 30–90 days), the permit lasts one to two years and renews up to four years and beyond, and spouses and children join with immediate work rights. After 18 months, Blue Card holders gain EU-wide mobility, and time on the card counts toward long-term residence.

The Decreto Flussi quota route

Each year a decree sets how many non-EU workers may enter Italy for employment, around 150,000 slots in 2025, split between seasonal, non-seasonal subordinate, self-employment and conversions. Employers submit on the "click day", first come, first served, and the window is often exhausted within minutes, which is why most successful applications are filed by an experienced Italian lawyer the moment it opens. Duration is usually two years for subordinate work, and it builds the residence history you need for PR after five years, though seasonal work does not lead to residence directly.

Digital nomad and other routes

The digital-nomad visa, introduced in 2024, suits highly skilled remote workers earning from abroad: roughly €28,000 a year in income, health insurance and accommodation proof, valid one year and renewable. The Italia Startup Visa is quota-free for founders of innovative startups, with a decision in about 90 days. Intra-company transfers and family reunification (spouses, minor children and dependent parents, with work rights) round out the picture. Retirees and people of independent means can use the elective residence visa, which requires passive income of roughly €31,000+ a year and allows no work.

The study-first route

International graduates of Italian universities can convert their student permit to a work permit at any time, with no Flussi window since the 2023 reform, and can take a 12-month job-search permit. Tuition at Italian public universities is low (often €1,000–3,000, and sometimes less), so studying first is both cheaper and strategically smarter than competing for quota places from abroad. Costs, PR and citizenship

Key 2026 fees: Type D work visa about €116, residence permit (permesso di soggiorno) around €70.50, and Schengen tourist visa €90. Permanent residence (Permesso di Soggiorno UE) comes after 5 years of continuous legal residence, and citizenship after 10 years (4 for EU citizens) with B1 Italian and a clean record. Frequently asked questions

Can I get an Italian work visa without a degree?+

Yes. The EU Blue Card accepts five years of professional experience as equivalent to a higher-education qualification, especially for IT, engineering and senior management. The Decreto Flussi route also does not require a degree but is quota-capped.

What is the difference between the EU Blue Card and Decreto Flussi?+

The Blue Card is quota-free, year-round and for qualified professionals above a salary threshold (about €35,500, or €28,200 in shortage sectors), with family work rights and faster processing. Decreto Flussi is the annual capped quota system for seasonal, trade and subordinate work.

How long does the Italian Blue Card process take?+

About 2–4 months total: the Nulla Osta takes 30–90 days, then the Type D visa at the consulate, then conversion to the residence permit within 8 working days of arrival.

Can my family live and work in Italy?+

Yes. EU Blue Card families get residence permits with immediate work rights, spouses can work without extra procedures, and children attend Italian public schools free. Family reunification also applies for spouses, minor children and dependent parents of residents.

Can I switch from a tourist visa to a work visa in Italy?+

No. A Schengen C visa cannot be converted into a work or residence permit. You need a Nulla Osta obtained by your employer and a Type D work visa from an Italian consulate before you arrive. The main exception is converting a student permit to a work permit.
