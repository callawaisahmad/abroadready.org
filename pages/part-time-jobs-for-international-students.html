Part-Time Jobs for International Students: Work Rules by Country (2026)

📅 Aug 18, 2026 ⏱ 29 min read ✏️ AbroadReady Team

Studying abroad is expensive, and most international students need to earn while they learn. But can international students work part-time? The answer depends entirely on which country you study in and what visa you hold. This guide covers the exact student work hours by country for 2026, including minimum wages, tax obligations, and the jobs that are easiest to get.

Key takeaways

  • Most countries allow part-time work for international students, but with strict hourly limits tied to your visa.
  • The UK and US allow 20 hours/week during term. Canada allows 24 hours/week. Australia allows 48 hours per fortnight. Germany has no weekly limit but caps at 140 days/year.
  • Working above your visa limit is a serious violation — it can lead to deportation and a multi-year ban.
  • On-campus jobs are the easiest to get in the first semester because employers understand visa rules.
  • You will need a tax number in every country (NINo in the UK, SIN in Canada, TFN in Australia).

Should you work part-time at all? The honest pros and cons

Before we dive into countries and rules, let me pause and give you an honest take that a lot of websites skip. Working part-time while studying abroad is not automatically a good idea. For some students it's the difference between affording the experience and not, and for others it quietly derails their studies. So let's weigh it properly.

The real benefits of working while you study

  • It covers day-to-day living costs. In expensive cities, a part-time job can genuinely pay your rent or your grocery bill, which means less financial stress and less dependence on family money.
  • It builds local experience and references. This is huuuge for your post-study job search. Employers in the destination country value local work experience, even part-time. A reference from a local restaurant manager can be more valuable to a future employer than you'd think.
  • It improves your language skills. There's no faster way to get comfortable with a new language than having to use it with customers and colleagues every day.
  • It builds a local network. You'll meet people outside the university bubble, which helps you feel less isolated and opens doors later.
  • It keeps you in touch with reality. For students who have never managed money, earning your own income is a crash course in budgeting that will serve you for life.

The honest downsides

  • Your grades can suffer. Whether they admit it or not, a lot of students who work maximum hours end up with lower marks. Your visa, and your future career, depend on passing your courses. Never let work undermine that.
  • It eats your study time. You only have so many hours in a day. Every hour at work is an hour not spent studying, resting, or building connections that actually matter for your career.
  • It's exhausting. Juggling a job, full-time study, and daily life in a new country is genuinely tiring. Burnout is common.
  • Your visa is on the line. Going even a little over your hour limit can have serious consequences.
  • It can trap you. Some students get so caught up in earning money they lose sight of why they came — to study. Try to keep your work in proportion.

The honest bottom line: if you need to work to make ends meet, do it, but keep your hours well within the legal limit and prioritize your studies. If you don't need the money, consider working a few hours anyway for the experience and network — it genuinely helps later. The key is that part-time work for international students should serve your goals, not rule your life.

How student work rules actually work: the fundamentals

Before the country-by-country detail, it really helps to understand the principles that all these rules share. Once you grasp the logic, the specifics stop feeling like a random list of numbers.

Your hours are tied to your term, not the calendar year

Almost every country distinguishes between "term time" (when classes are in session) and "scheduled breaks" (holidays, summer break, etc.). The strict hour limits almost always apply only during term time. During scheduled breaks, most countries let you work full-time — this is why the numbers in the comparison table are split into "during term" and "breaks."

There's a difference between "working" and "self-employment"

This is a subtle but crucial point. Being an employee of a cafe or campus library is usually clearly allowed (within hourly limits). But self-employment — freelancing, running your own business, being an independent contractor — is a completely different category, and many student visas prohibit it outright. This matters because a lot of students assume "freelancing online" is fine, only to discover it violates their visa. If you plan to do gig work or freelancing, check your specific visa conditions very carefully.

Every country asks for a tax identification number

You cannot legally work without a tax number in any major study destination. The USA needs an SSN, the UK a NINo, Canada a SIN, Australia a TFN, Ireland a PPSN, New Zealand an IRD number, and Germany a tax ID. Getting these takes time, so apply as soon as your visa is approved and you have an address. Don't wait until you've found a job — work on getting the number early.

Some jobs are restricted no matter the hours

Even within your hourly allowance, some types of work are off-limits on student visas. Common examples include jobs involving certain medical/regulated professions, roles that could compromise your student status, and in some countries, "gig economy" or casual platform work. Always confirm your specific conditions rather than assuming.

These fundamentals apply no matter where you study, so keep them in mind as we walk through each country.

Country-by-country work rules for international students

United States (F-1 visa)

In the US, F-1 students can work on campus for up to 20 hours per week during the academic term and up to 40 hours during breaks. Off-campus work is restricted in the first year and requires specific authorization (CPT or OPT) afterward. Common part-time jobs for international students on US campuses include library assistants, dining hall workers, and research assistants. The federal minimum wage is $7.25/hour, but campus jobs in many states pay $10–$15/hour.

Let me unpack the US rules a little more, because they're more nuanced than a simple hour limit. In your first academic year, F-1 students may generally only work on campus. After that first year, if you want to work off-campus, you need authorization. There are three main paths:

  • On-campus employment: Working for the university itself — libraries, dining, admin, research labs. This is the easiest because no separate authorization is needed and employers know the rules.
  • CPT (Curricular Practical Training): Off-campus work that's an integral part of your curriculum, like an internship, co-op, or practicum. It must be tied to your major and approved in advance. CPT is often unpaid or paid depending on the program.
  • OPT (Optional Practical Training): Up to 12 months of work related to your degree, usually taken after graduation. STEM graduates can extend this. OPT requires filing paperwork with US Immigration (USCIS) and can take months to be approved, so apply well ahead of your intended start date.

A few practical US notes. First, to get paid at most campus jobs, you'll need a Social Security Number (SSN), which you can apply for once you have an SSN-eligible campus job offer. Second, "economic hardship" employment is available in rare circumstances, but it's a strict process. And third, the US does not let F-1 students work the informal cash-in-hand gig economy the way some other countries do — self-employment on an F-1 visa is generally not allowed. That's a big difference worth internalizing early.

United Kingdom (Student visa)

The UK allows international students to work up to 20 hours per week during term time and full-time during vacations if your course is at degree level or above. The UK minimum wage for workers aged 21+ is £11.44/hour (National Living Wage). You need a National Insurance Number (NINo) to work legally.

There's an important condition worth flagging: the 20-hour limit applies if you're studying at degree level (or above) at a higher education provider. If you're on a below-degree-level course (like some shorter English language or foundation programs), your allowance may be more restricted, typically 10 hours per week. Check your visa's specific conditions — they're printed on the visa itself — rather than assuming.

The UK is one of the most student-friendly job markets. It's common to find work in retail, hospitality, bars and restaurants, campus jobs, and private tutoring. There's also a huge demand for student ambassadors and part-time roles in the gig economy (though confirm your self-employment status before freelance work). Universities in the UK have dedicated careers services and online job portals specifically for students, which is where most people find their first job. Getting your NINo should be your first step — you can apply once you have your visa and a UK address, and it can take a few weeks to arrive.

One thing UK students love: working full-time during the long summer break. If your program runs September to June, you get a genuine chance to earn serious money from June to September. Many students save most of their living costs for the year during summer.

Canada (Study permit)

Canada updated its rules in 2024. As of 2026, international students at designated learning institutions can work up to 24 hours per week off-campus during regular academic sessions and full-time during scheduled breaks. You need a Social Insurance Number (SIN) to work. The federal minimum wage is $17.30/hour (varies by province).

Canada's rules are among the most generous for part-time work for international students because off-campus work is allowed from day one (no first-year restriction). This matters a lot, because it means you can start job hunting immediately after you arrive rather than waiting a year like in the US. After graduation, the Post-Graduation Work Permit (PGWP) allows 1–3 years of full-time work.

A few practical notes for Canada. First, studying full-time is a requirement — the "24 hours per week" allowance only applies while you're a full-time student at a designated learning institution (DLI). If you drop below full-time status, you lose your work eligibility. Second, minimum wages vary by province and go up over time; Alberta and Ontario are common destinations. Third, get your SIN early — you need a study permit and can apply for a SIN once you're in Canada. Fourth, popular jobs for international students include retail, restaurants, call centres, campuses, and increasingly warehouse and delivery work during busy seasons.

For a full picture of part-time jobs for international students in Canada — how the study permit rules, your SIN, and provincial minimum wages combine — the study in Canada guide covers the country's work conditions and living costs in one place, and it connects directly to the post-study options you'll face after graduation.

Australia (Student visa subclass 500)

Australia permits international students to work up to 48 hours per fortnight (two weeks) during term and unlimited hours during scheduled breaks. The minimum wage is AUD 24.10/hour (as of July 2025). You need a Tax File Number (TFN) to work legally.

Australia's minimum wage is one of the highest in the world, making part-time work for international students genuinely meaningful income. A few hours a week at the Australian wage can cover a real chunk of living costs. Common jobs include hospitality, retail, cleaning, and delivery driving. Campus jobs exist but are more competitive than in the US or UK.

Australia is worth a little extra attention because of two things. First, the "48 hours per fortnight" rule means you average 24 hours per week, but it's flexible across the two-week window — some students work more one week and less the next, as long as they stay within 48 over the fortnight. Many employers also factor in that you can work unlimited during scheduled breaks, so seasonal hospitality jobs are full-time friendly. Second, Australia's rules show up in the reality that hospitality and farming jobs are a genuine, common path for students, especially in regional areas. Getting a TFN is quick and free online. Just remember that working in Australia as a student still requires you to maintain your full-time study load and meet your course's attendance requirements.

The best way to plan part-time jobs for international students in Australia is to combine these work rules with a realistic sense of living costs and visa conditions — that full picture is in our complete guide to studying in Australia, which details student visa conditions alongside costs.

Germany

Germany has a unique system: international students from outside the EU can work 140 full days or 240 half days per year (no weekly hour limit). This works out to roughly 20 hours/week if spread across the year. EU/EEA students have no restrictions. The minimum wage is €12.41/hour.

German part-time jobs for international students often include HiWi (research assistant) positions at the university, which are directly related to your studies and pay well. Students in Germany can also work as tutors, in cafés, or in the tech sector (especially in Berlin, Munich, and Hamburg). After graduation, Germany offers an 18-month job seeker visa.

Let me explain Germany's day-based system because it confuses people. You're allowed 140 full days or 240 half days per calendar year. If you work a standard full working day, that counts as one full day. If you work a half day (a few hours), it counts as half a day. So you have real flexibility — you could work 20 hours a week across the year and stay within your allowance, or concentrate your work into certain months. There are two exceptions worth knowing:

  • HiWi positions: Work directly tied to your studies (research assistant, tutor, etc.) is treated more favourably and may be exempt from the day limits.
  • Students from EU/EEA countries face no hour or day limits at all, since EU free-movement rules apply.

Working as a freelancer is also possible in Germany but requires separate permissions and isn't counted like normal employment, so it's best avoided unless you have specific approval. Germany's biggest perk is the after-graduation path: a genuine 18-month job-seeking visa that has made many students into long-term residents.

Ireland

Ireland allows international students to work up to 20 hours per week during term and 40 hours during holidays (for students studying at institution-recognized programs). The minimum wage is €13.50/hour. You need a PPS Number to work and pay tax.

There's a catch worth knowing about Ireland: the 20-hour rule normally applies, but it can be reduced to 10 hours per week for some students on certain English-language or foundation courses. Also, whether you can work is tied to your course being on the approved list (typically at least one year long and leading to a recognized qualification). Ireland's hospitality and tech sectors both hire student workers, and Dublin has lively student job markets. Getting a PPS number (the tax number) should be your first move.

Netherlands

Non-EU students in the Netherlands can work up to 16 hours per week year-round, or full-time during June, July, and August only. EU students have no restrictions. The minimum wage is approximately €13.27/hour (ages 21+). You need a work permit (TWV) sponsored by your employer.

The Netherlands has a distinctive rule: while you can work year-round up to 16 hours a week, your employer has to hold a TWV (work permit) for you. That means you can't freelance or work in most informal cash-in-hand roles — the employer is legally required to arrange the permit. In practice, many internationals work in hospitality, logistics, and seasonal work during the three summer months when full-time work is allowed. After graduation, the Netherlands offers a "search year" (zoekjaar) visa for graduates to find work, which is genuinely valuable.

New Zealand

International students in New Zealand can work up to 20 hours per week during term and full-time during scheduled breaks, provided you're studying full-time at an institution on the approved list. The minimum wage is NZD 23.50/hour. You need an IRD number for tax purposes.

New Zealand's rule is straightforward: 20 hours a week during term, full-time over breaks, and it applies to students on an exchange or at a full-time program. You'll need an IRD number for tax. Popular jobs include hospitality, retail, and tourism work (particularly in popular student towns like Auckland, Wellington, and Christchurch). New Zealand is smaller, so the job market is more relaxed but also more limited — it helps to be flexible about the kind of work you'll take.

Choosing a country: how work opportunities fit into your decision

If working while you study (or after you graduate) matters to you, your choice of country is one of the most important decisions you'll make. The work rules I've laid out above are a huge factor, but they're not the only one. Here's how to weigh them.

The hourly allowance and the wage aren't the whole story

Two countries might both let you work 20 hours a week, but feel totally different in practice. Consider the minimum wage, the cost of living, and crucially, how available student jobs actually are in your city. A high wage in an expensive city with fierce competition may be harder to turn into real money than a lower wage in a cheaper town with plenty of jobs.

Think about the path after graduation

For many students, part-time work is a bridge to post-study employment. Countries differ enormously here: Canada's PGWP, the UK's Graduate route, Germany's job-seeker visa, Australia's Temporary Graduate visa, and Ireland's Third Level Graduate Scheme all offer different lengths and conditions. If your long-term plan is to work and settle, the post-study work path may matter more than the part-time hourly limit. Our post-study work visa guide by country compares these in detail.

Language matters

In English-speaking countries, your existing English is usually enough to get a student job. In Germany, the Netherlands, and parts of Canada, knowing the local language dramatically widens your job options and pay. A student who speaks German will find far more (and better-paying) jobs in Germany than one who doesn't. Factor in how long you're willing to spend learning the local language.

Match your field to local demand

Tech students in Berlin or Dublin, finance students in London, and healthcare students in Canada or Australia all find that their field has strong local demand, which helps both part-time and post-study work. If your industry has a shortage in your destination country, you have a significant advantage.

Once you've picked a country that fits your work needs, the next practical step is nailing the funding. Working while you study is a great supplement, but your visa is granted largely on the funds you show upfront — so read our proof of funds by country guide to make sure your finances are in order before you even board the plane.

Best part-time jobs for international students

The easiest part-time jobs for international students to get depend on your country, language skills, and visa restrictions. Here are the most common options:

  • On-campus jobs: Library assistants, dining hall staff, computer lab monitors, and administrative assistants. These are the easiest to get because employers understand visa rules and student schedules.
  • Teaching/Research assistants: If you're a graduate student, TA and RA positions are common, pay well, and count toward your degree. Very common in Germany (HiWi positions) and the US.
  • Tutoring: Tutoring other students or language tutoring (teaching your native language) is flexible and well-paid.
  • Retail and hospitality: Shops, restaurants, and cafés are the most common employers of international students working part-time in the UK, Australia, and Ireland.
  • Delivery and gig work: Food delivery (Uber Eats, Deliveroo) is popular in the UK and Australia but check if your visa allows self-employment.
  • Freelancing: Some countries allow freelance work on a student visa (Germany does for unlimited hours), but most do not — check carefully.

Where to actually find the jobs

Knowing the rules is only half the battle — you also need to know where the jobs are advertised. This is one of the most practical parts of the whole process, so let me give you a genuine map of where international students find work.

Your university is your best friend

Almost every university runs a dedicated student jobs portal. These are gold, because every posting is (usually) already checked to be visa-compliant, and the employers understand they're hiring a student with hour limits. On top of the portal, look for:

  • Careers services: They run workshops, help with your CV and cover letters for local employers, and have direct relationships with companies that hire students.
  • Campus notice boards and newsletters: Old-school but effective. Departments, libraries, and labs post casual positions here.
  • Faculty and professors: Especially for research assistant and teaching assistant roles, asking a professor you know personally is one of the most effective ways in.
  • Student unions: They run cafes, shops, bars, and events, and they're very used to hiring international students.

Outside the campus

For local jobs, a few channels consistently work:

  • National job sites: Each country has its own — Reed, Indeed, and the government's Find-a-job in the UK; Seek in Australia and New Zealand; Workopolis/Indeed in Canada; the Federal Employment Agency (Bundesagentur für Arbeit) and StepStone in Germany.
  • Hospitality networks: Word of mouth is enormous in bars, restaurants, and cafes. Go in person, ask to speak to a manager, hand over a paper CV. In many hotel and retail jobs, this still works better than an online application.
  • Local job fairs: Universities and cities run regular job fairs where employers actively recruit students.
  • Specialist student agencies: Some agencies specialize in placing international students in casual work. Use caution and only use recognized ones.
  • University partnerships: Many universities have agreements with local employers to place students — ask your careers office.

The reality of timing

Here's a timing tip that saves students real frustration: the best time to find a job is before the semester starts and during the first few weeks, when employers are hiring for the year and other students haven't yet flooded the market. Waiting until after your first month means competing with everyone else who waited too. If you can, line up a job offer in advance or start applying the day you land.

Making money actually work for you: budgeting on a student wage

Here's the thing nobody says clearly: a part-time job can either be money that runs through your fingers, or it can genuinely transform your student experience. The difference is budgeting. Let me give you a realistic framework.

Know your true hourly "take-home"

Your hourly wage is never what actually lands in your bank account. Taxes (and sometimes a pension contribution) get taken out before you see the money. For example, a £11.44/hour UK rate might leave you with roughly £9.50–£10.50 depending on your tax code and how much you earn in the year. Always work from your net — post-tax — hourly figure when you budget, not the headline number.

Work out what your hours actually buy you

Sit down and calculate: if you work 20 hours a week at your net rate, how much is that per week, per month, and per term? Then subtract what you actually need for food, transport, phone, and discretionary spending. This tells you whether your job covers your costs or just supplements family support — and that changes how many hours you truly need.

Typical living costs to plan around

Living costs vary wildly by city. A student in a UK regional city, a Canadian town, or a German small city will have much lower costs than someone in London, Toronto, or Munich. As a rough guide, rent often eats 40–60% of a student budget, food 15–25%, and transport, phone, and other costs the rest. Our study abroad budget guide breaks this down in detail for each major destination, so it's worth reading alongside this article.

Tax, benefits, and getting paid: what you need to know

Tax is the most annoying but most important part of working as an international student, so let me give you a clear, practical rundown that applies in most countries.

You almost always need a tax number first

As mentioned, you'll need an SSN (US), NINo (UK), SIN (Canada), TFN (Australia), PPSN (Ireland), IRD number (New Zealand), or German tax ID before legitimate employers can pay you legally. If you work without one, you risk being paid under the table, which can cause problems with both tax authorities and your visa.

Your tax number is not the same as work permission

It's a common and dangerous misconception that having a tax number means you're allowed to work. It doesn't. The tax number just identifies you for tax purposes. Your actual right to work comes from your visa conditions. Always rely on your visa, not your tax number, to know what you're allowed to do.

The good news: students often pay little or no tax

Here's the pleasant surprise for many students: because your earnings are low relative to the annual tax-free allowance, a lot of part-time student work ends up costing little in tax. In the UK, for example, there's a tax-free personal allowance, and students working part-time often don't exceed it. In Australia, there's a tax-free threshold you can claim. In Canada, low earners may get most tax back at filing season. The practical takeaway: file your tax return every year (or follow the local instructions), because as a low earner you may be owed a refund.

Minimum wage and your rights

You are entitled to the minimum wage in every country, no matter what your employer might imply about you being "just a student." You're also entitled to basic workplace rights — meal breaks, safe working conditions, and in most places, holiday pay. These are not privileges; they're legal entitlements. If an employer tries to pay you below minimum wage, that's a red flag (and likely a scam or an exploitative situation you should avoid).

Avoiding work scams and exploitation

Unfortunately, international students are a target for work scams, partly because they're eager to find money and partly because they don't always know their rights. Let me arm you with the warning signs so you protect yourself.

Red flags to watch for

  • Being asked to pay to get a job. Legitimate employers never charge you to apply or to "secure" a position. Any "registration fee," "training fee," or "processing fee" is a scam.
  • Offers that sound too good. "Work from home, earn £500 a day, no experience needed" is never real. Real student jobs aren't that glamorous.
  • Pressure to act fast. Scammers create urgency so you don't think or check. A genuine employer won't demand you decide in an hour.
  • Requests for banking/login details. Never give a stranger your bank password, PIN, or details "for payment."
  • Job offers via WhatsApp, Telegram, or random emails. Genuine student jobs rarely come through unsolicited messaging.
  • "Cheque overpayment" schemes. A classic: someone "overpays" you and asks you to send the difference back. The original cheque bounces, and you're out the money.
  • Visa advice attached to a job offer. Untrained people offering to fix your "visa problem" in exchange for work are almost always scammers.

How to protect yourself

  • Only apply through official channels — university portals, recognized job sites, or in person at the business.
  • Never pay anyone to find you a job.
  • If a "recruiter" asks for money or bank details, walk away immediately and report it to your university or the local authorities.
  • Trust your instincts. If something feels off, it usually is.

If you ever worry you've been caught by a scam, or you see an opportunity that looks fraudulent, tell your university's careers or student support team — they are genuinely there to help and have seen it all before.

Five practical tips for your first weeks of work

Let me give you a short, actionable list that covers the stuff nobody warns you about in your first weeks of working as an international student:

  1. Sort out your tax number before you even need it. It can take weeks to arrive, so apply as soon as you have a valid address.
  2. Open a local bank account early. Most employers can only pay into a local account. Opening one usually needs your passport, visa, and proof of address.
  3. Keep a copy of your visa conditions. Take a photo and keep it somewhere safe. You'll want to double-check your hour limits before you sign any contract.
  4. Don't be afraid to ask about pay dates and how you'll be paid. It's normal to ask "when do I get paid and how?" before you start.
  5. Track your hours yourself. Don't rely solely on your employer's records. Keep your own simple log (a note on your phone is fine) so you can verify your payslips and never accidentally go over your visa limit.

From part-time student job to career: how working pays off after graduation

One of the best reasons to work as an international student isn't the money — it's the head start it gives your career. Especially if you plan to stay on after graduation, a part-time job while studying can be the difference between a smooth transition and a stressful scramble.

  • Local experience counts for a lot. Employers in destinations like Canada, the UK, and Australia value local work experience, even part-time. It shows you can function in their workplace culture.
  • References and reputation. A strong reference from a local employer is worth far more than most students realize when they later apply for graduate jobs.
  • It feeds your post-study visa eligibility. Countries that offer post-study work (like the PGWP in Canada or the UK Graduate route) don't require part-time work to qualify, but having local experience makes you far more competitive when you do apply for full-time graduate roles.
  • Some countries reward it indirectly. In places like Germany, work experience and language skills feed directly into your ability to transition to a full-time job-seeker or work visa.

In short, working part-time while you study isn't just about paying the rent — for many students it's the first rung on the ladder of a genuine career in a new country.

Does part-time work affect your scholarship?

If you're on a fully funded scholarship, check the terms carefully. Some scholarships like Chevening and Fulbright allow part-time work but may have hour restrictions. Others may prohibit employment entirely. Violating scholarship work terms can result in loss of funding. If you need to work, explore funding options that allow employment.

Tips for finding part-time work as an international student

  1. Start with campus job boards — they're designed for students and visa-compliant.
  2. Apply early — popular campus positions fill up before the semester starts.
  3. Get your tax number first — you cannot legally work without it in any country.
  4. Build a simple CV — highlight transferable skills even if you don't have local work experience.
  5. Network with classmates — many jobs are found through word of mouth.
  6. Check university career services — they often have partnerships with local employers.

Common misconceptions about working as an international student

Because so much misinformation floats around in student forums and social media, let me clear up the myths that cause the most confusion and, sometimes, real trouble.

"Getting a tax number means I'm allowed to work"

As I stressed earlier, this is false. A tax number just registers you for tax. It grants no work rights whatsoever. Your visa conditions are the only thing that determines whether and how much you can work. Don't be the person who "knew someone who worked full-time with just a tax number" — that person was breaking their visa conditions.

"If I don't get caught, it's fine"

This is the costliest myth of all. Countries increasingly cross-check employment records, tax filings, and visa databases. A breach that surfaces when you apply to renew your visa, apply for a post-study work permit, or later try to settle, can scupper everything. The short-term money is never worth risking your entire immigration history over.

"On-campus and off-campus hours can both be maxed out"

In most countries, the hourly limit is a total across all employers and all work — on-campus plus off-campus. You can't work 20 hours at a campus job and another 20 hours at a cafe if your visa allows only 20. Add up everything.

"I can always work full-time in the holidays"

Only during scheduled breaks recognized by your institution. Random weeks off, reading weeks, or days when you don't have classes are not automatically "breaks." If in doubt, check your institution's academic calendar or your visa conditions.

"Scholarships always stop me from working"

Not always. It depends entirely on the scholarship terms. Some fully funded scholarships permit part-time work; others prohibit it. The mistake isn't wanting to work — it's not reading your scholarship agreement. Plenty of students on generous scholarships still work a few hours for experience. Just check your specific terms first, as covered in our Chevening and Fulbright guides, and the general scholarships hub.

"Working is proof I can afford to stay"

No. Visa officers assess your financial situation at application time, mostly based on your declared funds and sponsorship — not on your future part-time earnings. You can't use "I'll get a job" to meet a proof-of-funds requirement. That's a separate matter, explained in our proof of funds guide and the broader proof of funds by country article.

Working during breaks, holidays, and summer: a bonus strategy

Here's a genuinely smart strategy that many international students use to dramatically reduce their financial stress: leverage your holiday periods for full-time work. Since most countries allow full-time work during scheduled breaks, a serious summer or winter-break job can cover a big slice of your year's living costs.

In the UK, for example, a full-time summer job at the National Living Wage can earn a meaningful amount toward your next year's rent. In Australia, seasonal hospitality and farm work over the long summer break is extremely common among students — and even connects to working-holiday-style income that some students rely on. In Canada, working full-time over the winter and summer breaks is standard practice.

To make the most of it:

  • Plan your breaks before they arrive. Employers hire seasonal staff weeks in advance. Apply during term when break jobs are being advertised.
  • Tell your regular employer first. Many students convert their part-time term job into full-time break work — you're already known and trusted.
  • Save the bulk of it. Resist the urge to spend break earnings as fast as you earn them. That money is your cushion for the following term.

What if you can't work at all on your visa?

Some visas genuinely don't allow work, or only with heavy restrictions. If you're on a course or visa type that prohibits employment, you're not stuck — you have alternatives. And these are worth knowing whether or not you can work.

  • Your first defense is good funding. The best solution is to arrive with a funding plan that doesn't depend on earning money on the side. See the options for funding studies without a full scholarship.
  • Scholarships and bursaries. Merit- and need-based awards reduce the pressure to work. Our scholarships hub has free money you don't repay.
  • Grants from your university. Many universities have emergency funds, hardship grants, and work-study-like programs for students who can't work externally.
  • Paid internships through your program. If your course includes a placement, internship, or co-op year, that's often a permitted, well-paid work period that doesn't count against your general work limit.
  • Volunteering. While unpaid, volunteering builds your CV, network, and local references — and is usually permitted even when paid employment isn't.

Whichever situation you're in, know your visa conditions cold, and build your plan around what they actually allow rather than what you hope they allow.

Final thoughts: work as a tool, not a trap

Here's the takeaway I want to leave you with. A part-time job as an international student is a genuinely powerful tool. It can pay your bills, fund your experience, build your CV, grow your language skills, and open a door to a career in a new country. But it can also quietly become the thing that derails your studies, drains your energy, and — if you push past your legal limits — jeopardizes your entire immigration future.

The students who get it right do three things consistently. First, they know their visa conditions cold and never gamble with them. Second, they treat their studies as the non-negotiable priority, keeping work hours sensible and manageable. And third, they use work strategically — for the money, the experience, and the network — rather than letting it take over their life.

Wherever you decide to study, let the work rules in this guide be your starting point, but always confirm the details against your own visa and your institution's guidance, because rules do change. And remember: the point of studying abroad isn't just to earn money — it's to build the future you came for. A part-time job should help you reach it, not hold you back.

Frequently asked questions

How many hours can international students work?+

Work hour limits vary by country. The US limits on-campus work to 20 hours/week during term. The UK allows 20 hours/week during term. Canada allows 24 hours/week off-campus. Australia allows 48 hours per fortnight. Germany has no weekly limit but caps at 140 full days or 240 half days per year. Rules change frequently, so check your visa conditions.

Can international students work off-campus?+

It depends on the country and visa type. In the US, F-1 students generally cannot work off-campus in the first year. In the UK, most student visa holders can work off-campus up to 20 hours/week during term. In Canada, international students can work off-campus up to 24 hours/week if studying full-time at a designated institution. In Australia, you can work off-campus with no restriction on employer, up to 48 hours per fortnight.

What are the best part-time jobs for international students?+

Common part-time jobs for international students include campus library assistants, teaching assistants, research assistants, retail assistants, food service workers, tutors, baristas, and customer service representatives. On-campus jobs are often easier to get because they understand student schedules and visa restrictions.

Do international students pay tax on part-time work?+

Yes, in most countries international students must pay income tax on part-time earnings. In the UK, you need a National Insurance Number. In Canada, you need a SIN. In Australia, you need a TFN. In the US, F-1 students on campus jobs may be exempt from FICA taxes but still pay federal income tax. Tax rates vary by country and income level.

Does part-time work affect my student visa?+

Working more than your visa allows is a serious violation that can lead to visa cancellation and deportation. Always track your hours carefully. Some countries also require you to maintain full-time enrollment — dropping below full-time status to work more can violate your visa conditions. Some scholarship programs also have work restrictions.

Do I need a tax number before I can start a job?+

Yes, in almost every country. Legitimate employers cannot pay you properly without a tax number (SSN in the US, NINo in the UK, SIN in Canada, TFN in Australia, PPSN in Ireland, IRD in New Zealand, tax ID in Germany). Apply for it as early as possible, as it can take weeks to arrive. A tax number does not give you the right to work — that comes from your visa — but you'll need it to be paid legally.

Can I work full-time during holidays?+

In most countries, yes — during recognized scheduled breaks (summer, winter, and spring holidays). During term time, you must stay within your hourly limit. Each country is different: the UK, Canada, Ireland, and New Zealand allow full-time work during scheduled breaks, as does Australia. Germany uses a day-based system. Always confirm which periods count as official breaks for your institution.

What is the minimum wage for international students?+

You're entitled to the same minimum wage as local workers. In 2026, approximate rates include AUD 24.10/hour (Australia), £11.44/hour (UK), €12.41/hour (Germany), €13.50/hour (Ireland), $17.30/hour federal (Canada), NZD 23.50/hour (New Zealand), and $7.25/hour federal in the US (though state and campus rates are usually higher). These change, so check current figures. Being a student doesn't mean you can be paid less than minimum wage.

Can international students work remotely or freelance?+

Usually not without specific permission. Freelancing and self-employment are treated differently from regular employment in most countries and are often prohibited or heavily restricted on student visas. Working remotely for a company back home may also count against your work limits or be disallowed. Check your visa conditions carefully before doing gig work, freelancing, or remote work — the rules differ sharply from country to country.

Can I work if I'm on a fully funded scholarship?+

It depends on your scholarship's specific terms. Some fully funded scholarships allow part-time work (often with hour limits); others prohibit it entirely. Always read your scholarship agreement, and if you're unsure, ask the award administrator. Violating a scholarship's work rule can cost you your funding. If your scholarship prohibits work, explore grants, bursaries, and unpaid volunteering instead.

What should I do if I'm worried about working more than my visa allows?+

Keep your own record of hours worked across all jobs, and know your exact limit. If you find you're at risk of exceeding it, reduce your hours immediately. If you've already over-worked, speak honestly to your university's international student advisor and consider qualified immigration advice before your next visa application. Never try to hide it — being upfront is almost always the safer path.
