IAESTE Internships: Paid Work Abroad for STEM

🗓️ Aug 5, 2026⏱️ 9 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

What are IAESTE internships?

IAESTE internships are among the longest-running and most reliable ways for STEM students to earn real work experience abroad. IAESTE, the International Association for the Exchange of Students for Technical Experience, has been arranging paid technical traineeships since 1948, and today its network spans more than 80 member countries. If you are studying engineering, computer science, mathematics or the natural sciences, an IAESTE placement can put you to work in a company, university or research institute on the other side of the world, with a salary attached rather than a footnote at the bottom of the offer letter.

What does this guide cover?

This guide explains what IAESTE actually does, who can apply, how the exchange model works, and how to move through the application process step by step, from registering on the exchange platform to clearing your visa and arriving at your desk on day one. The single biggest mistake students make with IAESTE is misunderstanding how the system works, so we will make that clear first. We also cover realistic costs, how the stipend is calculated, which countries offer the most placements, and the mistakes that quietly sink otherwise strong applications.

Key takeaways

What are the essentials of the IAESTE programme?

If you only have time to remember a handful of facts about IAESTE internships, make them these. The points below summarise how the programme is structured, how long placements usually run, who actually pays you, and where your application really goes.

  • IAESTE arranges paid technical traineeships for STEM students in more than 80 countries.
  • Most placements last 8 to 12 weeks over the summer, though longer internships are available.
  • You apply through your national IAESTE committee, not directly to a central portal.
  • Host employers pay a salary or stipend designed to cover local living costs.
  • The receiving committee helps with housing, insurance and a social programme for interns.

Why does the structure of the exchange matter to your application?

Because IAESTE internships are an exchange rather than a job board, the pathway is narrow and strictly sequential: register, engage your national committee, get nominated, get accepted, then prepare. There is no parallel track where you can email a hundred employers while your committee works behind the scenes, and understanding that early is the difference between a smooth application and a wasted year. If a single line in the list above is the one most students get wrong, it is the third one, because most candidates arrive expecting to apply directly to a host company and only discover the committee step after the deadlines have passed.

How far in advance should I start thinking about an IAESTE placement?

Start now, whatever stage you are at. The full cycle from application to confirmed placement typically takes four to eight months, and popular destinations and placement types fill early because placements are allocated on a rolling basis through international exchange. Students who apply in the autumn for a summer placement are in a far stronger position than those who apply in the spring, and the difference is often a choice of country rather than a choice of whether you get a placement at all.

What is IAESTE and how does it work?

What kind of organisation is IAESTE?

IAESTE is a non-profit organisation built on a reciprocal exchange model. It is not an agency, a recruitment consultancy or a company that profits by placing students. It is a membership network of national committees, and the entire institution exists to move technical students between countries so that employers and students on both sides benefit. That non-profit character explains a great deal about how IAESTE internships feel in practice: the process is slower than a private placement agency, the paperwork is heavier, and the support continues well after you land.

How does the reciprocal exchange model work?

A company or university in one member country offers a paid traineeship, and an IAESTE national committee in another country sends a qualified student to fill it. In return, the sending country expects to receive interns for its own employers. This give-and-take keeps the system fair and means that even students from countries with fewer local opportunities can access placements elsewhere. Every internship you receive is, in accounting terms, a slot that your own country has paid for by sending somebody out, which is why the numbers of outgoing and incoming interns are tracked so carefully by every committee.

Do I apply directly to employers for IAESTE internships?

In practice, you do not apply directly to employers for most positions. Instead, your national committee matches your profile to the offers available to your country and nominates you, which is why contacting your local IAESTE committee early is essential. Your committee holds the list of placements that your country is entitled to send interns against, and only a nominated candidate can be put forward. A host employer never sees an unsolicited CV through IAESTE, no matter how strong it is, so time spent optimising an application to a specific company is time better spent making your committee profile as competitive as possible.

Who decides where I end up working?

Three parties share the decision, and understanding the split makes the process feel less arbitrary. Your national committee chooses which candidates to nominate and for which offers. The IAESTE International office runs the matching step that pairs nominated candidates with the placements host committees have submitted. The host committee in the receiving country then reviews the matched candidate and holds the final say on acceptance. Because no single office controls the outcome, the strength of your profile at every one of those three stages matters.

Who can apply?

Which students are eligible for IAESTE internships?

IAESTE internships are aimed at students currently enrolled in a bachelor's, master's or PhD programme, and some committees also accept recent graduates, typically within about a year of finishing. The core focus is STEM: engineering, science, mathematics, information technology and related technical fields. Some positions are open to students from other disciplines, but the vast majority are technical. Enrolment status is the detail most people miss, because a committee checking your registration date will reject an application from someone who has already finished and is no longer a current student, even if the rest of the profile is excellent.

Do I need to be a citizen of an IAESTE member country?

You do not need to be a citizen of a member country to apply, but you usually apply through the committee in the country where you study, so check how your institution or country is connected to the network. An international student studying in Germany, for example, typically routes their application through the German committee structure rather than through their nationality, because the exchange obligation sits with the country of study. The practical first step for most people is therefore not a search for foreign embassies but a search for their own country's IAESTE office and a conversation with their university's international or exchange office.

Does my field of study limit me to one type of placement?

It limits you more than applicants expect, but less than employers expect. If you are a chemical engineering student, the placements open to you are chemical engineering placements, and the committee matching step will not push you into a software role just because a host is short-staffed. Broadening your appeal therefore means broadening within your discipline: a mechanical engineer who also lists CAD, finite element analysis and a second European language is a more attractive candidate than one who lists only the degree title. Committees rank on the detail they can match against a project description, not on the prestige of the degree.

Are IAESTE internships really paid?

Are IAESTE internships actually paid?

Yes. Every placement in the network is a paid traineeship, which is the single fact that separates IAESTE internships from the large majority of student exchange programmes. Host employers provide a salary or stipend calculated to cover the local cost of living, so the exact amount varies enormously by country and position. A placement in Switzerland will pay far more in absolute terms than one in Brazil, but both are normally enough for a student to live on during the internship. If a programme advertises an unpaid "IAESTE-style" placement, it is not an IAESTE placement, and you should be careful about who is actually brokering it.

Is the payment a salary or a stipend?

It depends on the host, and the terminology is not standardised. Some employers treat the intern as a payroll employee and pay a formal monthly salary through the company's HR system. Others pay a traineeship allowance calculated to meet the local cost-of-living benchmark, sometimes with housing provided separately on top. Industrial companies are more likely to pay a cash salary, while universities and research institutes more often pay a stipend tied to local rates for research students. Both are legitimate, and both are designed around the same principle: the amount should be sufficient that a student can live on it for the duration of the placement.

How does accommodation and the social programme work?

Housing is typically arranged or subsidised by the receiving committee, and many committees organise welcome activities, trips and a social programme so interns from different countries meet each other. That combination, a living wage, accommodation support and a built-in community, is what makes an IAESTE summer genuinely affordable. Interns routinely say the social programme matters as much financially as it does socially, because a packed itinerary of organised trips is a very effective way to keep a low stipend from being drained by weekends.

How to apply step by step

The process is slightly different in every country, but it follows the same broad pattern, and the steps always appear in this order.

Step 1: Where do I find offers and how do I register?

Search open internship offers on the IAESTE website and register on the exchange platform. Once registered, your national committee can see your profile and match you to offers. This is the step where most people lose momentum, because the platform profile is not a finished application. It is a searchable record that committees use when deciding whom to nominate, so the fields you fill in during registration should already reflect your field, your technical skills, your language levels and your country preferences. A thin profile that gets registered in July and never updated is effectively invisible to the matching team.

Step 2: How do I contact my national committee?

Your committee guides you through the application, tells you which offers are available to your country, and helps you prepare the required documents, typically a cover letter, CV, transcripts and sometimes language certificates. Committees are run by volunteers alongside full-time staff, and their bandwidth is finite, so the quality of your first email matters. Tell them your field, your year of study, your graduation date, your language levels and the destinations you can realistically reach, and ask for their current deadlines. Committees publish different submission windows each cycle, and only the committee knows the dates that actually matter for your country.

Step 3: How do I get nominated and accepted?

If the committee selects you for an offer, your documents are forwarded to the employer, who makes the final decision. Once accepted, you receive details about your start date, insurance and accommodation, and the committee helps with visa paperwork where needed. There is a difference between being nominated and being accepted, and the gap between them can be several weeks of silence. Nominations are often processed in batches, so a lack of news is not a rejection. When you are accepted, confirm in writing, then immediately start the visa and insurance threads, because those are the items most likely to delay a start date that has already been promised to your host.

How long does the whole step-by-step process take?

From your first email to a national committee to a confirmed placement, expect four to eight months. Registration and document preparation take a few weeks if you start early and considerably longer if you start late. Committee review and nomination add several more weeks. Matching, host review and acceptance commonly take one to three months, and the slowest stage is usually the one you do not control, because it depends on how many host committees are reviewing candidates for that cycle. Visa and insurance arrangements should then begin immediately, which is why the whole thing feels comfortable only if you start six to eight months ahead of your intended start date.

What documents do you need?

What documents do committees ask for?

Prepare a strong cover letter and an up-to-date CV that highlights relevant coursework, projects and technical skills. Many committees also ask for academic transcripts and evidence of English proficiency, since English is the working language for most positions even when the host country speaks another language. Beyond those core items, expect a signed application form, a copy of your enrolment or graduation certificate, and in some countries a language certificate such as IELTS or TOEFL. Requirements differ by committee, so treat the list you get from your own committee as the definitive one rather than any generic checklist you find online.

How do I write a persuasive motivation letter for IAESTE?

Write specifically about the kind of work you want to do, why that country or that type of organisation interests you, and how the placement connects to your academic and career goals. Generic statements that could describe any placement in any field are the single most common reason strong students are passed over during committee selection. If you are unsure how to structure a persuasive motivation letter, our guide to writing a winning statement of purpose covers techniques that apply here too. Keep the letter to one page, lead with a specific technical interest rather than with a generic wish to "broaden my horizons", and finish by naming the kind of host you are realistically suited to.

What is the fastest way to strengthen my IAESTE application?

Improve the specificity of everything you submit, because specificity is the only thing committees and hosts are short of. On your CV, name the languages, tools, laboratory techniques and software you have actually used rather than the categories they belong to. On your transcripts, make sure the document is legible and complete, because unreadable files routinely cause delays that cost students a cycle. In your platform profile, list every destination you would genuinely accept, since an over-narrow preference list is the most common self-inflicted reason for a slow match. Consistency across the CV, the letter and the platform profile also signals that you are organised, which is exactly what a host supervisor wants to hear.

Visas and insurance

Which visa do I need for an IAESTE placement?

Because you travel as a trainee rather than a student, visa rules differ from country to country. Some placements are short enough to fall under a visitor visa, while longer internships require a specific work or training visa. European countries within the Schengen area may require a long-stay visa for placements exceeding 90 days, Canada typically requires a work permit, and Japan and South Korea have specific intern visa categories that need documentation from the host organisation. There is no universal answer, which is why you must check the requirement for your nationality and your specific destination rather than assuming your usual study visa will carry over.

Who handles the visa paperwork?

The receiving IAESTE committee coordinates most of this, but start early and keep copies of every document. Committees routinely supply the invitation letter, the placement confirmation and the host organisation details that embassies require, and they will tell you which appointment type to book. What they cannot do is file on your behalf or guarantee an outcome, so treat every request they send you as urgent. For a general overview of what the process involves, see our student visa guide. Working on the wrong visa category is the fastest way to lose a placement you have already been accepted for, so verify rather than assume at every stage.

Does insurance come from the host or from me?

It depends on the country, and the answer is usually split rather than one or the other. Some host committees arrange group insurance policies for all incoming interns, which is simpler and often cheaper. Others require you to arrange your own coverage and will specify the minimum terms, which typically include medical treatment, hospitalisation and repatriation. Find out which applies before you buy anything, because group policies frequently exclude the very activities you will be doing. Budget roughly 50 to 150 US dollars for adequate short-term coverage, and confirm that the policy covers the full duration of the placement including any arrival and departure days.

Making the most of your placement

What do interns gain beyond the salary?

An IAESTE internship is as much about the experience as the salary. Interns come away with real technical experience, an international network and a CV that stands out to graduate programmes and employers. The technical experience is concrete: you have worked inside a professional organisation with real deadlines, real colleagues and real consequences, which is a fundamentally different signal from a course project. The network is genuinely international, because a single placement can bring together students from a dozen or more countries who go on to work in the same field. The CV line is unusually strong because the host organisation is a named, verifiable institution in a foreign country.

How do I get the most value from my placement?

Take part in the social programme, keep a record of the projects you work on, and ask your supervisor for feedback and a reference at the end. None of these take long and all of them compound. The social programme is where the cross-national contacts form, the project record is what you will still have in six months when you cannot remember which project was which, and the reference is a document that a graduate admissions committee or a hiring manager will take more seriously than an unsupervised internship. Ask for the reference before you leave, while the supervisor still remembers the specifics of your contribution, and offer to draft a summary of your work that makes writing the letter easy.

Should I treat the placement like a job or like a learning exercise?

Like both, with a bias towards the job. The students who benefit most treat the placement as a learning experience rather than simply a way to spend a summer overseas, and in practice that means turning up on time, asking questions and volunteering for tasks that are not assigned to you. Hosts host interns because they want a return on the supervision they invest, so a supervisor who spends three months explaining things to a student who only appears when told ends up regretting it. Meanwhile, the learning happens automatically if you engage like a professional, because professional work is where the real technical depth comes from. Ask what problem the team is trying to solve, offer your skills where they are useful, and treat every piece of feedback as free supervision from someone who knows the field.

How to apply: next steps

What are the next steps I should take right now?

Start by finding the IAESTE office for your country on iaeste.org and asking how the local cycle works, because committees often have their own application windows and deadlines. Prepare your documents, register on the platform, and check the open internships regularly so you can signal interest in the offers that fit you best. Treat that first email to your committee as the most important action in this entire guide, because everything else depends on getting a place in their cycle. Ask three specific questions: when does the current call open, what is the internal deadline for document submission, and which destinations does my country have access to this year.

How seriously should I treat the IAESTE application?

Treat your IAESTE application with the same seriousness as a job interview, because that is effectively what it is. You are being assessed on your technical background, your documentation, your communication and your reliability, and every document is reviewed by a committee that is also choosing between candidates from dozens of other countries. For more funded international work experience ideas, browse our internships hub and our playbook for funded summer research internships abroad. Where IAESTE is not the right fit, the same discipline of preparation applies to direct applications, and the documents you build for IAESTE will work for those too.

What should I do while I wait for a response?

Use the waiting period to remove the risks that could still cost you the placement. Start a visa document checklist for your likely destinations, research the cost of living in the two or three countries you would most like, and begin building the language documentation that a host may ask for. Keep your platform profile and CV aligned so that a sudden change of preference can be actioned in a day rather than a week. Applicants who move quickly when an offer lands are almost always the ones who chose a second-choice destination; applicants who wait for their first choice to be resolved frequently end up with nothing, because the cycle closes before the matching completes.

Which countries offer the most IAESTE placements and what they involve

IAESTE operates in over 80 countries, but the availability of placements varies significantly by destination. Understanding which countries have the strongest IAESTE programmes and what kinds of work are typically offered helps you target your application strategically.

Which European countries offer the most placements?

Germany, Austria and Switzerland consistently offer the largest number of IAESTE placements each year. In Germany, placements are spread across universities, research institutes such as the Max Planck and Fraunhofer societies, and industrial companies in engineering, chemistry, physics and computer science. Austrian placements tend to concentrate in technical universities and manufacturing companies, while Swiss placements often involve research groups at ETH Zurich or EPFL and smaller specialised firms. Germany's sheer volume is the strategic point: more positions means more likely matches, and a wide spread of host types means a mechanical or process engineering student is as likely to find a fit as a computer science student.

Where else can I find IAESTE placements?

Canada is another major IAESTE destination, with placements across universities and research centres in provinces such as Ontario, Quebec, British Columbia and Alberta. Canadian placements are popular because they typically offer competitive stipends, English is the working language in most placements and the country's quality of life is high. Asian destinations including Japan, South Korea, China and India have grown significantly in recent years, offering placements in engineering, information technology and manufacturing with exposure to rapidly developing industries. European countries such as the Czech Republic, Poland, Hungary and Turkey offer placements at lower cost of living, which means your stipend stretches further, and they are particularly attractive for students who want international experience without the high expenses of Western Europe or North America. Latin American countries including Brazil, Mexico and Colombia offer placements in engineering and environmental science, though the number of positions is generally smaller.

What kind of work do IAESTE placements involve?

The type of work depends on the host organisation. University placements typically involve research assistance, literature reviews, data analysis, laboratory work or technical projects. Industrial placements may involve product testing, quality assurance, process improvement, software development or design work. In all cases, the work is meant to be educational and relevant to your field of study. IAESTE does not place students in routine or unskilled positions; the programme exists to provide genuine professional development. That last point is worth stressing when you compare an IAESTE internship with a commercial internship at a large multinational, because the smaller host organisation typical of an IAESTE placement often gives one intern a much wider range of responsibilities than a branded programme allows.

How do I choose between two very different destinations?

Weigh what you will be able to say afterwards rather than what the country looks like in photographs. A placement in a country you have never visited may be worth more than a popular destination that adds nothing new, because employers and admissions panels read international experience as evidence of adaptability, and adaptability is only demonstrated by genuine unfamiliarity. On the other hand, a language you already speak, a region you want to work in long term, or a host organisation whose output you can actually talk about at interview will outperform a more exciting destination you had nothing to do in. Practical constraints matter too: airfare, visa lead times, language requirements and whether your committee has contacts in that country all affect whether a placement is genuinely achievable for you.

Health insurance and visa requirements for IAESTE placements abroad

Health insurance and visa arrangements are two of the most important practical aspects of an IAESTE placement, and they vary significantly depending on your nationality and destination country. Getting these wrong can derail an otherwise excellent placement, so attention to detail here is essential.

What are the health insurance requirements?

Most host countries require IAESTE participants to have health insurance that covers the full duration of the placement. Some host committees arrange group insurance policies for all incoming interns, while others require you to arrange your own coverage. Check with your national committee and the host committee early in the process to understand what is required. If you need to arrange your own insurance, ensure it meets the minimum requirements specified by the host country, which typically include coverage for medical treatment, hospitalisation and repatriation. Budget roughly 50 to 150 US dollars for adequate short-term coverage, depending on the destination and duration. Confirm the start and end dates of the policy against your actual travel dates rather than the placement dates, because the gap between those two is when people get stranded with an uncovered bill.

Which visa requirements apply to my destination?

Visa requirements depend entirely on your nationality and the country where you will be placed. Some countries require a specific intern or trainee visa, while others allow entry on a student visa or a short-term work permit. European countries within the Schengen area may require a long-stay visa for placements exceeding 90 days. Canada typically requires a work permit for IAESTE placements, and the process can take several weeks, so apply well in advance. Japan and South Korea have specific intern visa categories that require documentation from the host organisation. Your national IAESTE committee will provide guidance on visa requirements for your specific destination, but the ultimate responsibility for obtaining the correct visa rests with you. Start the visa process as soon as you receive your placement confirmation. Gather all required documents, including your placement letter, proof of insurance, financial evidence and any forms required by the host country's embassy or consulate. Processing times vary from a few days to several weeks depending on the country and the time of year.

Can I work on a tourist visa during an IAESTE placement?

No, and this is a common and expensive mistake. A common mistake is assuming that a tourist visa is sufficient for an IAESTE placement. In most countries, working on a tourist visa is illegal and can result in deportation and future visa restrictions. Always obtain the correct visa category for your placement, even if the process is more complex. The IAESTE network is experienced in navigating these requirements and will support you, but you need to be proactive and responsive throughout the process. Keep copies of all visa and insurance documents with you during the placement, both physically and digitally. A future visa restriction is the real cost here, because it does not just end this placement, it can close the door on the country you wanted to work in for years.

Who supplies the paperwork embassies ask for?

The host side of the paperwork comes from the host organisation and the receiving committee, not from you. A placement or invitation letter, a statement of the work you will be doing, confirmation of the dates and sometimes a contract or salary statement are all generated by the host and the receiving committee, and committees know the format each embassy expects because they process these applications all year. What you supply is the applicant side: passport, photographs, enrolment and graduation evidence, financial evidence, insurance certificate, and in some countries police clearance or medical certificates. Ask the receiving committee for their documents by name and in writing early, because embassy appointment slots, especially in peak summer season, are often booked weeks ahead of the date you think you will need them.

Making the most of your IAESTE placement for long-term career benefit

An IAESTE placement is more than a summer job abroad. It is a career development opportunity that, if approached strategically, can significantly strengthen your professional profile. The students who benefit most from IAESTE are those who treat the placement as a learning experience rather than simply a way to spend a summer overseas.

How should I behave from the first day?

From the first day, treat your placement like a professional engagement. Arrive on time, take initiative, ask questions and show genuine interest in the work and the organisation. Supervisors remember interns who contribute actively and are willing to take on challenging tasks. A strong performance can lead to a reference letter, a return offer or a recommendation to a graduate programme, all of which are valuable for your career. The first week sets the tone more than most people realise, because a supervisor forms their judgement of how much supervision you will need very early, and that judgement then determines how much responsibility they are willing to hand you later in the summer.

What should I document during the placement?

Document your work throughout the placement. Keep a record of the projects you contributed to, the skills you developed and any outcomes or results that followed from your work. This documentation will be useful when you write your CV, apply for graduate school or interview for jobs. Be specific about what you did and what you learned rather than describing the placement in general terms. Quantifiable results, such as the number of experiments conducted, the efficiency improvement achieved or the code modules developed, make your CV entries more compelling. A weekly written log is enough to make this painless, and it is far easier to write while you still remember the details than to reconstruct them six months later from photographs.

How do I build a network during the placement?

Build relationships with colleagues and fellow interns. The IAESTE network is global, and many placements bring together students from a dozen or more countries. These connections can become professional contacts, collaborators or friends for life. Attend social events, explore the local area with fellow interns and engage with the workplace community beyond your immediate project. The professional network you build during an IAESTE placement can open doors to future opportunities across multiple countries and industries. The most useful thing you can do is be the person who helps: introduce two colleagues from different teams, ask a more senior person for a coffee, offer to share a document you wrote. Reciprocity is what turns a contact into a reference.

What should I do once the placement ends?

After the placement, follow up with your supervisor and colleagues. A brief thank-you email reinforces the relationship and keeps the door open for future opportunities. Add the experience to your LinkedIn profile and professional CV, including the host organisation, your role and the skills you developed. Mention the placement in future job or graduate school applications as evidence of international experience, technical skills and adaptability. The IAESTE brand is well recognised in academic and industrial circles, and the placement carries weight in selection processes worldwide. Send the thank-you email while you are still in the country if you can, because a message written from home two weeks later reads as an afterthought, and include something specific about a project rather than a generic thank you.

Common mistakes in IAESTE applications and how to avoid them

IAESTE applications are straightforward, but several common errors reduce candidates' chances of receiving a placement. Understanding these pitfalls before you apply helps you present a stronger candidacy, and all of them are avoidable with a few days of preparation.

What is the most damaging timing mistake?

The first mistake is applying too late. IAESTE placements are filled on a rolling basis through international exchange, and popular destinations and placement types fill early. Students who submit their applications in the spring for a summer placement often find that their preferred destinations are no longer available. Apply as early as your national committee allows, ideally six to eight months before the intended placement start date. This is not just about being organised. The exchange works by countries balancing incoming and outgoing interns against a fixed quota, so a late applicant is not competing for the last few popular slots, they are competing against a quota that has already been allocated to a different candidate from their own country.

Why is a generic application a problem?

The second mistake is a vague or generic application. When your national committee forwards your profile to IAESTE International, it competes against profiles from students in dozens of other countries. A generic statement of interest that could apply to any placement in any field does not distinguish you. Write specifically about what kind of work you want to do, why you want to do it in a particular country or type of organisation, and how the placement fits your academic and career goals. The test is simple: if you could paste the paragraph into an application to a completely different programme without changing a word, it is not doing any work for you, and the committee can tell within seconds.

What mistakes do candidates make with language and skills?

The third mistake is ignoring the language and skills requirements. Many placements require English, but some also require or prefer German, French or the local language. If you have language skills, list them prominently and be honest about your level. Similarly, highlight any specific technical skills such as programming languages, laboratory techniques or software tools that are relevant to your field. Be honest about your level because overstating a language is discovered within weeks, usually by being handed a document in that language on your first morning. Understating, on the other hand, means your profile never gets looked at for roles you could genuinely have filled, so list the skills you have and describe the level precisely rather than modestly.

Which documentation errors cause rejections?

The fourth mistake is incomplete or inaccurate documentation. Missing transcripts, unsigned forms, blurred documents or incorrect personal details can delay your application or result in rejection. Review every document carefully before submission. Ensure that your CV is up to date, your transcripts are current and all forms are completed fully and signed where required. Check the spelling of your name as it appears on your passport against every other document, because a mismatch there is a routine reason for a visa delay later on, and a name mismatch on an application form is a routine reason for a rejection now. Keep one master folder of correctly named, legible files so that a last-minute resubmission takes minutes rather than days.

What about unrealistic expectations about money?

The fifth mistake is unrealistic expectations about the stipend. IAESTE placements provide a stipend intended to cover basic living costs, not a salary. The amount varies by country and host organisation, and in some destinations it may not fully cover accommodation and food. Research the cost of living in your target destination and plan your budget accordingly. Some students supplement their stipend with personal savings, which is perfectly normal and advisable. A well-planned budget ensures you can focus on the work experience rather than financial stress. Assume you will also pay for flights, visas, insurance and some personal travel, and bring enough to cover your first few weeks before any stipend payments begin, because in some countries payment is made monthly in arrears.

Are IAESTE internships really paid and what does the stipend cover

The question of whether IAESTE internships are truly paid is one of the most common concerns for prospective applicants, and the answer requires some nuance. IAESTE internships do come with a stipend, but the amount and what it actually covers vary significantly by country and host organisation. The stipend is intended to cover basic living costs rather than provide a salary equivalent, and understanding that distinction before you apply is what stops the experience turning into a financial surprise.

Are IAESTE internships really paid?

Yes, IAESTE internships are genuinely paid, and this is not a nominal or symbolic payment. The stipend is calculated against the cost of living in the host country so that a student can support themselves there for the duration of the placement, which is why the same placement looks generous in one destination and modest in another. What the payment is not is a professional salary: nobody takes an IAESTE internship for the income, and the organisations that advertise them do not describe them as employment in the usual sense. The right comparison is not with an entry-level graduate job but with your own cost of living at home, because that is roughly what the stipend is designed to fund.

What does the stipend look like in different countries?

In Germany, the typical IAESTE stipend covers accommodation, either provided directly as housing or as a cash allowance, and a monthly cash payment for food and personal expenses. The combined value is enough to live on modestly but not to save money or fund extensive travel. In Canada, stipends tend to be more generous relative to the cost of living, and some host organisations offer additional benefits such as meal plans or transit passes. In Switzerland, the stipend is higher in absolute terms but the cost of living is also much higher, so the purchasing power is comparable to other destinations. That last example is the clearest illustration of why comparing raw amounts is misleading: the number that matters is the local stipend relative to local prices.

Do some hosts pay more than the standard amount?

Some host organisations, particularly industrial companies, pay a cash salary on top of or instead of the IAESTE-standard stipend. This is more common in some countries than others and depends on the individual host. When you receive a placement offer, ask the host organisation or your national IAESTE committee specifically about the financial terms. Do not assume that every IAESTE placement comes with the same stipend. Ask four concrete questions: what is the monthly amount, is accommodation included in cash or provided directly, when is the first payment made, and what happens if the placement is extended. You should also ask whether the payment is subject to tax locally, because in a few countries the stipend is treated as taxable income and the net figure will be lower than the headline number.

What does the stipend not cover?

What the stipend typically does not cover includes international airfare, visa fees, personal travel and entertainment beyond basic cultural activities. Budget for these expenses separately, and bring enough savings to cover your first few weeks before the stipend payments begin. In some countries, stipend payments are made monthly in arrears, which means you will need personal funds to cover initial expenses. Flights are the item people consistently underestimate, because a summer peak-season ticket from South Asia to Europe can cost several hundred dollars, and that has to be funded from savings rather than from the placement. Some committees and hosts do offer partial airfare support, but it is not standard and it is not something to plan around without written confirmation.

Is the stipend enough to live on?

The honest assessment is that IAESTE stipends provide a foundation rather than a fortune. They make an international internship financially feasible for students who might otherwise be unable to afford a placement abroad, but they do not eliminate the need for some personal financial planning. Students who approach the placement with realistic expectations and adequate savings have a much more positive experience than those who arrive underestimating the costs involved. The practical test is whether you can cover your first month from savings. If you cannot, the placement is still worth taking, but you need a larger buffer, and you should discuss the payment schedule with your host before you commit to it.

How the IAESTE placement matching process works

How is IAESTE matching different from a normal internship?

The IAESTE matching process is one of the most distinctive features of the programme. Unlike most internship programmes where you apply to a specific host and the host selects you, IAESTE uses a centralised international matching system where your national committee preferences are matched against available placements by the IAESTE International office. This is why you can be offered a placement at a company whose name you have never heard of, in a city you never considered. It is a feature rather than a bug: the system is built to fill real vacancies with qualified candidates, so the fit is on skills and availability rather than on brand recognition.

What happens during the international matching step?

The process begins when your national committee submits your candidate profile, including your academic background, skills, interests and country preferences, to the international matching team. Host committees in other countries submit their available placements, including the host organisation description, the project requirements and the skills they are seeking. The matching team then pairs candidates with placements based on the alignment of skills, interests and preferences. Candidates are effectively competing with a global pool, and the information your committee puts into the profile is the raw material for every decision that follows, which is why completeness at the nomination stage pays off for months afterwards.

Do host committees have the final say?

Yes. The matching is not purely algorithmic. Host committees review matched candidates and decide whether to accept them. This means that even if you are matched with a placement, the host committee has the final say. Flexibility in your country and field preferences significantly improves your chances. Students who are willing to go to less popular destinations or who have skills in high demand tend to receive offers faster. Because the host committee is making a judgement about working with you for three months, anything that reduces their uncertainty helps: a clear project description, an accurate CV, and a short note explaining why their placement in particular interests you.

How long does matching take?

The timeline from application to placement offer is typically three to six months. You may not know your specific host organisation until several weeks before the placement begins. Trust the process: IAESTE has been refining this matching system for decades, and the vast majority of placements result in positive experiences for both the intern and the host. Silence during this period is normal and generally means your profile is in a queue rather than in a reject pile. The one thing you can usefully do is keep your profile and availability current, because a profile with an out-of-date availability note or a new qualification added will be matched differently from one left untouched since you registered.

What if IAESTE internships are not available to me?

If IAESTE internships are not available to you because of your field of study, nationality or enrolment status, several alternative programmes offer similar international work experience. The AIESEC global internship programme places students from all disciplines in international work placements, including paid and volunteer positions in business, education and community development. Many countries also have bilateral exchange agreements that include non-STEM fields, so check with your university international office for options relevant to your discipline. If you are in a STEM field but your country is not an IAESTE member, look at similar programmes run by national organisations. The National Science Foundation funds international research experiences for US students through its IRES programme. The Royal Society of London offers research placements in the UK for international students. The DAAD RISE programme places students from specific countries in German research laboratories during the summer.

What if my preferred destination is already full?

If IAESTE placements in your preferred destination are full, consider applying for placements in other countries where demand is lower. IAESTE has branches in over 80 countries, and the availability of placements varies from year to year. A placement in a country you had not initially considered may offer a richer and more surprising experience than the popular destination you had in mind. Do not hold out for a specific city if the cost is losing you a placement entirely, because the scarcity is real and the queue does not shorten. Ask your committee which countries have unfilled offers this cycle, since committees frequently know where the gaps are weeks before candidates do.

Budgeting for an IAESTE placement: realistic costs and savings tips

What are the main costs of an IAESTE placement?

Understanding the real costs of an IAESTE placement helps you plan financially and avoid surprises. The main expenses to budget for are international airfare, visa fees, health insurance, accommodation beyond what the stipend covers, food, local transport, personal expenses and initial setup costs. Only the first three are fixed and knowable in advance; the rest depend on how you live, and students who cook and share accommodation consistently come in well under the average. Build the budget around a monthly figure rather than a total, because the placement is a repeating monthly pattern and the month-three total is usually the month-three cost multiplied by three.

How much should I budget for flights?

International airfare is typically your largest single expense. Booking at least three months in advance and being flexible with dates can reduce costs significantly. Some destinations are cheaper to reach than others: flights from South Asia to Germany or Austria, for example, are often more affordable than flights to North America or East Asia. Use flight comparison tools and set price alerts to find the best fares. Be realistic about when you will know your dates, though, because many hosts confirm exact start dates only weeks before arrival, which limits how much flexibility you actually have. A round trip booked on confirmed dates is often cheaper than a speculative open ticket, so compare the two rather than assuming flexibility is free.

What do visas and health insurance cost?

Visa fees vary by country and nationality. Research the specific requirements for your destination well in advance, as some visas require in-person appointments at consulates that may be located in a different city from your university. Budget 50 to 200 US dollars for visa fees depending on the destination, and add travel costs for any consulate visits required. Health insurance is mandatory in most host countries, and budget 50 to 150 US dollars for adequate short-term coverage. Some IAESTE host committees arrange group insurance, while others require you to arrange your own, and the difference between the two can be significant enough to ask about before you accept a particular placement. Accommodation costs vary widely: in Germany, student dormitories cost 300 to 500 euros per month, while private accommodation can cost more. Factor in the deposit, which is typically one or two months of rent, because that deposit is a large one-off cash requirement that catches people out on arrival.

What are daily living costs in a typical destination?

Daily expenses depend on the destination. University canteen meals in Germany cost 3 to 5 euros, while eating out costs 10 to 20 euros. Public transport in most German cities costs 50 to 80 euros per month with a student discount. Personal expenses, weekend travel and entertainment vary by individual but typically range from 100 to 300 euros per month. Total monthly costs for a placement in Germany typically range from 600 to 1,000 euros, which the stipend partially covers. Note how quickly canteens and student transport passes change that total, which is why the single highest-return saving available to most interns in Germany is a semester transport ticket rather than a cheaper lunch.

What savings tips do IAESTE alumni recommend?

Practical savings tips include cooking at home rather than eating out, using student discounts for transport and cultural activities, sharing accommodation with fellow interns, and setting a weekly spending limit. Many IAESTE alumni report that careful budgeting allowed them to save modest amounts during their placement, particularly in lower-cost destinations. The key is to plan before you go and to track your spending during the placement. Track in whatever way you actually use, because a method you do not maintain is worth nothing, and review the numbers every week rather than every month, because by the time a monthly total surprises you the money is already gone.

How to prepare for your IAESTE placement arrival in a new country

What should I research before I leave?

The first few days of an IAESTE placement in a new country can feel overwhelming, but thorough preparation makes the transition smoother. Before you leave, research the practical details of your destination: local transport options, banking requirements, mobile phone providers and the location of your accommodation relative to your workplace. Many IAESTE national committees provide welcome packs or online guides with this information, so check your committee resources carefully. Ask your receiving committee specifically what they provide, because some committees handle almost everything and others leave you to arrange everything, and that difference determines how much work your first week actually takes.

How do I arrange my arrival and airport transfer?

Arrange your airport transfer in advance. Some IAESTE host committees offer airport pickups, while others provide detailed directions and public transport information. If you are arriving late at night or in an unfamiliar city, consider booking a taxi or shuttle service rather than navigating public transport with luggage for the first time. The small additional cost is worth the peace of mind. Tell your host supervisor your flight number and arrival time, and agree a fallback plan in case of delays, because the one scenario that reliably ruins a first impression is standing in an unfamiliar arrivals hall at two in the morning with no way to reach the accommodation.

Which documents should I carry with me?

Bring essential documents in both physical and digital form: your passport, visa, placement confirmation letter, insurance documents, university enrolment proof and emergency contact information. Keep a printed copy of your accommodation address and the contact details of your host organisation supervisor. Store digital copies in a secure cloud service so they are accessible from anywhere. The printed set matters more than people expect, because not every embissary, police station or hospital will accept a phone screen, and you do not want to discover a missing document while trying to show it.

What should I do on my first day at the host organisation?

On your first day at the host organisation, arrive early, dress professionally and bring your placement confirmation and identification. Introduce yourself to your supervisor and colleagues, and ask about the daily schedule, workplace norms and any immediate tasks you can begin working on. First impressions matter, and demonstrating enthusiasm and initiative from day one sets a positive tone for the entire placement. Ask the small practical questions that will save you later: where is the nearest affordable lunch, which door needs a badge, what time do people actually stop working, and who is the person to ask when something breaks.

How do I settle into a routine during the first week?

In the first week, establish your daily routine: your commute, your meal times, your work schedule and your evening activities. Set up a local bank account if your placement lasts more than a few weeks, and register with any local authorities that require foreign residents to sign in. Buy a local SIM card or mobile plan so you can communicate easily with colleagues, fellow interns and your contacts at home. The sooner you settle into a routine, the sooner you can focus fully on the work and the cultural experience. A useful trick is to fix one thing each day for the first week: the commute on day one, the bank on day two, and so on, so that by the end of the week nothing administrative is still hanging over you.

How much growth do alumni report from the placement?

IAESTE alumni consistently report that their placement delivered far more than a line on a CV. The experience of working as a paid professional in a laboratory, company or research institute on the other side of the world builds technical skills, professional confidence and a global network in ways that classroom learning cannot match. Many alumni describe the technical growth as dramatic. Working alongside experienced professionals on real projects, with real deadlines and real consequences, forces you to apply your academic knowledge in ways you have never done before. The feedback you receive, from supervisors and colleagues, sharpens your understanding of your field and teaches you professional standards that employers value. Alumni often report returning to their universities with significantly stronger practical skills and a clearer sense of the career direction they want to pursue.

What personal benefits come from living abroad during a placement?

The cultural and personal growth is equally valuable. Living and working in a foreign country independently, navigating a new language, forming friendships across cultures and adapting to a different workplace environment builds resilience, adaptability and confidence. These qualities are prized by graduate programmes and employers, and they are the kind of development that stays with you for life. It is also the part that is hardest to fake in an interview, because interviewers can tell the difference between someone who has genuinely been confused by a bureaucratic system and pushed through it, and someone who has only read about doing so. The stories you tell about the small difficulties usually carry more weight than the ones about the achievements.

How does an IAESTE placement help my applications?

For students considering graduate school, an IAESTE placement provides concrete material for statements and interviews. The ability to describe a specific technical project completed abroad, the challenges faced and the skills mastered, is far more compelling than generic claims about passion for your field. Many alumni received strong reference letters from their IAESTE supervisors, which played a significant role in their graduate admissions success. Professionally, the IAESTE network is a lasting asset. The connections you build with colleagues, fellow interns and the national committee community can lead to job offers, research collaborations and mentorship opportunities for years after the placement. The IAESTE brand is recognised in academic and industrial circles worldwide, and alumni often find that the placement opens doors that would otherwise remain closed.

Frequently asked questions

What is IAESTE?+

IAESTE, the International Association for the Exchange of Students for Technical Experience, arranges paid technical traineeships for STEM students in more than 80 countries through national committees.

Are IAESTE internships paid?+

Yes. Host employers provide a salary or stipend calculated to cover local living costs, and the amount varies by country and position.

Who can apply for IAESTE internships?+

Current undergraduate, master's or PhD students in STEM fields, and some committees also accept recent graduates, usually within about a year of finishing.

How do I apply for an IAESTE internship?+

You register on the IAESTE platform and apply through your national committee, which matches your profile to offers available to your country and handles nominations.

How long do IAESTE internships last?+

Most placements last 8 to 12 weeks over the summer, though longer internships are available in some countries.
