Fully Funded Masters Scholarships Without IELTS

🗓️ Apr 16, 2026⏱️ 10 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

The IELTS exam is a barrier for many talented students: it costs money, requires travel to a test centre, and adds pressure to an already demanding application. The good news is that scholarships without IELTS genuinely exist, and many fully funded masters programmes will accept alternative evidence of your English or waive the requirement entirely. This guide explains how these waivers work and how to find and win scholarships without IELTS.

It is important to be honest from the start: no IELTS does not mean no English requirement. Universities still need to know you can cope with study in English. What changes is how you prove it. Once you understand the alternatives, a whole range of funded options opens up.

Key takeaways

  • No IELTS is common, not rare. Many universities accept alternative proof of English or waive the test in specific situations.
  • Waivers usually rest on one of a few grounds: prior study in English, a waiver letter, an interview, or an accepted alternative test.
  • Prepare evidence early. A medium-of-instruction letter from your previous university is one of the most powerful documents you can hold.
  • Read each programme carefully. Waiver policies vary by university and even by department.
  • Never assume. Always confirm the exact requirement in writing before you rely on a waiver.

How English requirements really work

Universities require proof of English because degrees are demanding and they want you to succeed. IELTS is simply the most common way to provide that proof, not the only way. Many institutions accept a range of evidence, and some waive formal testing when your background already demonstrates strong English.

This distinction matters. When you search for scholarships without IELTS, you are really searching for programmes that accept an alternative form of proof. Reframing the question this way makes your search far more productive.

Common ways to get an IELTS waiver

There are several established routes to studying with funding but no IELTS. Most successful applicants use one of the following.

1. Previous study in English (medium of instruction)

This is the most common and reliable route. If your previous degree was taught entirely in English, many universities will accept a medium-of-instruction letter from that institution instead of IELTS. The letter, issued on official letterhead, confirms that English was the language of teaching and assessment.

If your undergraduate degree was in English, request this letter early. It is free, it is powerful, and it unlocks a large number of programmes.

2. Studying in an English-speaking country

Some universities waive the English test if you completed your prior education in a country where English is an official language, or where you spent a qualifying period studying in English. Policies differ, so check the specific list each university publishes.

3. A formal waiver letter or interview

Certain programmes assess English through a short interview or by issuing a conditional waiver at the admissions office's discretion. In these cases the admissions team judges your English directly, often during a video call that also covers your motivation.

4. Accepted alternative tests

Even where a test is required, IELTS is often not the only one accepted. Universities may accept other recognised English tests, some of which are cheaper or can be taken online at home. If you must take a test, check which alternatives your target programmes accept before booking anything.

Which programmes are most likely to waive IELTS

Some categories of scholarship and programme are more flexible than others.

Government scholarships in non-English-speaking countries

Many national scholarship schemes in countries where English is not the first language are pragmatic about English proof, especially for programmes taught in the local language or for those that include a language preparation year. These schemes frequently accept alternatives to IELTS.

Programmes with a language preparatory year

Where a scholarship includes a preparatory year in the local language, formal English testing may not be central to admission at all, since you will study in the host language.

Universities with flexible admissions policies

Individual universities set their own English policies, and many publish clear waiver conditions. Reading these policies carefully is the single most effective way to build a list of realistic scholarships without IELTS. How to build your no-IELTS application

Winning a funded place without IELTS is about presenting strong alternative evidence and a compelling overall application.

Step 1: Confirm the requirement in writing

Never rely on a forum post or a rumour. Read the official admissions page, and if the waiver conditions are unclear, email the admissions office and keep their reply. A written confirmation protects you from nasty surprises later.

Step 2: Gather your evidence early

Request your medium-of-instruction letter, gather transcripts that show English-taught courses, and collect any other proof of your English ability. Having these ready means you can apply the moment a suitable programme opens.

Step 3: Strengthen the rest of your application

When you skip IELTS, the rest of your application carries more weight. A clear, well-written statement of purpose demonstrates your English directly and shows your motivation. Our statement of purpose builder can help you produce a polished, persuasive statement that doubles as evidence of your written English.

Step 4: Prepare for a possible interview

If a waiver depends on an interview, treat it seriously. Practise speaking about your background, your goals and your chosen field in English. Speaking confidently and clearly is exactly what the admissions team wants to see. Our AI advisor can help you rehearse common interview questions.

An IELTS waiver is a privilege, not a loophole. Universities grant it because they believe you can thrive in English. Your job is to prove that belief is justified.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming a waiver applies. Policies vary by department and change over time. Always confirm.
  • Leaving evidence to the last minute. Official letters can take weeks to issue.
  • Neglecting your written English. If you skip a test, your essays must be flawless.
  • Ignoring alternative tests. Sometimes a cheaper, at-home test is the fastest path.
  • Applying to only one programme. Waiver-friendly programmes are plentiful, so apply widely.

How to request a medium-of-instruction letter

Because the medium-of-instruction letter is so valuable, it is worth knowing exactly how to obtain one. Contact the registrar, academic office or examinations office at the university where you studied. Explain that you need an official letter, on institutional letterhead and signed by an authorised officer, confirming that the language of instruction and assessment for your programme was English.

Ask for the letter to state the exact name of your degree, the dates you studied, and a clear statement that teaching and examinations were conducted in English. Request more than one original or certified copy if you plan to apply to several universities. Give the office plenty of time, because official documents can take days or weeks to produce, and a delay here can cost you a deadline elsewhere.

Regions and programmes known for flexibility

While policies are set institution by institution, some patterns are worth knowing when you build your shortlist.

Non-English-speaking European countries

Many universities in continental Europe run English-taught masters programmes and are pragmatic about proof of English, particularly where applicants studied their bachelor degree in English. Some accept a medium-of-instruction letter or an admissions interview in place of a formal test.

Programmes taught in a local language

Where a masters is delivered wholly in the host country's language, an English test may be irrelevant to admission. Instead, proof of proficiency in the language of instruction, or completion of a preparatory language year, becomes the relevant requirement.

Scholarships that assess English directly

Some funded programmes gauge your English through the application itself and an interview, judging your written statement and your spoken answers rather than a test score. For these, the quality of your application materials is your proof, so they must be excellent.

What if a programme still asks for some English proof?

Sometimes a waiver is partial: a university might drop the test requirement but still expect evidence of English somewhere in your file. In these cases, lean on the documents you already have. A medium-of-instruction letter, transcripts listing English-taught courses, prior work or study in English, and a flawless written statement together build a convincing picture. If a test is genuinely unavoidable, remember that IELTS is rarely the only accepted option, and a cheaper or at-home alternative may satisfy the requirement.

A practical application timeline

Approaching a no-IELTS application methodically keeps you ahead of deadlines:

  1. Months out: research programmes and note their exact English policies.
  2. Early on: request your medium-of-instruction letter and gather transcripts.
  3. Mid-process: draft and refine your statement of purpose until it is polished.
  4. Before submitting: confirm each programme's waiver conditions in writing.
  5. After applying: prepare for any interview that forms part of the assessment.

A structured tool makes it easier to compare waiver-friendly options side by side and keep track of which programmes fit your situation.

A realistic expectation

Studying abroad without IELTS is entirely possible, and many students do it every year. But approach it strategically. Target programmes whose published policies match your situation, prepare your alternative evidence early, and make the rest of your application excellent. Do that, and the lack of an IELTS score becomes a non-issue rather than an obstacle.

Understanding what English level universities expect

Even when a formal test is waived, universities still have a level of English in mind. Degrees involve reading dense material, writing essays or a dissertation, following fast-paced lectures and taking part in discussion. A waiver is the university's way of saying it believes your background already demonstrates that level, not that English does not matter.

This is why the alternatives all point back to genuine ability: a degree taught in English, a persuasive written statement, or a fluent interview. If you are honest with yourself that your English is strong enough for demanding academic work, a waiver route is a sound strategy. If you have doubts, investing time in your English before you apply will pay off both in admission and in your studies.

Free and low-cost ways to strengthen your English

Whether or not you take a test, improving your English strengthens every part of your application and prepares you for study abroad. You can build your skills without spending much:

  • Read widely in your field in English, from articles to textbooks, to absorb academic vocabulary and style.
  • Write regularly and ask a fluent friend or mentor to review your work, since your statement must be flawless.
  • Listen actively to lectures, talks and podcasts in your subject to train your ear for academic speech.
  • Practise speaking about your goals and your field aloud, so an interview feels natural rather than intimidating.

These habits cost little and compound over time, and they matter far more than a single number on a test report.

Putting it all together

The path to a funded place without IELTS is not a shortcut around English but a different way of proving it. Identify programmes whose published policies fit your background, secure your alternative evidence early, write a statement strong enough to speak for your English, and prepare for any interview. Do this across several programmes rather than pinning your hopes on one, and you give yourself a genuine, realistic route to fully funded study abroad. Knowing which specific scholarships and programmes in each country are known to accept alternatives to IELTS helps you target your applications strategically.

Turkey: The Turkiye Burslari scholarship does not require IELTS for applicants who can demonstrate English proficiency through other means, such as a medium-of-instruction letter or a Duolingo English Test score.

China: The Chinese Government Scholarship accepts a range of English proficiency evidence. Some Chinese universities waive English test requirements entirely if your previous degree was taught in English.

Hungary: Stipendium Hungaricum accepts applicants who can demonstrate English proficiency through their previous degree's medium of instruction or through an internal language assessment conducted by the Hungarian university.

Japan: The MEXT scholarship accepts applicants based on English proficiency demonstrated through their degree or through a university-level English test. Many Japanese universities accept alternative English evidence including MOI letters and Duolingo scores.

When targeting these countries, always verify the current English proficiency requirements on the official university or scholarship website, as policies can change.

How to request a medium-of-instruction letter from your Pakistani university

A medium-of-instruction letter, commonly called an MOI letter, is one of the most widely accepted alternatives to IELTS for Pakistani students. Knowing how to obtain one correctly ensures it meets the requirements of the receiving institution.

An MOI letter is an official document from your university confirming that the language of instruction for your degree programme was English. It is typically issued by the registrar's office or the controller of examinations. The letter should be printed on official university letterhead, signed and stamped by an authorised official, and dated.

The letter should include: your full name as it appears on your passport, your student registration or roll number, the name of the degree programme you completed, the dates of your enrollment, and a clear statement that the medium of instruction and examination for your programme was English.

To request the letter, visit your university's registrar or examinations office with a written request that specifies the information the letter must contain. Bring a copy of your degree certificate and transcripts as supporting documents. If possible, provide the receiving university's specific requirements in writing.

A common issue is that some Pakistani universities issue MOI letters in Urdu or use non-standard formats. If this happens, request an English-language version specifically. The letter must be in English to be accepted by foreign universities.

Processing times vary by university. Some issue the letter on the same day; others may take one to two weeks. Request the letter at least one month before you need it.

Keep the following tips in mind. First, request multiple copies. Second, ensure the letter is recently dated, as some institutions accept MOI letters only if they are issued within six months or one year of the application date. Third, ask whether a modified version can be issued if your university has a standard format that does not match the scholarship's requirements.

Duolingo English Test and other IELTS alternatives accepted by funded programmes

The landscape of English proficiency testing has expanded significantly, and several alternatives to IELTS are now accepted by fully funded masters programmes.

Duolingo English Test: The Duolingo English Test has gained wide acceptance among universities worldwide. It is taken online at home, costs a fraction of IELTS, and provides results within forty-eight hours. As of 2026, many universities in Turkey, China, Hungary, Germany, and Scandinavia accept Duolingo scores.

TOEFL iBT Home Edition: The TOEFL Home Edition is the same test as the standard TOEFL iBT but taken from home under proctored conditions. It is accepted by most institutions that accept the standard TOEFL.

Cambridge English Qualifications: Some universities accept Cambridge C1 Advanced or C2 Proficiency. These qualifications do not expire, which is an advantage over IELTS and TOEFL scores.

PTE Academic: The Pearson Test of English Academic is accepted by a growing number of universities. It provides results quickly, typically within two to five days.

University-internal English tests: Some universities, particularly in Turkey, China, and Japan, offer their own English proficiency assessments as part of the application process.

When choosing an alternative test, consider three factors: acceptance by your target programmes, availability in Pakistan, and your comfort with the test format. Research each option and, if possible, take a free practice version before committing.

Remember that the goal is not merely to avoid IELTS but to provide convincing evidence of your English proficiency. Whatever test you choose, prepare for it seriously.

A practical tip is to have your MOI letter notarised or attested by your university's notary office or the relevant authority. Notarisation adds a layer of authenticity that can prevent questions during the review process.

How to prove English proficiency through university interviews and assessments

Some funded programmes assess your English ability through an interview or internal assessment rather than requiring a standardised test score. Understanding how these assessments work helps you prepare effectively.

Many universities in Turkey, China, Hungary, and Japan conduct interviews as part of their scholarship selection process, and these interviews serve a dual purpose: evaluating your academic fit and assessing your English communication ability.

To prepare for an English proficiency interview, focus on three areas. First, practice speaking about your academic background and research interests in English for extended periods. Second, practise listening and responding to questions in real time. Having a friend ask you unpredictable questions in English and answering without preparation is the best way to build spontaneous speaking ability. Third, familiarise yourself with common interview questions for your field of study.

Some universities also use written English assessments as part of their admissions process. These may take the form of a short essay, a reading comprehension exercise, or a translation task. Practice writing timed essays on academic topics to prepare.

A key advantage of interview-based English assessment is that it allows you to demonstrate communicative ability, which is what universities actually need to know. Interview assessments capture real communicative ability more authentically than multiple-choice tests.

If your target programme offers an interview-based English assessment, treat it as an opportunity rather than an obstacle. Prepare thoroughly, speak clearly, and demonstrate the genuine English ability that will serve you throughout your studies.

Building a strong no-IELTS application strategy for Pakistani students

Successfully applying to fully funded masters programmes without IELTS requires more than simply finding programmes that waive the test. A strategic approach maximises your chances across multiple applications.

Start by creating a two-column list. In one column, list the scholarships and programmes you are targeting that require IELTS. In the other, list those that accept alternatives. This gives you a clear picture of your options and helps you prioritise.

Next, strengthen the parts of your application that do not depend on English test scores. Without IELTS to signal your English ability, other elements carry more weight: your academic transcript, your motivation letter, your CV, your recommendation letters, and any research experience.

Your motivation letter becomes particularly important when you are not providing an IELTS score. Write it in clear, well-structured English that demonstrates your language ability implicitly. Have a native or near-native English speaker review it for grammar, vocabulary, and coherence.

Recommendation letters from professors who taught you in English-medium classes serve as indirect evidence of your English ability. Ask your recommenders to mention your English communication skills and your performance in English-medium instruction.

If you are applying to programmes that accept Duolingo or other alternative tests, prepare for them seriously. A poor score on an alternative test is worse than no score at all.

Finally, be honest about your English level. If your English is genuinely strong enough for academic study, a no-IELTS application strategy works well. If you are using the no-IELTS route to avoid a weakness rather than to save time and money, reconsider. The no-IELTS route is a convenience, not a loophole.

Country-specific approaches for Pakistani students seeking no-IELTS scholarships

Each destination country has its own approach to English proficiency requirements, and understanding these differences helps Pakistani students navigate the no-IELTS landscape more effectively.

Turkey: Turkish universities and the Turkiye Burslari programme are among the most flexible regarding English evidence. Many accept a medium-of-instruction letter from your previous university, the Duolingo English Test, or their own internal assessment. This flexibility makes Turkey one of the most accessible destinations for Pakistani students who want to avoid IELTS. Check the specific requirements of each university, as policies vary.

China: Chinese universities vary widely in their English requirements. Some top-tier universities, particularly those with established English-taught programmes, accept MOI letters or Duolingo scores. Others require IELTS or TOEFL regardless of your previous degree's language of instruction. The key is to check each university individually, as there is no national policy.

Malaysia: Malaysian public universities, including Universiti Malaya and Universiti Putra Malaysia, commonly accept MOI letters from English-medium institutions. Malaysia's own bilingual education system makes admissions offices familiar with the concept of medium-of-instruction documentation. This makes Malaysia a reliable no-IELTS destination.

Germany: German universities take a decentralised approach. Each university sets its own English requirements, and many accept MOI letters, particularly for programmes that do not list a specific test requirement. DAAD, the main funding body, does not mandate IELTS specifically but requires evidence of English ability, which can take various forms.

Japan: Many Japanese universities with English-taught programmes accept MOI letters or Duolingo scores, particularly for the MEXT scholarship. Some universities also conduct their own English assessments as part of the admissions process. Japan's approach is generally flexible, but the specific requirements vary by university.

Scandinavia: Universities in Sweden, Norway, and Finland have increasingly accepted alternative English evidence, including Duolingo scores and MOI letters. However, living costs in Scandinavia are significantly higher than in other destinations, so factor this into your budgeting.

The overarching principle is that no-IELTS does not mean no English requirement. Every university needs to verify that you can study in English. The difference is how they verify it, and by choosing destinations that accept your strongest form of English evidence, you put yourself in the best possible position.

Free and low-cost ways to strengthen your English during the application process

While preparing your no-IELTS applications, strengthening your English ability serves a dual purpose: it improves your application documents and prepares you for English-medium study. Several free and low-cost resources can help you improve during the application period.

Academic reading: Reading academic articles in your field is one of the most effective ways to build both vocabulary and comprehension. Access free articles through Google Scholar, ResearchGate, or university repositories. Read one article per day and summarise its main argument in your own words. This practice builds the reading comprehension skills you will need for your master's programme.

Academic writing practice: Write short academic paragraphs or essays on topics related to your field. Use the free writing feedback tools available online, such as Grammarly's free version, which catches basic grammar and spelling errors. While not a substitute for human feedback, these tools help you identify patterns in your errors.

Listening and speaking: Watch academic lectures on YouTube or Coursera in your field. Listen actively, take notes, and then summarise what you heard in a short written or spoken response. This practice builds both listening comprehension and the ability to process academic content in English.

Vocabulary building: Use free vocabulary apps like Anki or Quizlet to build flashcard decks of academic vocabulary in your field. Study ten to fifteen new words per day, using spaced repetition to ensure they move from short-term to long-term memory. Focus on words that appear frequently in academic writing: analyse, demonstrate, significant, consequently, and similar terms.

Conversation practice: If you can find a language exchange partner, either in person or through free apps like Tandem, regular conversation practice builds fluency and confidence. Even thirty minutes of English conversation three times per week produces noticeable improvement over several weeks.

Free online courses: Platforms like Coursera, edX, and FutureLearn offer free courses on academic writing, English grammar, and study skills. Taking one of these courses during your application period demonstrates initiative and improves your English simultaneously.

The key is consistency over intensity. Thirty minutes of focused English practice every day produces better results than a four-hour study session once a week. Build English practice into your daily routine, whether it is reading before bed, listening to a podcast during your commute, or writing a short paragraph after lunch.

With your documents prepared and your target programmes identified, this final checklist ensures that your no-IELTS application is complete, polished, and submitted on time.

English proficiency documentation: Confirm that you have the correct English evidence for each programme. If submitting an MOI letter, verify that it is on official letterhead, signed and stamped, in English, and dated within the required timeframe. If submitting a Duolingo or other test score, confirm that the score meets the minimum requirement and that it has been sent to the correct institution.

Academic documents: Verify that your transcripts and degree certificates are in English or accompanied by certified translations. Check that the names on your documents match your passport exactly. If any discrepancy exists, get it corrected before submitting.

Motivation letter: Review your motivation letter for each programme to ensure it is specific to that programme, addresses the scholarship's values, and is free of grammatical errors. Have at least one other person read it before submission.

Recommendation letters: Confirm with your recommenders that all letters have been submitted by the deadline. Send a polite reminder one week before the deadline if you have not received confirmation.

CV or resume: Update your CV to reflect your most recent achievements, experiences, and skills. Tailor the emphasis for each programme: highlight research experience for research-focused programmes, and professional experience for practice-oriented ones.

Application form: Complete every section of the application form. Double-check spelling, dates, and contact information. Incomplete forms are a common cause of delays or rejections.

Submission timing: Aim to submit your application at least 48 hours before the deadline. This buffer protects against technical issues with the application portal, internet outages, or last-minute document requests.

Confirmation: After submitting, verify that you receive a confirmation email or portal acknowledgment. If you do not receive confirmation within 24 hours, contact the programme's administrative office to confirm receipt.

By following this checklist systematically for each application, you ensure that no detail is overlooked and that your no-IELTS application presents you in the strongest possible light.

Frequently asked questions

Can I really get a fully funded scholarship without IELTS?+

Yes. Many fully funded programmes accept alternatives to IELTS, such as a medium-of-instruction letter confirming your previous degree was taught in English, an admissions interview, or another accepted English test. No IELTS does not mean no English requirement, only a different way of proving your English.

What is a medium-of-instruction letter?+

It is an official letter from your previous university, on letterhead, confirming that English was the language of teaching and assessment for your degree. It is one of the most powerful ways to waive IELTS, it is usually free to request, and you should get it early if your studies were in English.

Which scholarships are most likely to waive IELTS?+

Government scholarships in non-English-speaking countries, programmes that include a language preparatory year, and universities with published flexible admissions policies are the most likely to accept alternatives. Programmes taught in a local language often place little weight on IELTS at all.

Should I still improve my English if I skip IELTS?+

Absolutely. When you skip the test, the rest of your application, especially your written statement and any interview, carries more weight as proof of your English. Strong, clear English throughout your application is essential to convince admissions teams you can succeed.

How do I confirm that a programme will waive IELTS for me?+

Read the official admissions page and, if anything is unclear, email the admissions office directly and keep their written reply. Never rely on rumours or forum posts, because waiver policies differ by university and department and can change between cycles.
