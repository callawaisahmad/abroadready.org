US F-1 Student Visa from Pakistan 2026: Complete Guide

🗓️ Aug 31, 2026⏱️ 28 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

Securing a US F-1 student visa from Pakistan is one of the most important steps in your study-abroad journey, and it is also the step that causes the most anxiety. Every year thousands of Pakistani students receive admission offers from American universities, but the visa process — with its multiple fees, strict documentation requirements, and face-to-face embassy interview — can feel opaque and intimidating. This guide is written specifically for Pakistani students in 2026. It walks you through every stage, from receiving your I-20 to standing in front of a consular officer in Islamabad, Karachi or Lahore, with concrete figures, realistic timelines and practical advice that reflects how the process actually works on the ground in Pakistan.

Whether you are an undergraduate heading to a community college in California, a master's student joining a research programme at a state university in Texas, or a PhD applicant heading to an Ivy League campus in the northeast, the F-1 visa process is essentially the same. What changes is the strength of your financial documentation, the depth of your preparation for the interview, and the way you present your ties to Pakistan. This guide covers all of it in detail.

Key takeaways

  • The F-1 visa is a nonimmigrant student visa for full-time study at a SEVP-certified US school. You apply after receiving your Form I-20.
  • In 2026 the SEVIS I-901 fee is $350 for new F-1 students (confirm on fmjfee.com) and the MRV visa application fee is $185 (confirm on travel.state.gov).
  • You must complete the DS-160 online application, pay both fees, book an appointment, and attend an in-person interview at the US Embassy in Islamabad or a US consulate in Karachi or Lahore.
  • Financial evidence must match or exceed the total cost of attendance listed on your I-20. Pakistani students typically show six months of bank statements, sponsor letters, property documents, and employment verification.
  • The consular officer evaluates your nonimmigrant intent under Section 214(b) of the Immigration and Nationality Act — you must demonstrate strong ties to Pakistan and a clear plan to return after studies.
  • Processing after a successful interview is usually 3–5 business days, but administrative processing can take weeks. Apply early — ideally three to four months before your programme starts.
  • Beware of visa agents and fraudulent websites. The official appointment system is through ustraveldocs.com/pk. No agent can guarantee a visa.

Understanding the F-1 Visa: What It Is and Who It Is For

The F-1 nonimmigrant visa is the standard visa issued to international students who want to pursue full-time academic studies in the United States. It is governed by Section 101(a)(15)(F) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, and it covers study at colleges, universities, conservatories, seminaries, language training programmes, and other academic institutions that are certified by the Student and Exchange Visitor Program (SEVP).

If you have been accepted by a US institution and that institution has issued you a Form I-20, you are eligible to apply for an F-1 visa. The F-1 is specifically for academic study — if you are coming to the US for vocational or non-academic training, a different visa category (M-1) applies. For the vast majority of Pakistani students heading to American universities, the F-1 is the correct and only option.

If you won a Fulbright or another exchange scholarship administered by the US Embassy in Pakistan, your programme sponsor will guide you toward the J-1. If you applied to and were accepted by a US university on your own, the F-1 is your route.

The Six Stages of Getting Your F-1 Visa

The F-1 visa process follows a clear sequence. Each stage depends on completing the previous one, so a mistake or delay early on cascades forward. Here is the overview, followed by a detailed breakdown of each stage:

  1. Get accepted by a SEVP-certified US school and receive your Form I-20
  2. Pay the SEVIS I-901 fee ($350 for new F-1 students in 2026)
  3. Complete the DS-160 online nonimmigrant visa application
  4. Pay the MRV visa application fee ($185 in 2026)
  5. Book and attend your visa interview appointment
  6. Attend the interview and receive a decision

Let us walk through each stage in the depth that Pakistani students need.

Stage 1: University Admission and the Form I-20

Nothing in the visa process can begin until you have been accepted by a US school that is certified by the Student and Exchange Visitor Program (SEVP). SEVP certification means the school is authorised to enroll international students and issue the documents required for F-1 visa applications. You can verify a school's SEVP certification on the Study in the States school search tool.

What is the Form I-20?

The Form I-20, officially titled "Certificate of Eligibility for Nonimmigrant Student Status," is the document that makes your F-1 visa application possible. It is issued by your school's Designated School Official (DSO) after you have been accepted and have provided evidence of financial support. The I-20 contains critical information:

  • SEVIS ID (starts with N followed by 10 digits) — this is your unique student identifier in the US government's database
  • Program information — your school name, programme of study, degree level, expected start date, and expected programme completion date
  • Estimated costs — the school's estimate of your tuition, fees, and living expenses for one academic year
  • Funding information — how you plan to cover those costs (personal funds, family sponsorship, scholarships, assistantships, or a combination)
  • Sponsor details — name and relationship of whoever is financially responsible

How the I-20 reaches you

After you accept an admission offer and demonstrate that you have sufficient funds, the school's international student office processes your I-20. In most cases, the school will ship the physical, original I-20 to your Pakistani address via international courier (FedEx, DHL or similar). Some schools are now offering electronic I-20s (eI-20s), but you should confirm with your school whether a paper original is required for the visa interview — in most cases at US embassies and consulates, the original signed I-20 is still expected.

When you receive the I-20, check every detail immediately: your name must match your passport exactly, the programme dates must be correct, and the financial figures must be accurate. Any discrepancy will cause problems at your interview. If you spot an error, contact your DSO immediately for a corrected I-20.

You must sign the I-20 in the designated space before your interview. The DSO will have already signed it. Do not make any other marks or changes on the document.

The I-20 and cost of attendance

The cost of attendance (COA) figure on your I-20 is central to your entire visa application, because the consular officer will compare your financial documents against this number. The COA typically includes:

  • Tuition and fees
  • Room and board (or on-campus housing estimate)
  • Books and supplies
  • Personal expenses
  • Health insurance (if mandatory)
  • Dependents' expenses (if applicable)

For a typical US public university, the annual COA for an international student might range from $25,000 to $40,000. For private universities, this can be $50,000 to $75,000+. The exact figure on your I-20 is what matters for your visa — not what you read online or what another student paid.

Stage 2: Pay the SEVIS I-901 Fee

Before you can schedule a visa interview, you must pay the SEVIS I-901 fee. This fee funds the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System (SEVIS), the US government database that tracks your status as an international student from the moment you enter the United States.

How much is the SEVIS fee in 2026?

As of 2026, the SEVIS I-901 fee for new F-1 students is $350. This fee is set by the Department of Homeland Security and is subject to change — always confirm the current amount at fmjfee.com before paying. Note: F-2 dependents do not pay a SEVIS fee.

How to pay the SEVIS fee from Pakistan

You pay the SEVIS fee online at fmjfee.com. The process is straightforward:

  1. Visit fmjfee.com and select "Pay I-901 Fee"
  2. Choose "F-1 Student" as your visa classification
  3. Enter your SEVIS ID (from your I-20, starting with N), your name, date of birth, and passport number
  4. Pay using a credit card, debit card, or select other payment methods available to Pakistani applicants. Visa and Mastercard are generally accepted
  5. Save or print the payment confirmation immediately — you will need this receipt for your visa interview

The payment confirmation shows the amount paid, the SEVIS ID, and the payment date. Keep both a digital copy and a printed copy. You will present the printed receipt at your interview, and you will need the SEVIS ID for your DS-160 form.

When should you pay?

Pay the SEVIS fee after you have your I-20 in hand but before you complete your DS-160 and book your interview. The fee must be processed and visible in the system before your appointment — give it at least three business days after payment before scheduling your interview. Paying too early (before you have confirmed your I-20 details) or too late (right before your interview) can cause delays.

Stage 3: Complete the DS-160 Online Application

The DS-160 is the Online Nonimmigrant Visa Application form used by the US Department of State. It is the longest and most detailed part of the paperwork, and it is where many Pakistani students make avoidable errors. Every answer you provide becomes part of your official visa record.

Accessing the DS-160

Go to ceac.state.gov/genniv — this is the only official website for the DS-160. Select "Pakistan" as your country and choose the location where you will attend your interview (Islamabad, Karachi or Lahore). The form will save your progress as you go, so you do not have to complete it in one sitting.

Step-by-step DS-160 walkthrough for Pakistani F-1 applicants

Here is what each section requires, with Pakistan-specific guidance:

Personal information: Enter your full name exactly as it appears on your passport. Include any other names or aliases. Provide your date of birth, city of birth, country of birth, nationality, and national identification number (your CNIC number). If you have dual nationality, disclose it. Answer the gender question and provide your marital status.

Address and phone: Your current home address in Pakistan, your primary phone number, your email address, and any social media handles you use. The social media question is not optional — the DS-160 asks which platforms you use and your usernames. Provide accurate information; failing to disclose is worse than disclosing.

Passport information: Your passport number, country of issuance, city of issuance, issue date, and expiration date. Your passport must be valid for at least six months beyond your intended date of entry into the United States. If your passport expires within six months, renew it before applying.

Travel information: For the question about your specific travel plans, you can state "no" if you have not yet booked flights. You will need to provide the name and address of the school you plan to attend (copy from your I-20) and the intended date of arrival in the US (typically 30 days or fewer before your programme start date, as allowed by F-1 regulations).

Previous US travel: If you have previously been to the US (for example on a B-1/B-2 visitor visa, a previous student visa, or under the Visa Waiver Programme), provide those details including visa numbers and dates. If you were ever refused a US visa, disclose this — including the date, location, and visa type. Dishonesty here is a permanent red flag.

Family information: Names, dates of birth, and addresses of your parents, your spouse if married, and your children if applicable. Provide your father's and mother's employment details — even if they are retired, self-employed, or deceased, you must answer.

Work and education: Your employment history for the past five years and your educational history starting from secondary school. Include your university name, degree, dates of attendance, and GPA if applicable. Mention any professional training or certifications.

Security and background questions: A series of yes/no questions about criminal history, drug use, immigration violations, disease, and other grounds of inadmissibility. For the vast majority of Pakistani student applicants, all answers are "no." Answer honestly — these questions are not trick questions, but they are legally binding.

Visa classification: Select F-1 (Student). Enter your SEVIS ID from your I-20.

Photo upload: You must upload a digital photograph that meets US visa photo requirements: 2x2 inches (51x51mm), square aspect ratio, white or off-white background, taken within the last six months, showing your full face with a neutral expression. Use a professional photo studio in Pakistan that understands US visa specifications — many studios in major cities are familiar with these requirements.

Sign and submit: Review every page before submitting. Once submitted, you cannot edit the DS-160. Print the confirmation page — it has a barcode and a confirmation number (starting with AA). You will need this printed confirmation at your interview.

Common DS-160 mistakes Pakistani students make

  • Name mismatch: The name on the DS-160 must match your passport exactly. Using your father's name as a middle name when it is not on your passport, or abbreviating your name differently, creates discrepancies that can delay or derail your application
  • Wrong passport number: Double-check this. A typo in your passport number means your SEVIS record and DS-160 do not match
  • Incomplete education history: Some students list only their most recent degree. List all post-secondary education
  • Social media question skipped: This field is not optional. Provide your accounts even if you do not think they matter
  • Not saving progress: The DS-160 times out after 20 minutes of inactivity. Save frequently using the save button. Write down your application ID

Stage 4: Pay the MRV Visa Application Fee

The MRV fee (Machine Readable Visa fee) is the visa application fee charged by the US Department of State. It is separate from the SEVIS fee. For an F-1 student visa in 2026, the MRV fee is $185. This fee is non-refundable, even if your visa is denied.

How to pay the MRV fee from Pakistan

In Pakistan, the MRV fee is paid through Habib Bank Limited (HBL) as part of the appointment scheduling process through the US visa appointment service. The process works as follows:

  1. Go to ustraveldocs.com/pk and create an account or log in
  2. Select the visa category (F-1 Student) and follow the prompts
  3. You will be directed to pay the MRV fee via HBL — this may involve generating a bank challan (payment slip) that you take to any HBL branch, or using HBL's online payment channels
  4. After payment, the system will update to show your fee has been received, and you can proceed to book your appointment
  5. Save the payment confirmation receipt — you need it at the embassy

Important: The payment process and available channels can change. Always follow the instructions on ustraveldocs.com/pk as your primary guide, since this is the only authorised appointment and payment portal for US visas in Pakistan.

Stage 5: Book Your Appointment

Once your SEVIS fee is paid and your DS-160 is complete, you can book your visa interview appointment through ustraveldocs.com/pk. This is the official and only authorised system for US visa appointments in Pakistan.

Where can Pakistani students interview?

Pakistani applicants for F-1 visas can attend their interview at one of three locations:

  • US Embassy, Islamabad — located in the Diplomatic Enclave, this is the primary location for visa interviews in Pakistan
  • US Consulate General, Karachi — located on Desert Road in the Saddar area
  • US Consulate General, Lahore — located on Canal Bank Road in the Gulberg area

Appointment availability and wait times vary significantly by location and time of year. During peak season (May through August, when most Pakistani students plan to depart for fall semester), wait times can stretch to several weeks. During off-peak months, appointments may be available within a few days. Check current wait times at ustraveldocs.com/pk and choose the location with the earliest available slot if you are flexible.

Booking tips for Pakistani applicants

  • Book early: Once you have your I-20 and have paid the SEVIS fee, do not wait to book your appointment. Even if your programme starts in September, book as soon as you have the required documents ready
  • Check for emergency appointment slots: If your programme start date is approaching and no regular slots are available, you may request an emergency appointment through the same portal. You will need to explain the urgency and provide your I-20 with the programme start date
  • Morning appointments tend to move faster: Arrive at your scheduled time, but be aware that morning slots often have shorter wait times within the embassy
  • You can reschedule: If something comes up, you can reschedule your appointment, but do not reschedule excessively — the system may restrict your access if it detects repeated cancellations

Stage 6: The Visa Interview

This is the stage that matters most. The visa interview at the US Embassy or Consulate is typically brief — often just three to five minutes — but the consular officer is making a critical decision based on what you say, what you present, and what your documents show. Understanding what the officer is looking for, and preparing accordingly, is the difference between approval and refusal.

What happens at the embassy on interview day

Here is the typical flow at the US Embassy in Islamabad or the consulates in Karachi and Lahore:

  1. Security screening: You will pass through airport-style security. No electronic devices (phones may be allowed in some locations but are restricted), large bags, or food. Travel light — carry only your documents in a clear folder
  2. Document check: A staff member verifies that you have the DS-160 confirmation, passport, I-20, and photo
  3. Fingerprinting: Your fingerprints are taken electronically at a window
  4. The interview: You approach the consular officer's window. The officer speaks English, and the interview is conducted in English. You have a brief conversation (usually 2–5 minutes) about your study plans, finances, and ties to Pakistan
  5. Decision: The officer tells you the outcome immediately: approved (you keep your passport for visa stamping), refused under Section 214(b) (you receive a refusal letter), or placed in administrative processing (Section 221(g), you are asked to submit additional documents)

Typical interview questions and how to answer them

While every interview is different, Pakistani F-1 applicants are commonly asked some variation of these questions. The key to answering well is to be concise, honest, and specific.

"Why did you choose this university?"
Do not say "because it is a good university." Instead, mention specific features: a particular professor's research, a programme's unique feature, a lab, a ranking in your field, or a connection to your career goals. Example: "I chose the University of Texas at Austin because their electrical engineering department has one of the top power systems labs in the US, and my research interest in renewable energy microgrids aligns directly with Dr. Ahmed's work on grid-integrated solar storage."

"Why do you want to study in the US?"
Talk about academic quality, research opportunities, access to resources, or specific industry connections. Avoid saying "because America is better" or focusing on lifestyle factors. Keep it academic and career-focused.

"What will you study?"
State your programme and degree level clearly. You should be able to name your major, explain what it involves in two or three sentences, and connect it to your career goals in Pakistan.

"Who is sponsoring your education?"
Name your sponsor (parent, family member, employer, or scholarship) and briefly describe their financial capacity. "My father is sponsoring me. He is a senior government officer in the Pakistan Administrative Service and his annual income is sufficient to cover my tuition and living expenses as listed on the I-20."

"What does your father do?" / "What does your sponsor do?"
Give a brief, factual answer. "My father runs a textile export business in Faisalabad. He has been in this business for 20 years and the business generates [X] per year." If asked for more detail, provide it, but do not ramble.

"How much is your bank balance?"
State the figure from your bank statements. "My father has approximately [X] PKR in his [bank name] account, which has been maintained for the past six months." Do not quote a figure you cannot back up with documents.

"Do you plan to work in the US after graduation?"
This is a test of nonimmigrant intent. The honest answer for most F-1 students is that they plan to return to Pakistan. If you are interested in OPT (Optional Practical Training) or CPT, you can mention these as legal programmes within the F-1 framework, but frame your answer around returning to Pakistan: "I plan to return to Pakistan after completing my OPT, which is a training programme allowed under my visa. I want to gain two years of practical experience at a US company before returning to join my family's business / to work in Pakistan's growing tech sector."

"What are your plans after you return to Pakistan?"
Be specific. Name a company, an industry, a city, or a career goal. "After completing my MBA, I plan to return to Karachi and work in the banking sector. Pakistan's financial services industry is growing rapidly, and an American MBA will position me for leadership roles at institutions like HBL or MCB."

"Why should I give you a visa?"
This direct question is not uncommon. A strong answer combines your clear study plan, your financial readiness, and your ties to Pakistan: "I have been accepted by [University] for [Programme], I have the financial means to support myself as shown on my I-20 and in my bank statements, and I have strong family and professional ties to Pakistan that will bring me back after my studies."

Section 214(b) and nonimmigrant intent

The single most important legal concept in your F-1 interview is Section 214(b) of the Immigration and Nationality Act. Under this section, every F-1 visa applicant is presumed to have "immigrant intent" — that is, the officer assumes you plan to stay in the US permanently unless you prove otherwise. It is your job to overcome this presumption by demonstrating:

  • You have a genuine, specific reason to study at this particular US institution
  • You have the financial means to complete your studies without working illegally
  • You have strong ties to Pakistan that compel you to return home after your studies

Ties to Pakistan can include: family relationships (especially if you are responsible for elderly parents, siblings' education, or family property), property ownership, a job offer or family business waiting for you, bank accounts and investments in Pakistan, a spouse or children remaining in Pakistan, or community and religious ties.

The consular officer evaluates your ties holistically. There is no single document that "proves" nonimmigrant intent — it is the totality of your application, your documents, and your interview performance.

What happens if you are refused under 214(b)?

A 214(b) refusal is not permanent. You can reapply at any time, but you should do so only if your circumstances have materially changed — for example, you have stronger financial documentation, your ties to Pakistan have strengthened, or you can address the specific concern the officer raised. Simply reapplying with the same documents a week later is unlikely to produce a different result.

When you reapply, you must complete a new DS-160, pay the MRV fee again, and schedule a new interview. The previous refusal will be in your record, and the new officer will see it. Be prepared to explain what has changed since your last application.

Financial Requirements: What You Need to Show

The financial documentation is where many Pakistani F-1 applications succeed or fail. The consular officer needs to see that you can pay for your education without resorting to unauthorised employment in the US.

How much money do you need?

There is no single dollar amount that applies to every applicant. Your required amount is determined by the cost of attendance (COA) listed on your Form I-20, which includes tuition, fees, room, board, books, personal expenses, and health insurance for one academic year. If your programme is longer than one year, you technically only need to show funds for the first year, though showing more strengthens your application.

For context, here are realistic COA ranges at different types of US institutions:

Bank statements: the six-month rule

The standard expectation for Pakistani F-1 applicants is six months of bank statements from the primary account holder (you or your sponsor). The statements should show:

  • Consistent, sustainable income: Regular salary deposits, business income, or other verifiable revenue streams. A healthy bank balance that is built up gradually over months is far more convincing than a large lump-sum deposit made days before the interview
  • No sudden large deposits: If a large amount appears suddenly (e.g., a relative transferring money just before the statement period), the officer may question where the money came from. If this happens, be prepared to explain with supporting documentation
  • Adequate closing balance: The closing balance should cover or exceed the COA on your I-20

If your sponsor's salary goes into one bank account but they maintain savings in another account or in a different bank, you can show statements from both. The key is that the funds are accessible, documented, and their source is verifiable.

Sponsorship and affidavit of support

Most Pakistani F-1 applicants are sponsored by their parents. If your parents are your sponsors, you should provide:

  • Affidavit of support — a signed declaration (sometimes notarised) stating that your sponsor commits to covering your tuition and living expenses for the duration of your studies. There is no single required format, but the letter should include your name, the sponsor's name and relationship, the university and programme name, and a clear statement of financial commitment
  • Sponsor's bank statements — six months, showing consistent income and adequate balance
  • Sponsor's employment letter or business proof — a letter from the employer on company letterhead confirming the sponsor's position, salary, and length of employment, or business registration documents if the sponsor is self-employed
  • Tax returns or tax documents — if available, these add credibility. In Pakistan, FBR (Federal Board of Revenue) tax returns or notices showing declared income are helpful but not always available. If your sponsor files taxes, include them; if not, explain the situation honestly
  • Property documents (optional but helpful) — land records, property valuation, or vehicle registration can supplement the financial picture and also demonstrate ties to Pakistan

What if you have a scholarship or assistantship?

If you have been awarded a scholarship, teaching assistantship, or research assistantship, this significantly strengthens your application. Provide the official award letter with your I-20 package. The scholarship amount will be factored into the I-20 financial summary, reducing the amount you need to show from personal or family funds.

Even with a full scholarship covering tuition, you may still need to show funds for living expenses (room, board, personal spending) that are not covered by the award.

Funds in PKR vs USD

Your bank statements will be in Pakistani Rupees. The consular officer understands this — they evaluate the PKR amount converted to USD at the prevailing exchange rate. There is no need to convert your funds to USD before the interview. However, if you also hold a USD-denominated account (for example, in a Pakistani bank's foreign currency account or an overseas account), showing that alongside your PKR account is helpful.

The Interview: Practical Tips for Pakistani Students

Beyond the preparation covered above, here are practical, Pakistan-specific tips for interview day.

Dress professionally

There is no formal dress code, but dressing in clean, professional attire shows respect for the process. For men, a collared shirt and trousers are appropriate. For women, modest professional attire is fine. You do not need a suit, but avoid t-shirts, jeans, or casual clothing.

Arrive on time

Embassies and consulates have strict scheduling. Arrive at your appointment time, not too early (you may be turned away) and not late. Bring only your documents — leave your phone, laptop, bag, and any large items at home or in your car. Storage facilities at US embassies in Pakistan are extremely limited.

Speak in English

The interview is conducted in English. If your English is not strong, practise common interview questions beforehand. The officer is not testing your language proficiency the way a TOEFL examiner would — they are assessing whether you can communicate clearly enough to understand and respond to questions. If you genuinely do not understand a question, it is better to ask for clarification than to give an irrelevant answer.

Be concise and confident

The officer sees hundreds of applicants. Short, direct answers are better than long, rambling explanations. Have a clear mental outline of your answers to the most common questions. Make eye contact, speak at a normal pace, and do not fidget.

Do not volunteer unnecessary information

Answer the question asked and stop. If the officer wants more detail, they will ask a follow-up. Over-explaining can sometimes create confusion or raise questions you did not intend to answer.

Do not argue or become emotional

If the officer asks a challenging question, remain calm and answer factually. If you are refused, accept the decision gracefully. Arguing, crying, or raising your voice will not change the outcome and may negatively affect future applications.

After the Interview: What Happens Next

If you are approved

If the consular officer approves your visa, they will keep your passport for visa stamping. You will be given instructions to check the status of your passport at ustraveldocs.com/pk. Passport return with the visa stamp typically takes 3–5 business days through the designated courier service (usually TCS or another authorised delivery partner in Pakistan).

Your passport will contain the F-1 visa stamp, which includes your name, passport number, visa category (F-1), number of entries (usually "M" for multiple), and the visa validity period. The validity period indicates the window during which you can use the visa to enter the United States — it does not determine how long you can stay. Your length of stay in the US is determined by your I-20 programme dates and your F-1 status, not the visa stamp's expiry date.

If you are placed in administrative processing

Some applicants receive a blue or yellow 221(g) slip requesting additional documentation or indicating that their case requires further administrative processing. This is not a refusal — it means the embassy needs more time or information. Processing can take anywhere from two weeks to several months. If this happens, provide the requested documents promptly and check the status of your case online.

If you are refused under 214(b)

You will receive a standard refusal letter. You can reapply at any time, but as noted above, you should do so only when your circumstances have meaningfully changed. The refusal letter does not contain the specific reason for refusal — it is a standard form. If you want to understand why you were refused, reflect honestly on your interview: was your financial documentation insufficient? Were your ties to Pakistan unclear? Did you struggle to articulate your study plan? Address whatever weakness you identify before reapplying.

Maintaining F-1 Status After You Arrive in the US

Getting the visa is only half the story. Once you enter the United States on an F-1 visa, you must maintain your F-1 status throughout your studies. Violating your status can result in deportation and make it extremely difficult to obtain any future US visa.

Full-time enrollment

You must be enrolled full-time at your SEVP-certified school. For most programmes, full-time means at least 12 credit hours per semester for undergraduates and as defined by the graduate school for graduate students. Dropping below full-time without DSO authorisation is a status violation.

Keeping your I-20 current

Your I-20 must remain valid. If you change your major, extend your programme, change your funding, or make any other significant change, your DSO will issue an updated I-20. Do not let an expired I-20 sit in your drawer — address any needed updates promptly.

Reporting requirements

You must report certain changes to your DSO within 10 days: changes of address, changes of name, changes of major, and changes of financial support. Your DSO will update SEVIS accordingly.

Work restrictions

F-1 students have limited work authorisation:

  • On-campus employment: You may work up to 20 hours per week on campus while school is in session, and up to 40 hours per week during scheduled breaks. You do not need separate work authorisation for on-campus jobs
  • Curricular Practical Training (CPT): Work authorisation for employment that is an integral part of your curriculum (e.g., an internship required by your programme). Your DSO authorises CPT, and it must be related to your field of study
  • Optional Practical Training (OPT): Up to 12 months of work authorisation after completing your programme (or after each degree level). STEM graduates may qualify for a 24-month STEM OPT extension, for a total of 36 months. You must apply to USCIS for an Employment Authorisation Document (EAD) card before starting OPT
  • Severe economic hardship: In limited circumstances, if you suffer unforeseen financial hardship, you may apply for off-campus work authorisation through your DSO and USCIS

Working without authorisation — for example, taking a job at an off-campus restaurant or doing freelance work for a US company — is a serious status violation. It can result in termination of your SEVIS record, deportation, and a permanent bar from future US visas.

Travel and re-entry

If you leave the US and wish to return, you need a valid F-1 visa stamp in your passport (unless you are travelling to Canada, Mexico, or adjacent islands for fewer than 30 days, in which case automatic visa revalidation may apply under certain conditions). When re-entering the US, carry your valid passport, F-1 visa, I-20 (with a valid travel signature from your DSO), and proof of ongoing enrollment.

Common Refusal Reasons and How to Avoid Them

Understanding why Pakistani F-1 applicants are refused helps you prepare a stronger application. The most common reasons include:

1. Insufficient financial evidence

This is the single most common reason for refusal among Pakistani applicants. If your bank statements do not clearly show funds covering your COA, or if the funds appear recent and unexplained, the officer will refuse under 214(b). Solution: build a consistent financial record over six months or more, explain any unusual deposits, and have your sponsor's income documentation ready.

2. Unable to articulate study plan

If you cannot clearly explain why you chose this university, this programme, or this field of study, the officer may doubt that your application is genuine. Solution: prepare specific, detailed answers. Know the name of your department, your major, and two or three reasons why this school is the right fit.

3. Weak ties to Pakistan

If you cannot demonstrate compelling reasons to return to Pakistan, the officer may conclude you intend to immigrate. This is particularly common for younger applicants who have limited property, family responsibilities, or career prospects in Pakistan. Solution: document everything that ties you to Pakistan — family obligations, property, a family business, community ties, or a clear career plan that requires you to be in Pakistan.

4. Previous visa refusal or immigration violation

If you have been refused a US visa before, or if you have overstayed a previous visa in any country, this will be in your record. Solution: be honest about previous refusals on the DS-160 and in the interview. Explain what has changed since the last refusal.

5. Dishonesty or inconsistency

If your DS-160 answers do not match your documents, or if your verbal answers contradict your written application, the officer will refuse you and may issue a finding of material misrepresentation, which can result in a permanent bar. Solution: be completely honest on the DS-160 and in the interview. Never fabricate documents, income, or ties.

6. Attended a low-credibility institution

If your I-20 is from a school with a high visa refusal rate or a reputation for being used primarily as an immigration pathway rather than a genuine educational institution, you may face additional scrutiny. Solution: choose a reputable, accredited institution. If you have already been admitted to a less well-known school, be prepared to explain why it is the right academic choice for you.

Pakistani-Specific Tips and Considerations

HEC attestation

Pakistani educational documents (degrees, transcripts) should ideally be attested by the Higher Education Commission (HEC) of Pakistan. While the US embassy does not explicitly require HEC attestation for the F-1 visa interview, having attested documents adds credibility and is useful if the officer asks to verify your qualifications. Get your degrees and transcripts attested by HEC before your interview — the process is straightforward through HEC's online portal and regional offices.

Attested and notarised documents

Beyond HEC attestation, some financial documents (especially affidavits of support) may benefit from notarisation by a notary public or an oath commissioner in Pakistan. This is not strictly required but adds a layer of formality that can help.

Currency conversion and PKR amounts

Pakistani bank statements will show amounts in PKR. The consular officer will mentally convert these to USD. At a rough rate of 280 PKR per USD in 2026, a balance of PKR 5,000,000 is approximately $17,850. If your COA is $30,000, you would need to show additional funding from another source or a second sponsor. Be clear about the math in your head before the interview.

Choosing the right embassy location

If you live in Sindh or Balochistan, the Karachi consulate may be most convenient. If you live in Punjab, Lahore may be easiest. If you live in KPK, Islamabad is the natural choice. However, you are not required to interview at the location closest to your home — you can choose any of the three. Some applicants choose a location that historically has shorter wait times or less crowding. Check wait times for all three locations before booking.

Pre-departure orientations

EducationUSA Pakistan (the US Embassy's advising network) occasionally organises pre-departure orientations for Pakistani students heading to the US. These are free and cover topics like cultural adjustment, US academic expectations, travel tips, and maintaining F-1 status. Check EducationUSA Pakistan's social media pages and website for announcements.

Scams to Avoid

The F-1 visa process attracts scammers who prey on students' anxiety. Protect yourself by knowing what is real and what is not.

Fake visa agents

No agent, consultant, or fixer can guarantee you a US visa. Anyone who claims otherwise is lying. The visa decision is made solely by the consular officer at the embassy. Agents who charge large fees and promise connections at the embassy are running a scam. You can complete the entire process yourself — the DS-160 is designed for individual applicants, and the official instructions on ustraveldocs.com/pk are clear and detailed.

Unofficial websites

Only use these official websites:

  • ustraveldocs.com/pk — for booking appointments and paying the MRV fee
  • fmjfee.com — for paying the SEVIS I-901 fee
  • ceac.state.gov/genniv — for completing the DS-160
  • travel.state.gov — for official fee and policy information
  • studyinthestates.dhs.gov — for SEVP school search and student resources

Scam websites often look similar to the real ones (with slight URL differences) and may ask for unnecessary personal information or charge extra fees. Always verify the URL before entering any information.

Document forgery

Never, ever submit forged bank statements, fake employment letters, or fabricated academic documents. US embassies have sophisticated verification methods, and the consequences of fraud are severe: a permanent visa bar under INA Section 212(a)(6)(C)(i), potential criminal prosecution, and a permanent record that will affect every future immigration application to any country.

Social media scams

Be sceptical of visa advice from anonymous social media accounts, WhatsApp groups, or Facebook pages. While some groups share genuinely helpful peer advice, others spread misinformation or are run by agents trying to attract clients. Always cross-reference any advice against official US government sources.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I apply for an F-1 visa while I am already in the US on a B-1/B-2 visa?

Generally, no. You cannot change from B-1/B-2 status to F-1 status while remaining in the US in most circumstances. You would need to return to Pakistan and apply for the F-1 visa at the embassy. There are very limited exceptions (change of status applications filed with USCIS), but these are complex, slow, and not recommended for students who can simply return home and apply normally.

How many times can I reapply after a refusal?

There is no legal limit on how many times you can reapply for an F-1 visa after a refusal. However, each application costs money (the MRV fee is non-refundable), and each refusal is recorded. Reapply when you have a materially stronger case, not simply because you hope for a different officer or a different mood.

Do I need to book a round-trip flight?

No. You should book a one-way flight to the US. A round-trip ticket could actually raise questions about whether you intend to return to Pakistan. When you enter the US, you will be admitted in F-1 status for the duration of your programme, not based on a return flight date.

Can my F-1 visa be valid for multiple entries?

Yes. Most F-1 visas issued to Pakistani students are multiple-entry, marked with an "M" on the visa stamp. This allows you to travel in and out of the US during the validity of your visa without reapplying each time.

What if my programme dates change after I get my visa?

If your programme start date changes (for example, you defer admission to a later semester), you need an updated I-20 with the new dates. Your F-1 visa stamp remains valid as long as it has not expired and you have a valid I-20. However, you cannot enter the US more than 30 days before your new programme start date.

Is there a quota or limit on F-1 visas for Pakistani students?

No. There is no numerical limit or quota on F-1 visas. Every eligible applicant can receive one. The number of F-1 visas issued to Pakistani students fluctuates year to year based on the number of applicants and the overall US-Pakistan diplomatic relationship, but there is no cap.

Do I need health insurance for the F-1 visa?

You do not need health insurance to obtain the F-1 visa itself. However, most US universities require F-1 students to have health insurance as a condition of enrollment, and you will need to purchase a plan (either the university's mandatory plan or an approved alternative) once you arrive. Some I-20s include health insurance in the COA figure.

A Complete Checklist for Pakistani F-1 Applicants

Use this checklist to track your progress through the visa process:

  • I have been accepted by a SEVP-certified US school
  • I have received my Form I-20 and verified all details
  • I have signed my I-20
  • I have paid the SEVIS I-901 fee ($350) at fmjfee.com
  • I have saved/printed my SEVIS fee receipt
  • I have completed the DS-160 at ceac.state.gov/genniv
  • I have printed my DS-160 confirmation page
  • I have paid the MRV fee ($185) via HBL through ustraveldocs.com/pk
  • I have saved/printed my MRV fee receipt
  • I have booked my interview appointment
  • I have six months of bank statements (mine or my sponsor's)
  • I have my sponsor's affidavit of support
  • I have my sponsor's employment/business documentation
  • I have my academic transcripts and degree certificates (HEC-attested if possible)
  • I have my test scores (TOEFL/IELTS/GRE/GMAT)
  • I have my admission letter from the university
  • I have my passport (valid for at least 6 months beyond intended entry)
  • I have a passport-size photo meeting US visa specifications
  • I have practised my interview answers
  • I know the COA on my I-20 and can explain how my funds cover it
  • I can clearly explain why I chose this university and what I plan to study
  • I can clearly explain my plans to return to Pakistan after my studies

Final Thoughts

The US F-1 student visa from Pakistan is not a mystery — it is a structured process with clear steps, clear requirements, and clear criteria. The consular officer's job is to determine whether you are a genuine student with adequate funding and a legitimate plan to study and return home. Your job is to demonstrate exactly that, through your documents, your DS-160 answers, and your interview performance.

The most successful Pakistani F-1 applicants share three traits: they are well-prepared (they know their documents, their I-20 figures, and their answers), they are honest (they do not fabricate or exaggerate), and they are specific (they can name their university, their programme, their sponsor, and their career plan in Pakistan with confidence and detail).

If you are starting this process, begin now. Do not wait until the last minute to gather your documents, pay your fees, or book your appointment. The earlier you start, the more time you have to fix problems, gather additional documentation if needed, and prepare for the interview without panic.

Frequently asked questions

How much is the US F-1 visa fee for Pakistani students?+

As of 2026, the SEVIS I-901 fee for new F-1 students is $350, and the MRV (visa application) fee is $185. Confirm both on travel.state.gov and fmjfee.com before paying, as fees can change. Budget roughly PKR 150,000–160,000 in combined fees at current exchange rates.

How much bank balance is required for a US student visa?+

There is no fixed dollar amount written in law, but you must show you can cover at least the first year of tuition plus living expenses as listed on your I-20. For most Pakistani applicants this means showing PKR 4,000,000–8,000,000 (or the USD equivalent), depending on the university. The consular officer wants to see sustainable, documented funds - not a sudden deposit.

How long does the US F-1 visa take to process in Pakistan?+

After your interview, routine F-1 visa processing in Pakistan typically takes 3–5 business days, though administrative processing (Section 221(g)) can extend this to several weeks or months. Appointment wait times at the US Embassy in Islamabad and consulates in Karachi and Lahore fluctuate, so check ustraveldocs.com/pk for current wait times.

What documents do I need for the F-1 visa interview?+

At minimum you need: a valid passport, DS-160 confirmation page, MRV fee receipt, SEVIS I-901 fee receipt, Form I-20 signed by you and the university, passport-size photograph meeting US visa specifications, academic transcripts and diplomas, English-test scores, bank statements and financial sponsorship documents, and any evidence of ties to Pakistan such as property documents or family obligations.

Can my parents sponsor my F-1 visa?+

Yes. Parental sponsorship is the most common scenario for Pakistani F-1 applicants. Your parents will need to provide an affidavit of support, recent bank statements (typically six months), employment letters or business proof, and tax documentation. The funds must appear consistent and sustainable - a large lump sum deposited recently will raise questions.
