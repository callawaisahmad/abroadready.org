Scholarship Application Fee Scams: How to Spot and Avoid Fake Scholarships

📅 Sep 3, 2026 ⏱ 10 min read ✍️ AbroadReady Team

You found a scholarship worth $10,000 and the email says you've been selected. All you need to do is pay a $50 processing fee and the money is yours. You reach for your card — and that is exactly where the scam wants you. Scholarship application fee scams cost students millions every year, and they are getting more convincing. Fake university emails, professional-looking websites, and social media ads are pulling in applicants who cannot tell the difference between a real opportunity and a trap.

Here is the fact that stops most scams before they start: do scholarships charge application fees? No. Legitimate scholarships never charge you to apply. If someone asks for money upfront, it is a scam, full stop. This guide breaks down every type of fake scholarship out there, shows you real examples of scam emails, gives you the scholarship scam red flags 2026 you need to watch for, and walks you through exactly what to do if you have already been caught.

Key takeaways

  • Legitimate scholarships NEVER charge application fees. If you are asked to pay money to apply, receive, or process a scholarship, it is a scam.
  • The most common trick is the advance-fee scam: a small "processing fee" of $10–$200 that leads to bigger demands or identity theft.
  • Over 500,000 students lose money to scholarship scams annually in the US alone. Globally the numbers are far higher.
  • Always verify a scholarship by checking the official organization's website, contacting them directly, and searching scam databases.
  • If you have been scammed, contact your bank immediately, file a cybercrime report, and warn others.
  • Safe scholarship sources include official university websites, government portals (Fulbright, Chevening, DAAD, Erasmus Mundus), and established databases.

The golden rule: legitimate scholarships never charge fees

Before we get into the different scam types, you need to burn this into your memory: a real scholarship will never ask you for money. Not an application fee. Not a processing fee. Not an administrative charge. Not a "refundable deposit." Not a fee to unlock the full listing. Not a fee to transfer the funds to your account.

Scholarship providers fund their awards through endowments, corporate sponsors, government budgets, or private donors. The entire point of a scholarship is to give money to students, not take it from them. The moment anyone connected to a scholarship asks you to pay, you are not dealing with a scholarship. You are dealing with a scammer.

This is also true for fully funded scholarships — if a program claims to be fully funded but still asks you to pay an upfront fee before you can access the application, walk away.

Common scholarship scam types

Scholarship scammers are creative, but they tend to fall into five categories. Knowing these patterns is your best defense.

1. Upfront fee scams

The scammer advertises a scholarship and requires an "application fee," "processing fee," or "registration fee" before you can apply or before they release the funds. The fee is usually small — $10 to $200 — because they know most people will not question that amount. Some operate as subscription traps: you pay once, then find recurring charges on your card. Others collect fees from thousands of students and vanish overnight.

2. Fake university emails

You receive an email that looks like it comes from a university — it uses their logo, their formatting, even a name that sounds like a real department. The email says you have been pre-selected for a scholarship and asks you to "confirm your eligibility" by providing personal details or paying a fee. The sender address is the giveaway: something like scholarships-university@gmail.com or admissions@cambr1dge-edu.com instead of the real domain.

3. "You've been selected" scams

You never applied for anything, but you receive a message telling you that you have been "chosen" or "nominated" for a prestigious scholarship. This plays on excitement and urgency. The email or message usually includes a deadline ("you must respond within 48 hours") to pressure you into acting without thinking. Real scholarships do not randomly select people who never applied.

4. Data harvesting

Not every scam asks for money directly. Some are designed to collect your personal information: full name, date of birth, passport number, bank account details, home address, and phone number. The "scholarship application" form asks for far more information than any legitimate scholarship would need at the initial stage. Once they have your data, they can commit identity theft, open accounts in your name, or sell your information to other scammers.

5. Advance-fee fraud

This is the most elaborate version. You are told you have won a large scholarship — $20,000, $50,000, sometimes more. To release the funds, you need to pay a "tax," "insurance fee," or "bank transfer fee." They may even send you a fake check that appears to clear (it does not — it bounces days later). By the time you realize the check is fake, you have already sent real money. This is a variation of the classic 419 scam adapted for the scholarship space.

Scholarship scam red flags

These are the specific warning signs that should make you stop and verify before you do anything:

  1. They ask for money. Any fee, any amount, any reason. This is the single biggest red flag. Legitimate scholarships do not charge application fees, processing fees, or any other upfront cost.
  2. Artificial urgency. "You must respond within 24 hours" or "This offer expires tonight." Scammers create panic so you skip the verification step. Real scholarship deadlines are measured in weeks or months, not hours.
  3. Generic greeting. "Dear Applicant" or "Dear Student" instead of your actual name. Legitimate scholarship programs have your name because you applied or were nominated through a known process.
  4. Asking for bank or card details early. No legitimate scholarship asks for your bank account number, credit card details, or payment information at the application stage. Some may ask for bank details at the very end, after you have been officially selected and are going through disbursement — but never to apply.
  5. The offer is too good to be true. A full-ride scholarship from an organization you have never heard of, awarded to you without an application, for an amount that covers everything plus spending money. If it sounds unreal, it is.
  6. Grammatical errors and typos. Professional scholarship programs proofread their communications. Emails full of spelling mistakes, awkward phrasing, or broken English are a strong indicator of fraud.
  7. Suspicious sender domain. Check the email address carefully. Legitimate organizations use their official domain (e.g., @fulbright.org, @daad.de). Watch for subtle misspellings like @daad-de.org or @fulbrightscholarship.com.
  8. No official website or social media presence. If you search for the scholarship and find nothing — no official page, no LinkedIn profile, no news coverage — it does not exist. Real scholarships leave a digital footprint.
  9. They found you, not the other way around. You never applied, but they are offering you money out of nowhere. Legitimate scholarships either require an application or are awarded through a known selection process at an institution you are already connected to.
  10. Pressure to act now, verify later. "Pay first, we will send the documents after." Every time. A real organization lets you verify everything before you commit any money or personal information.

Real examples of scholarship scam emails

Knowing what these scams look like in practice helps you spot them faster. Here are three common patterns, with analysis of what is wrong.

Example 1: The "processing fee" email

From: scholarships@global-fund-education.com

Subject: Congratulations! You have been selected for the Global Education Fund Scholarship 2026

Dear Applicant,

We are pleased to inform you that you have been selected as a recipient of the Global Education Fund Scholarship worth $15,000 for the 2026 academic year. Your profile was reviewed from our global database and you meet the criteria.

To confirm your scholarship and receive your funds, please pay a one-time processing fee of $75 via the link below within 48 hours:

[PAY NOW BUTTON]

Failure to confirm within the deadline will result in automatic disqualification.

Best regards,
Scholarship Committee

What is wrong: You never applied. The sender domain is not associated with any known scholarship body. "Dear Applicant" is generic. They ask for a processing fee. The 48-hour deadline pressures you into paying without verifying. No phone number, no physical address, no link to an official organization website.

Example 2: The fake university email

From: financial.aid@oxford-university-scholarship.co.uk

Subject: University of Oxford — International Merit Scholarship Invitation

Dear Student,

The University of Oxford is offering a limited number of International Merit Scholarships for outstanding students from Pakistan. You have been shortlisted based on your academic record.

Please reply to this email with a copy of your passport, your academic transcripts, and a processing fee of $120 to secure your place. Only 20 spots remain.

Regards,
Dr. James Mitchell
Financial Aid Office, University of Oxford

What is wrong: The real University of Oxford uses @ox.ac.uk, not @oxford-university-scholarship.co.uk. Real universities do not email random students asking for passport copies and fees. There is no "Dr. James Mitchell" in Oxford's financial aid office. Only 20 spots remaining is classic urgency manipulation. Check our guide on how to spot scholarship scams for more on this.

Example 3: The advance-fee scam

From: awards@erasmusmundus-grants.org

Subject: Award Confirmation — Erasmus Mundus Joint Master Degree Grant

Dear Scholar,

Congratulations! Your application has been reviewed and you have been awarded a full Erasmus Mundus scholarship covering tuition, travel, and living expenses (€25,000).

To finalize your grant and release the first payment, you must pay a refundable insurance deposit of €200. This deposit will be returned to you along with your first scholarship disbursement.

Please provide your bank details and transfer the deposit to the account below within 5 business days.

Best wishes,
Erasmus Mundus Selection Board

What is wrong: The real Erasmus Mundus program uses @erasmus-plus.ec.europa.eu and never asks for deposits. A "refundable insurance deposit" is not part of any legitimate Erasmus Mundus process. Asking for your bank details at this stage is a data harvesting play. Real Erasmus Mundus selections are made through the official application portal, not by email from a random domain.

How to verify a scholarship is legitimate

When you see a scholarship opportunity, do not trust it by default. Verify it. Here is the step-by-step process:

  1. Check the official website. Go directly to the scholarship provider's website by typing the URL into your browser yourself. Do not click links in the suspicious email. If the scholarship is real, it will be listed on the organization's official site. If it is not there, it does not exist.
  2. Verify the sender's domain. Look at the actual email address, not the display name. A display name can say "University of Cambridge" while the actual address is scammer123@gmail.com. Check for misspellings in the domain — this is the most common trick.
  3. Contact the university or organization directly. Use contact details from the official website (not from the email). Call their admissions or financial aid office. Ask: "Is this scholarship real? Did you send this email?" If they say no, you have your answer.
  4. Search scam databases. The Better Business Bureau Scam Tracker, the FTC, and sites like ScholarshipScam.org maintain databases of reported scams. Search for the scholarship name or organization.
  5. Search the scholarship name online. Type the exact scholarship name into a search engine and add "scam" or "fake." If other students have reported it, you will find warnings quickly.
  6. Ask your university. If you are already enrolled or have an acceptance letter, ask your university's international student office or financial aid office. They maintain lists of legitimate scholarships and can quickly tell you if something is real.

What to do if you have been scammed

If you have already paid a fee or shared personal details with a fake scholarship, take these steps immediately:

  1. Contact your bank or card company. Call them right away. Explain that you were the victim of a scam. If you paid by credit card, request a chargeback. If you paid by debit card or bank transfer, ask them to reverse the transaction. Time matters — the sooner you contact them, the better your chances of recovering the money.
  2. Change your passwords. If you created an account on the scam website using the same password you use elsewhere, change it everywhere. Scammers often test stolen credentials on email, banking, and social media accounts.
  3. File a cybercrime report. Report the scam to the relevant authority in your country:
    • US: FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov
    • UK: Action Fraud at actionfraud.police.uk
    • Pakistan: FIA Cyber Crime Wing at cybercrime.gov.pk
    • India: National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in
    • EU: Your national consumer protection agency
  4. Report to the university whose name was used. If the scammer impersonated a university, contact that university's IT security or admissions office. They may already be aware and can help warn other students.
  5. Warn others. Post about the scam (without sharing your personal details) on student forums, social media, and WhatsApp groups. The more people know, the fewer victims the scammer will find.
  6. Document everything. Save the emails, screenshots of the website, payment receipts, and any other communication. You will need this evidence for your report.

Understanding your proof of funds requirements can also help you avoid scams that target students who are already anxious about finances.

Safe scholarship sources

The best way to avoid scams is to use sources you can trust. Stick to these:

  • Official university websites. Every university has a scholarships or financial aid page. Go there directly. If you are applying to a university, check their specific scholarship pages for international students.
  • Government scholarship portals. These are the gold standard:
    • Fulbright Program (US)
    • Chevening (UK)
    • DAAD (Germany)
    • Erasmus Mundus (EU)
    • Australia Awards
    • HEC Pakistan (for Pakistani students)
  • Established scholarship databases. ScholarshipPortal, DAAD scholarship database, IDP, and similar platforms that have been around for years and are known to the education community.
  • Your own education ministry or higher education commission. Most countries maintain lists of approved international scholarships for their citizens.

Frequently asked questions

Do legitimate scholarships ever charge application fees?+

No. Legitimate scholarships never charge application fees, processing fees, or administrative fees upfront. If a scholarship asks you to pay money before you can apply or receive the award, it is almost certainly a scam. The only legitimate fees you might encounter during your study abroad journey are university application fees (paid directly to the university) and visa fees (paid to the government). Scholarship providers make money from sponsors, endowments, or donors — they do not take money from applicants.

What is the most common scholarship scam?+

The most common scholarship scam is the advance-fee fraud, where you receive an email saying you have been selected for a scholarship worth thousands of dollars, but you need to pay a small "processing fee," "administrative charge," or "registration fee" to claim it. The fee is usually between $10 and $200 — small enough that many people pay it without thinking. Once you pay, the scammers either disappear or come back asking for more money. Some scammers also harvest your personal data (passport numbers, bank details, addresses) to commit identity theft.

How do I check if a scholarship email is fake?+

Check the sender's email address — legitimate organizations use official domains (e.g., @fulbright.org, @daad.de), not Gmail, Yahoo, or random domains. Look for generic greetings like "Dear Applicant" instead of your actual name. Search for the scholarship name on the official organization's website. Contact the university or organization directly using contact details from their official website — not from the email. Check scam databases like the FTC, BBB, or scholarship scam alert sites. If the email asks for money, personal bank details, or creates extreme urgency, it is almost certainly fake.

What should I do if I paid a scam scholarship fee?+

Contact your bank or card company immediately to report the transaction and request a chargeback if possible. File a report with your country's cybercrime authority — in the US the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov, in the UK Action Fraud, and in Pakistan the FIA Cyber Crime Wing at cybercrime.gov.pk. Report the scam to the university or organization whose name was used. Change any passwords if you shared login credentials. Warn friends and family so they do not fall for the same scam. Document everything — save the emails, screenshots, payment receipts, and any communication for your report.

Where can I find legitimate scholarships?+

The safest sources for legitimate scholarships are: official university websites (check the financial aid or scholarships page directly), government scholarship portals (e.g., Fulbright, Chevening, DAAD, Erasmus Mundus, Australia Awards), established scholarship databases like ScholarshipPortal, DAAD database, and IDP, and your own country's higher education commission or education ministry websites. Avoid random social media posts, unsolicited emails, or third-party sites that require payment to access scholarship listings.
