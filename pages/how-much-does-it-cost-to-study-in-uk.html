How Much Does It Cost to Study in the UK? Complete 2026 Guide

📅 Sep 3, 2026 ⏱ 12 min read ✏️ AbroadReady Team

The UK remains one of the most popular destinations for international students — and also one of the most expensive. If you're trying to figure out the real cost of studying in the UK for international students in 2026, you've probably already noticed that the numbers you find online range wildly, often because they mix up home and international fees, forget to include visa costs, or use outdated tuition figures.

Here's what this guide actually covers: every realistic cost you'll face as an international student in the UK, from tuition and rent to the visa application fee and the health surcharge you have to pay before you even board a flight. You'll also get city-by-city budget comparisons so you can pick a destination that matches what you can afford, not just what looks good on a prospectus.

Key takeaways

  • International undergraduate tuition in the UK ranges from £11,400 to £38,000 per year; taught master's degrees run from £12,000 to £40,000.
  • Living costs are £1,529 per month in London or £1,171 per month outside London — the figures the UK government uses for visa financial requirements.
  • The student visa application fee is £490, and the Immigration Health Surcharge is £776 per year for students.
  • Total first-year cost for most international students lands between £25,000 and £55,000, depending on the university and city.
  • You can work up to 20 hours per week during term time to help with day-to-day expenses.
  • Choosing a city outside London can save you £5,000–£8,000 per year on living costs.

Tuition fees: what international students actually pay

Tuition is the single biggest cost you'll face, and the amount varies enormously depending on three things: the level of your degree, the subject you're studying, and the type of university you choose. UK universities set their own fees for international students, and there's no national cap the way there is for home students.

Undergraduate degrees

International undergraduate tuition in 2026 typically ranges from £11,400 to £38,000 per year. Most bachelor's programs in England and Wales last three years, so your total tuition bill over the full degree could be anywhere from £34,200 to £114,000. In Scotland, Scottish and EU students pay nothing at Scottish universities, but international students pay the full rate — usually between £18,000 and £35,000 per year, and many Scottish programs run for four years.

Taught master's degrees

A one-year taught master's (MA, MSc, MEd, etc.) is one of the more cost-effective routes because you're only paying one year of tuition. Fees for international students typically fall between £12,000 and £40,000, with the average landing around £20,000–£25,000. Because it's a single year, the total financial commitment — tuition plus living — is often lower than a three-year bachelor's, which is why many students from South Asia and Africa target this route.

Postgraduate research degrees

PhD and MPhil programs have more variable pricing. Annual fees for international research students typically range from £15,000 to £30,000, though some STEM and lab-based programs charge more. The good news is that funded PhD positions — where the university or a research council covers your fees and pays a stipend — are more common at the postgraduate research level. If you can secure full funding, your out-of-pocket cost drops to almost nothing.

How subject affects price

The field you choose has a big impact on tuition. Here's a rough guide to how fees break down by subject area:

  • Humanities and social sciences (English literature, history, politics, sociology): £11,400–£20,000 per year. These are generally the cheapest programs at most universities.
  • STEM fields (engineering, computer science, physics, biology): £16,000–£32,000 per year. Lab-based subjects cost more because of equipment and facilities.
  • Business and MBA: £18,000–£45,000+ per year. The MBA programs at top schools like London Business School or Oxford's Saïd can exceed £50,000 for a single year.
  • Medicine and dentistry: £25,000–£52,000 per year. Medicine is the most expensive subject, and the program typically runs five to six years.

Russell Group vs post-92 universities

The 24 Russell Group universities — including Oxford, Cambridge, UCL, Imperial, Edinburgh, Manchester, and King's College London — tend to charge higher fees, especially for popular and competitive programs. A humanities degree at a Russell Group university might start at £18,000–£22,000, while the same subject at a post-92 university (institutions that gained university status after 1992, like Bolton, Teesside, or the University of East London) could cost £12,000–£15,000.

Neither is automatically better or worse — it depends on what you want from the experience. Some of the cheapest universities in the UK for international students offer solid teaching and good employment outcomes. If budget is your primary concern, a post-92 university in a less expensive city can deliver a perfectly good education at a fraction of the price.

Living costs: the real monthly numbers

Outside of tuition, your day-to-day living costs are the next biggest part of the equation. The UK government sets a monthly figure that visa applicants must demonstrate they can cover: £1,529 per month if you're studying in London, and £1,171 per month if you're studying elsewhere in the UK. These figures are for 2026 and are used to calculate how much money you need to show in your bank account when you apply for your visa.

But what do those numbers actually translate to in real life? Here's a realistic monthly budget breakdown for a typical international student.

Monthly budget: London

  • Rent (shared flat or halls): £800–£1,200
  • Food and groceries: £200–£350
  • Local transport (Oyster card / contactless): £100–£180
  • Phone and internet: £20–£40
  • Entertainment and socialising: £100–£200
  • Course materials and printing: £30–£60
  • Total: approximately £1,250–£2,030 per month

Monthly budget: outside London

  • Rent (shared flat or halls): £450–£800
  • Food and groceries: £150–£250
  • Local transport: £50–£100
  • Phone and internet: £20–£35
  • Entertainment and socialising: £60–£150
  • Course materials and printing: £20–£50
  • Total: approximately £750–£1,385 per month

Rent is where the biggest difference lies. A room in a shared house in central London will easily cost £900+, while the same size room in Leeds or Birmingham might be £500. Groceries, transport, and socialising are cheaper outside the capital too, though not as dramatically.

Visa and immigration costs

Before you can even start worrying about rent and groceries, you'll need to pay several immigration-related fees. These are non-negotiable, and the amounts are fixed by the UK government.

Student visa application fee

The UK student visa (formerly Tier 4) costs £490 to apply from outside the UK. If you're applying to extend or switch to a student visa from within the UK, the fee is the same. This fee is non-refundable regardless of whether your application is approved or refused.

Immigration Health Surcharge (IHS)

The Immigration Health Surcharge is a mandatory payment that gives you access to the NHS while you're in the UK. For students, the rate in 2026 is £776 per year (reduced from the standard rate of £1,035 that non-student visa holders pay). You must pay the full amount upfront for the entire duration of your visa.

For a typical one-year master's program, that's £776. For a three-year undergraduate degree, you'd pay £2,328 upfront. For a four-year course, £3,104. It stings to pay this on top of everything else, but the upside is that once it's paid, you get access to the NHS — meaning no additional health insurance costs, no surprise medical bills, and no worrying about what happens if you get sick.

Biometrics and other fees

You'll also need to provide biometric information (fingerprints and a photograph) at a visa application centre. In most countries this is included in the visa application process, but some application centres charge a separate service fee. Budget around £20–£50 for this depending on where you apply. If you need to attend an English language test (like IELTS for UKVI), that's an additional £185–£200.

Total immigration costs for a one-year course: roughly £1,500–£1,600 on top of tuition and living costs. If you're applying from Pakistan, our UK student visa from Pakistan guide covers the specific steps and documentation you'll need.

One-off costs most students forget about

Beyond tuition, rent, and visa fees, there are several costs that catch first-time students off guard.

  • Flight: Depending on where you're flying from, a one-way ticket to the UK costs anywhere from £200 to £1,200. Students from South Asia typically pay £400–£800 for a return ticket.
  • Accommodation deposit: Most landlords or university halls require a deposit of four to six weeks' rent upfront, plus the first month's rent. In London that could be £1,500–£2,000 before you've unpacked a bag.
  • Initial setup costs: Bedding, kitchen essentials, warm clothing (UK winters are real), a laptop if you need one, and other basics. Budget £300–£600 for this.
  • Travel to and from the airport: A Heathrow Express ticket into central London costs £25. A taxi from Manchester Airport to the city centre runs about £30–£50.
  • CAS and university admin fees: Some universities charge a deposit of £1,000–£3,000 to secure your place and issue a Confirmation of Acceptance for Studies (CAS), which you need for the visa. This is usually deducted from your first tuition payment, not an extra charge, but you need the cash available upfront.

All told, expect to spend £2,000–£4,000 on these one-off costs before your first term even starts. For more on planning your overall budget, our proof of funds requirements guide explains exactly what documentation you'll need to show.

How to fund your UK studies

Knowing the costs is one thing; figuring out how to pay for them is another. Here are the main avenues available to you.

Scholarships

The Chevening Scholarship is one of the most well-known fully funded options — it covers tuition, living costs, travel, and visa fees for a one-year master's at any UK university. Other major scholarships include the Commonwealth Scholarship, GREAT Scholarships, and university-specific awards. Many UK universities offer partial tuition discounts of 10%–50% for strong international applicants. Start searching early and apply to as many as you qualify for using our scholarship finder.

Part-time work

As a UK student visa holder, you can work up to 20 hours per week during term time and full-time during holidays (if your course is at degree level or above at a listed institution). Part-time roles in retail, hospitality, tutoring, or campus jobs typically pay the UK minimum wage of £11.44 per hour (for 21+), giving you roughly £400–£700 per month during term time. That's enough to cover food and transport, but not rent or tuition. Read our guide to part-time work rules for the full breakdown of what you can and can't do.

Cost-saving strategies

  • Choose a one-year master's instead of a multi-year program to cut both tuition and living costs.
  • Pick a post-92 university outside London — you can save £5,000–£10,000 per year compared to a Russell Group school in the capital.
  • Apply for university-managed accommodation in your first year — it's often cheaper than private renting and includes bills.
  • Cook at home instead of eating out. Meal prepping can cut your food bill from £350/month to £150/month.
  • Use your university's student discount schemes (Totum card, UNiDAYS, Student Beans) for everything from transport to clothing.
  • Look into post-study work visa options early — the Graduate Route gives you two years to work in the UK after you graduate, which means you can earn back some of what you spent. Our post-study work visa options guide covers how this works.

Frequently asked questions

How much is tuition for international students in the UK?+

Undergraduate tuition for international students typically ranges from £11,400 to £38,000 per year. Taught master's degrees cost between £12,000 and £40,000 per year. Humanities and social science programs sit at the lower end, while STEM fields and medicine are higher. Russell Group universities tend to charge more than post-92 institutions. Business and MBA programs at top schools can exceed £45,000.

What is the Immigration Health Surcharge?+

The Immigration Health Surcharge (IHS) is a mandatory fee paid when you apply for a UK student visa. It gives you access to the National Health Service (NHS) while you're in the UK. As of 2026, the IHS costs £776 per year for students (reduced from the standard £1,035 rate). For a typical 12-month course, you pay £776; for a two-year course, £1,552. You must pay the full amount upfront before your visa is processed.

How much money do I need to show for a UK student visa?+

You need to show your full first-year tuition fees plus living costs of £1,529 per month if studying in London or £1,171 per month outside London, for up to 9 months. For example, a one-year Master's in London with £18,000 tuition requires roughly £18,000 + £13,761 = £31,761 in your bank account. The balance must be held for 28 consecutive days before you apply.

Can I work to cover costs while studying in the UK?+

Yes. UK student visa holders can work up to 20 hours per week during term time and full-time during scheduled holidays, provided your course is at a listed institution and at degree level or above. Part-time work typically earns £400–£700 per month depending on hours and location, which helps cover day-to-day expenses but won't come close to covering full tuition. See our part-time work rules guide for details.

Which UK cities are cheapest for international students?+

London is the most expensive city for students, with total monthly costs of £1,300–£1,800. Manchester, Birmingham, and Leeds are significantly cheaper at £900–£1,200 per month. Edinburgh sits in the middle at around £1,000–£1,300. Choosing a city outside London can save you £5,000–£8,000 per year on living costs alone, and many excellent universities are based in these more affordable cities.
