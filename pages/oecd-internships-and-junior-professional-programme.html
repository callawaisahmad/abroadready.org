OECD Internships & Junior Professional Programme

🗓️ Aug 5, 2026⏱️ 9 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

What is the OECD and why do OECD internships matter?

The OECD offers two distinct ways to build an international career in economic and social policy: a well-known internship programme and a more advanced entry route called the Junior Professional Programme (JPP). Both are open to candidates of all nationalities, which makes them genuinely different from many government organisations, and both are funded. The OECD, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, is based in Paris and works on policy across nearly forty member countries plus many partner economies, so interns and junior professionals join a genuinely global policy environment.

Understanding the organisation before you apply makes everything else easier. The OECD is an intergovernmental forum, not a university, a think tank or a trade association. Its members are governments, and its output exists to help those governments make better decisions. That single fact shapes everything about OECD internships: the work is applied rather than purely academic, the deadlines follow policy calendars rather than semester calendars, and interns are treated as contributors to real deliverables rather than as visitors observing from the outside. If you understand that, the rest of this guide answers questions you can answer more honestly in your application.

What does this guide cover?

This guide explains how OECD internships work, what the Junior Professional Programme is, who is eligible for each, and how to make your application competitive. It also covers what the stipend actually pays for, how the two routes differ, what life in Paris costs in practice, when the deadlines fall, and where an OECD placement can take you afterwards. Read it end to end if you are starting from scratch, or jump to the question that matches the stage you are at: eligibility, application, funding, logistics or career planning.

Key takeaways

  • OECD internships are open to candidates of all nationalities who are enrolled in a degree or have recently graduated.
  • Interns receive a monthly allowance designed to cover living costs in Paris.
  • The Junior Professional Programme is a paid two-year fixed-term role for recent graduates, open to candidates of all OECD member and partner countries.
  • Both routes are based mainly in Paris, though some assignments are hybrid or in field offices.
  • Applications go through oecd.org/careers, and competitive candidates show strong analytical and language skills.

What should you remember most about OECD internships?

If you remember nothing else, remember these six points. OECD internships are open on the basis of enrolment or recent graduation rather than citizenship, and the Junior Professional Programme is open to nationals of all member and partner countries. Both are funded, both are based in Paris, and both run through the OECD careers portal. Because demand is high, the difference between a rejected and a successful OECD internships application is rarely the qualification on your transcript; it is how specifically you connect your skills to a directorate's work.

How OECD internships work

Where do OECD interns actually work?

The OECD internship programme places students in its policy directorates, where they contribute to research, analysis, event support and communications. Interns work with experienced analysts on projects that feed into real OECD output, such as country reviews, working papers and statistical reports. You are not placed in a generic pool and rotated at random. You are matched, or you apply, to a specific team with a specific mandate, and your work sits inside that team's deliverables from the first week.

What will you actually produce during OECD internships?

Typically the work is a mix of data work, drafting and coordination. In the Economics department, an intern might assist with data collection and analysis for flagship publications such as the OECD Economic Outlook. In the Education and Skills directorate, you might help prepare survey instruments or analyse results from the Programme for International Student Assessment. In the Environment directorate, you might contribute to policy reviews or draft sections of working papers on climate policy. Expect spreadsheets, draft text, chart production, literature summaries, meeting support and the occasional literature review. Expect to be asked to do something nobody has done before and to show the team how you solved it.

How long does an OECD internship last?

Assignments typically run from one to six months and can sometimes be renewed, with many interns joining for three months over the summer. Most placements fall in the two-to-six-month range, with three to four months the most common duration. If you need a longer stint, say clearly on your application that you are available for a longer period and be flexible about start and end dates, because matching length to team needs is one of the most common reasons a strong candidate is placed in a different window than hoped.

Who is eligible for OECD internships?

Eligibility is broad. You need to be enrolled in a full-time degree programme or to have graduated within the past two years, and you should have a good command of one of the OECD's working languages, most often English, with French a strong advantage. There is no age limit and no citizenship requirement. The practical baseline, then, is student status or very recent graduation, a working English, and the availability to be in Paris for the length of the placement.

Is the OECD internship paid?

How much is the OECD internship allowance?

Yes, OECD interns are paid. Interns receive a monthly allowance intended to cover the cost of living in Paris, which is widely reported to be around one thousand euros per month, though you should confirm the current figure on the official programme page. The amount is normally reviewed periodically, so treat any number you read as indicative rather than guaranteed and check the posting or your offer letter for the figure that applies to your intake.

Does the OECD internship allowance cover rent, flights and everything else?

No. Interns are expected to arrange their own accommodation and travel, so the allowance is a contribution rather than a full salary. Budget the difference carefully before you accept, because Paris rents, international flights and visa fees will not be covered. The later sections on living in Paris set out typical housing costs and transport fares so you can model your own budget rather than discovering the gap after you have said yes.

Does an OECD internship lead to a job offer?

Not automatically. You are not an employee of the OECD, and the internship does not lead to a staff contract by itself, though it is an excellent entry signal. Plenty of OECD interns do convert, and the relationships you build during a placement often open doors later, but you should treat an internship as training and exposure rather than as a job offer in disguise. The route into permanent employment is the Junior Professional Programme, which is a separate application to a separate set of posts.

What is the Junior Professional Programme?

Is the JPP a job or another placement?

The Junior Professional Programme is the OECD's structured route for recent graduates who want to work at the organisation. It is a paid, two-year fixed-term appointment at a junior analyst level, and JPP participants carry real responsibility from the start, contributing to directorate work, country reviews and policy research. The salary is a proper OECD salary with benefits, not an intern allowance. Where an intern supports a team, a junior professional is treated as part of the team, with an assigned work programme and a supervisor accountable for your development.

Who can apply to the Junior Professional Programme?

The programme is open to candidates from all OECD member countries and partner economies, and to both university graduates and those with a few years of early-career experience. In practice that means a completed degree plus some relevant professional exposure, and the ability to commit to a full-time role in Paris. Participants are expected to relocate to Paris unless the assignment is elsewhere, so the eligibility test is as much about logistics as it is about paper qualifications.

How competitive is selection for the JPP?

Selection is competitive and usually involves an online assessment and interviews. Because the positions are paid at professional salary levels and offer genuine career progression, the applicant pool is strong and the process is longer and more demanding than internship recruitment. The JPP is essentially a recruitment pipeline: successful participants often transition into regular OECD positions or move into other international organisations with the experience and credentials gained. Treat it as a professional job application, because that is what it is.

How the application process works

Both routes are managed through the OECD careers website at oecd.org/careers, and the process for each is different. Understanding which system you are entering before you start writing saves a lot of wasted effort, because the tone, the length and the evidence you present all change depending on whether you are chasing a three-month placement or a two-year professional appointment.

How do I apply for OECD internships?

Internship vacancies are posted on the OECD careers site, often with rolling deadlines. Create a profile, upload a tailored CV and cover letter, and apply to specific openings rather than submitting a general application. Because demand for OECD internships is high, applications that connect the applicant's coursework to a specific directorate's priorities stand out. Read the vacancy description line by line and mirror its language about outputs and skills in your cover letter, because the people reading it are usually the people who will supervise you.

How do I apply to the Junior Professional Programme?

The Junior Professional Programme has its own recruitment cycle, with applications opening for a defined period each year. The process typically includes an online application with motivation questions, a technical assessment, and structured interviews. Prepare by studying the OECD's current priorities, from climate and digitalisation to education and employment, and be ready to discuss how your skills map to real OECD projects. The motivation questions carry more weight than candidates expect, so write full answers rather than two-line summaries and show specific knowledge of the work rather than general admiration for the organisation.

Making your application competitive

How do I show analytical ability in an OECD internships application?

The OECD works in policy analysis, so your application needs to demonstrate analytical writing. Highlight coursework, theses or prior work where you handled data, wrote policy briefs or produced research reports, and name the relevant OECD work streams you would like to contribute to. Language skills matter, so list every language you can work in, not just the ones you are fluent in. Panels are looking for evidence that you can take an ambiguous question, work through data and come back with something a decision maker could act on.

What should my OECD cover letter say?

Your cover letter should read like a short statement of purpose, explaining why the OECD, why this team and why you. Our guide to writing a winning statement of purpose walks through the same structure, and our advice on building a competitive profile applies directly to international applications like this one. Keep it to the length the vacancy asks for, front-load your strongest evidence, and close with something specific about the directorate you have researched rather than a generic statement about your enthusiasm.

Practical considerations

Can I afford to live in Paris on an OECD stipend?

Budget carefully before accepting, because Paris is one of the most expensive cities in Europe and the allowance is a contribution rather than a wage. Start your housing search and your visa research early, and if you need a visa you should begin long before the start date. The OECD can provide documentation to support visa applications, but the responsibility for obtaining a visa sits with you. For a general walkthrough, see our student visa guide.

What if I am not selected the first time?

Both the internship and the JPP reward persistence. The OECD receives thousands of applications for a limited number of places, so it is normal not to be selected on the first attempt, and many successful candidates applied across several cycles. Treat a rejection as feedback: ask which part of your evidence was thin, strengthen that area, and apply again in the next window rather than abandoning the organisation entirely.

Identify two or three directorates that fit your interests, tailor your CV and cover letter to each, and apply early in the cycle. If you meet the JPP eligibility window, prepare your application materials well before the deadline and practise for the assessment. Early applications are not automatically favoured, but they give you time to fix a weak draft before the window closes.

What should I do while I wait for a response?

What you actually do as an OECD intern day to day

What does a typical week of OECD internships look like?

Understanding the day-to-day work of an OECD intern helps you decide whether the programme fits your career goals and gives you material for a more convincing application. OECD internships are placed within specific directorates or divisions, and the work varies significantly depending on which team hosts you. A common week mixes a day or two of focused analytical work, a day on drafting or editing, a day on data and chart production, and regular team meetings where you present progress and take direction. Event support and communications tasks often fill the gaps, particularly during the weeks around a flagship launch, a ministerial meeting or a public debate.

Is the work substantive or just administrative?

The common thread across all placements is policy relevance. The OECD does not conduct purely academic research; everything it produces is intended to inform government policy across member and partner countries. As an intern, you will typically be assigned a specific project or a set of tasks within an ongoing project, supervised by a professional staff member. The work is real and contributes to published outputs. You are not fetching coffee or filing papers. Interns are expected to produce analysis, draft text and contribute meaningfully to the team's workload, and a strong piece of your analysis can end up in a report that ministers read.

What is the pace and culture like?

The pace is professional and expectations are high, but the environment is also supportive. Interns are included in team meetings, invited to seminars and lectures, and encouraged to attend internal events. The working language is English or French depending on the division, and many teams operate bilingually. If you are fluent in both languages, that is a significant advantage. Feedback is usually direct, which is uncomfortable at first and useful later, so ask for it early and often rather than waiting until a deliverable is already finished.

How does the work differ between quantitative and policy placements?

The experience varies by division. Economics and finance placements tend to be highly quantitative, involving econometric analysis, data manipulation and statistical modelling. Policy-oriented placements in areas such as education, health or environment may involve more writing, literature review and stakeholder consultation. Technology and data science placements may involve programming, data visualisation and database management. Regardless of the specific tasks, the common expectation is that you produce work of professional quality that your team can use, and that you hand it over in a form a colleague can pick up without asking you a question.

How is an OECD internship different from the JPP?

The Junior Professional Programme and the internship programme serve different purposes and target candidates at different stages, and confusing the two is a common mistake. The internship programme is aimed at current students or recent graduates who are still building their professional skills. It is a training and exposure opportunity, typically lasting a few months, and it is open to a wide range of candidates with appropriate academic backgrounds. There is no expectation that you already know policy, and no expectation that you will stay.

Is the JPP a more advanced route into OECD careers?

The Junior Professional Programme, by contrast, is a more advanced entry point designed for candidates who already hold a graduate degree and have some professional experience. JPP positions are regular staff appointments, not temporary placements. Junior professionals are hired on fixed-term contracts, typically for one to two years, and they perform the same work as other professional staff members. The JPP is essentially a recruitment pipeline: successful participants often transition into regular OECD positions or move into other international organisations with the experience and credentials gained.

How does JPP selection compare with internship selection?

The selection process for the JPP is more rigorous than for OECD internships. It typically involves a written application, a technical assessment or test, and one or more interviews. Competition is intense because the positions are paid at professional salary levels and offer genuine career progression. At the time of writing, JPP salaries are competitive with entry-level positions in other international organisations, and the programme includes benefits such as health insurance and paid leave. Expect more rounds, more preparation and a longer wait from submission to decision.

Which route should I choose?

For students deciding between the two, the internship is the right choice if you are still completing your degree or have recently graduated and want to explore international policy work. The JPP is the right choice if you have a master's degree or equivalent, at least one to two years of relevant professional experience, and you are ready to commit to a full professional role at an international organisation. Both programmes are valuable, but they lead to different outcomes and require different levels of preparation. An OECD internship can strengthen a future JPP application significantly, so if the JPP is your long-term goal, consider the internship as a strategic stepping stone rather than a separate choice.

Building a competitive OECD application: what selection panels look for

OECD selection panels assess applicants on a combination of academic quality, relevant skills, motivation and alignment with the organisation's mission. Understanding what panels prioritise helps you allocate effort in your application materials and present yourself in the strongest possible light. In practice, the panels rank candidates on the same four axes for every post, whether the post is an internship or a JPP vacancy, which means the same checklist works for both.

What does the OECD look for in an internship application?

Academic quality is the baseline requirement. The OECD recruits from strong universities worldwide, and your transcripts, references and any publications or research outputs will be scrutinised. However, academic excellence alone is not sufficient. The OECD values practical skills: quantitative analysis, policy writing, data visualisation and the ability to synthesise complex information into clear, actionable summaries. If you have experience with statistical software such as Stata, R, Python or SAS, mention it prominently. If you have policy writing experience, such as briefing notes, policy briefs or government reports, highlight that as well. A candidate who can do both the analysis and the writing is rare, and panels notice.

Why does motivation and mission alignment matter so much?

Motivation and mission alignment matter enormously. The OECD is a policy organisation, not a university or a think tank, and it serves a specific constituency: governments of member and partner countries. Your application should demonstrate that you understand this role and are motivated by the prospect of contributing to evidence-based policy. Avoid framing your motivation purely in terms of personal career development. Instead, connect your interests to the OECD's current priority areas, which at the time of writing include productivity, digital transformation, green growth, inequality and skills.

Do language skills really tip the balance for OECD internships?

Language skills are a significant advantage. English is the working language of most divisions, but French is also widely used, particularly in governance and legal affairs. Bilingual candidates are strongly preferred for many positions. If you are proficient in additional languages, especially those spoken in OECD member or partner countries, include that information in your application. Language proficiency is not just a communication tool; it reflects cultural awareness and the ability to work across linguistic boundaries, which is central to the OECD's collaborative model. Being multilingual also widens the directorates you can be placed in, because a bilingual intern can be useful in meetings rather than only in drafting.

What documents do you need for OECD internships?

Finally, attention to detail in the application itself signals professionalism. The OECD receives applications from highly qualified candidates worldwide, and small errors, formatting inconsistencies or incomplete sections can result in immediate rejection. Proofread everything carefully, follow the application instructions exactly, and ensure that your supporting documents are complete and in the requested format. A polished application demonstrates the precision and thoroughness that the OECD expects in its professional work, and it removes any doubt about whether you can hold the same standard on a policy output.

Practical logistics: living in Paris as an OECD intern

If you are selected for an OECD internship or JPP placement, you will spend your time at the OECD headquarters in Paris, France. Paris is one of the most expensive cities in Europe, so understanding the practical costs and logistics before you arrive is essential for a smooth experience. Most of the difficulty people encounter in OECD internships is not academic; it is administrative, financial and logistical, and almost all of it is manageable if you start early.

Where will I be based, and can I work remotely?

Your base is OECD headquarters in Paris, on the edge of the 16th arrondissement, with most teams working in offices a short walk from the Seine and the metro. Some assignments are hybrid, particularly in roles that involve heavy data work or writing, and a small number of postings are tied to field offices or regional activities. If remote or hybrid working matters to you, read the working arrangements in the vacancy rather than assuming, and ask at interview stage, because policies differ between directorates.

Does the OECD provide housing for interns?

The OECD does not provide accommodation for interns, though it may offer assistance in finding housing through its housing information service. Many interns find accommodation through university housing networks, shared apartments or temporary rental platforms. The cost of a shared room in Paris typically ranges from 600 to 900 euros per month depending on the neighbourhood and the type of accommodation. Living further from the centre reduces costs but increases commute time, so weigh the trade-off carefully, and remember to ask for a Guaranteed Standard Student Housing contract if you are under 25, since French law caps how much landlords can charge students in purpose-built student accommodation.

What does OECD internships funding actually cover day to day?

The OECD internship stipend, at the time of writing, is modest relative to the cost of living in Paris. It is intended to cover basic expenses rather than provide a comfortable income. Budget carefully from the start: food, transport, insurance and personal expenses add up quickly. France has a strong and affordable public transport system, and a monthly Navigo pass covering the Paris region costs roughly 86 euros. Many interns cook at home rather than eating out regularly, and student-priced meals are available at university canteens if you register. If your accommodation is at the top of the price range, you will need a personal buffer on top of the stipend.

What health insurance do I need in France?

Health insurance is required for your stay in France. The OECD may provide or facilitate coverage, but verify the specifics in your offer letter and arrange supplementary insurance if needed. EU citizens may be covered through their national health system; non-EU citizens should ensure they have adequate coverage before arriving. The French health system is efficient but navigating it in a foreign language can be daunting, so ask the OECD HR team or other interns for guidance during your first week, and keep copies of any forms you submit.

Can you still enjoy Paris on a student budget?

Paris itself is a remarkable place to spend a few months. The city's cultural, intellectual and social life is extraordinary, and even on a tight budget you can enjoy museums, parks, libraries and neighbourhoods that make the experience genuinely enriching beyond the professional development. Many museums offer free entry on the first Sunday of each month, and the city's public parks and riverside spaces provide free recreation. Plan your finances realistically so that financial stress does not undermine what should be a formative professional experience, and remember that OECD interns usually get free or discounted access to the organisation's own events too.

OECD internship and JPP application timelines and key deadlines

How do OECD internship deadlines work?

The OECD operates on a rolling or cyclical application system depending on the programme, and knowing the timing is crucial for planning. The internship programme typically accepts applications multiple times per year, with intake periods often aligned to quarterly start dates. At the time of writing, the main application windows open approximately three to four months before each intake period, though the exact dates vary and are published on the OECD careers portal. Rolling deadlines do not mean casual deadlines: a vacancy closes when it is filled, and the strongest applications often arrive in the first weeks.

When do Junior Professional Programme vacancies appear?

The Junior Professional Programme follows a different cycle. JPP positions are posted when they become available, which may be once or twice per year depending on the division and budget. JPP applications typically close within a few weeks of posting, and the selection process can take several months from submission to offer. Monitor the OECD careers page regularly and set alerts for new postings in your area of interest, because a window of a few weeks is easy to miss if you are not checking.

What documents do both programmes require?

For both programmes, the application requires a CV, a cover letter, academic transcripts and sometimes a writing sample or technical assessment. Prepare these documents well in advance so that when a posting appears, you can apply quickly without rushing. Tailor your cover letter to the specific division or position; generic applications are rarely successful because the OECD receives hundreds of strong applications for each opening. Keep master versions of your documents in a single folder so that tailoring takes minutes rather than an evening.

When should I start preparing OECD internships materials?

A practical approach is to begin preparing your application materials six months before you intend to start an internship. Use that time to strengthen any skill gaps, such as improving your statistical software proficiency, taking a short online course in policy analysis or economics, or building your writing portfolio with policy-relevant pieces. The stronger your skills at the point of application, the higher your chances of selection. Six months is not arbitrary; it is enough time to complete a course, produce two or three portfolio pieces and write a draft you are willing to submit.

What OECD internship interview questions should you prepare for?

After submitting your application, the OECD typically responds within four to eight weeks for internships and longer for JPP positions. If you are shortlisted, expect one or two rounds of interviews, which may be conducted online. Prepare for technical questions related to your field, as well as behavioural questions about your motivation, teamwork skills and ability to work in a multicultural environment. The OECD values candidates who can demonstrate both technical competence and the interpersonal skills needed to thrive in an international policy environment. Prepare a two-minute version of your own work, because being able to describe your process, not just your conclusion, is usually what separates the candidates at this stage.

What OECD internships pay and how the stipend compares

Are OECD internships still unpaid?

The funding question is the first thing most students ask about OECD internships, and the honest answer is that the picture has changed. For many years OECD internships were unpaid, which made them inaccessible to students who could not self-fund a stay in Paris. At the time of writing, the OECD offers a paid internship programme with a monthly stipend, though the amount is modest relative to the cost of living in Paris. The stipend is intended to cover basic living costs for a student, and it is typically adjusted annually, so confirm the current figure before you make financial plans.

Is the OECD stipend enough to live on?

It is important to set expectations correctly. The OECD internship stipend is not a salary and it is not designed to produce savings. It covers rent in shared accommodation, food, transport and basic expenses, but students who plan comfortable travel or expensive hobbies will need personal resources. In practice, many interns find that the stipend covers most daily costs if they budget carefully, but that an additional personal buffer is helpful for the first weeks before the first payment arrives. Ask your university for emergency funds, hardship support or a departmental travel bursary, because most universities will fund an unpaid or low-paid overseas placement if the placement is academically credited.

How does the OECD stipend compare with other international internships?

Compare this against other internship options in international organisations. UN agencies, which moved to paid internships in recent years, offer stipends that also vary by location and agency. The World Bank and IMF pay substantially more for their internship programmes. The European Commission's traineeship scheme, commonly known as the Blue Book, pays a monthly grant that is higher than the OECD stipend in most cases. Understanding this landscape helps you decide whether the OECD internship is the right financial fit for your situation, and it is worth listing your other options before you commit, especially if you have a competing offer with a higher stipend and a comparable policy focus.

What is OECD internships funding in the context of the JPP?

For the Junior Professional Programme, the financial picture is different and more favourable. JPP positions are paid at professional salary levels, which at the time of writing are competitive with entry-level positions in other international organisations. Benefits typically include health insurance, paid leave and contributions toward pension or retirement arrangements depending on the contract. This is why the JPP is a genuine career entry point rather than a training placement: it compensates you at a professional level for professional work, and the financial comparison with an internship stipend is stark.

Which route is the better financial fit for me?

The practical implication for students is a straightforward division. The OECD internship is the right choice when you are still building skills and can accept a modest stipend in exchange for world-class experience and networking. The JPP is the right choice when you have the graduate qualification and professional experience to step directly into a compensated professional role. Budget your Paris life accordingly whichever programme you pursue, and confirm the exact financial terms in your offer letter before committing. Neither route is a financial decision, but both have real financial consequences, so do not let the prestige of the name do the maths for you.

Career paths after an OECD internship or JPP placement

What can I do after an OECD placement?

The value of an OECD internship or Junior Professional Programme placement extends far beyond the months you spend in Paris, and understanding the career options that follow helps you choose the programme strategically and make the most of your time there. Both routes are recognised credentials across the international policy world, and alumni of both programmes work in government, academia, multilateral institutions, consulting, the private sector and civil society. The most common destinations are national government departments, central banks, research institutes and international consultancies, and the OECD name carries weight in all of them.

Does an internship lead to a job at the OECD?

Within the OECD itself, the internship is a proven pathway into professional employment. Many current OECD analysts and policy advisors completed an internship earlier in their careers, and the relationships built during an internship often lead to later job offers. The JPP is an even more direct route into the OECD's professional workforce: a successful JPP placement frequently converts into a regular staff appointment, and the fixed-term contract is designed with that conversion in mind. Keep your supervisor's contact details, ask for a reference before you leave, and stay on the OECD alumni list so that you are considered when a post opens.

Does an OECD credential carry weight outside the organisation?

Beyond the OECD, the experience carries weight with other international organisations. Multilateral bodies, development banks, United Nations agencies and regional organisations all recruit from the OECD alumni pool, and the policy analysis and writing skills you develop are directly transferable. For students from Pakistan and other developing countries, an OECD credential also strengthens candidacies with national governments, central banks, research institutes and international consultancies that work on economic and social policy. In many hiring processes, an OECD placement counts as substantive professional experience, which matters because employers often discount purely academic CVs.

Which skills do OECD alumni actually gain?

The skills you develop are the real asset. OECD interns and junior professionals learn to work in a multicultural, multilingual environment; to synthesise complex data into policy-relevant analysis; and to communicate research findings in formats that decision makers actually use. These capabilities are rare and valuable. When you apply for graduate programmes, your OECD experience differentiates you from applicants with purely academic backgrounds and provides concrete material for your statements and interviews. Think of the placement as a conversion of your academic training into applied competence, and describe the conversion in those terms when you write about it.

How do I get the most out of my placement?

To maximise the career benefit, be intentional during your placement. Attend the OECD's knowledge events and seminars, build relationships with senior analysts in fields that interest you, and seek feedback on your work. Document your projects and outputs carefully so you can point to concrete achievements in future applications. Maintain contact with supervisors and colleagues after you leave, and do not hesitate to ask for references when the time comes. An OECD placement is a career accelerator, but only if you approach it with deliberate strategy rather than passive participation.

Does teamwork experience strengthen an OECD internship application?

One area that many applicants underestimate is the importance of demonstrating teamwork and cross-cultural competence. The OECD is a multilateral organisation where staff work across teams, countries and cultures daily. If your application can show evidence of successful collaboration in diverse settings, you will stand out from candidates who emphasise only individual achievements. Group projects, cross-cultural research and international experience all count. Describe your contribution specifically, naming what the team achieved and what part of that you owned, because a vague claim of teamwork carries no weight next to a specific one.

Monitor the portal regularly and set alerts for new postings in your area of interest. In short, plan around intake dates for internships and around posting dates for JPP vacancies, because the two behave very differently.

What are the OECD internships 2026 deadlines like?

A practical approach is to begin preparing your application materials six months before you intend to start an internship. Use that time to strengthen any skill gaps: improve your statistical software proficiency, take a short online course in policy analysis, or build your writing portfolio with policy-relevant pieces. The stronger your skills at the point of application, the higher your chances of selection. Confirm timelines in the posting itself, as they vary by division and intake period, and treat any third-party summary of OECD internship deadlines as a starting point rather than a source of truth.

What should I have ready when a JPP posting appears?

Junior Professional Programme positions are posted when they become available, which may be once or twice per year depending on the division and budget. JPP applications typically close within a few weeks of posting, and the selection process can take several months from submission to offer. Prepare your materials in advance so you can apply within days of a posting appearing. That means a CV kept current month by month, a cover letter skeleton with the OECD-specific motivation paragraph already written, and your academic transcripts downloaded in the format the portal accepts.

Do I need a writing sample or technical assessment?

For both programmes, the application usually requires a CV, a cover letter, academic transcripts and sometimes a writing sample or technical assessment. Prepare these documents well in advance so that when a posting appears, you can apply quickly without rushing. Tailor your cover letter to the specific division or position. Generic applications are rarely successful because the OECD receives hundreds of strong applications for each opening. If a writing sample is requested, write it as a two-page policy brief with a recommendation, not as an essay, because that is the format the work actually takes.

What is the long-term value of an OECD internship?

The value of an OECD internship or Junior Professional Programme placement extends far beyond the months you spend in Paris. Both routes are recognised credentials across the international policy world, and alumni work in government, academia, multilateral institutions, consulting and civil society. Within the OECD itself, the internship is a proven pathway into professional employment, since many current analysts completed an internship earlier in their careers and the relationships built during an internship often lead to later job offers. The JPP is an even more direct route into the professional workforce, as a successful placement frequently converts into a regular staff appointment.

Where do OECD alumni go, and what do they take with them?

Beyond the OECD, the experience carries weight with other international organisations. Multilateral bodies, development banks, United Nations agencies and regional organisations all recruit from the OECD alumni pool. For students from Pakistan and other developing countries, an OECD credential strengthens candidacies with national governments, central banks, research institutes and international consultancies. The skills you develop are the real asset: OECD interns and junior professionals learn to work in a multicultural, multilingual environment, to synthesise complex data into policy-relevant analysis, and to communicate research findings in formats that decision makers actually use. These capabilities are rare and valuable in the job market, and they are what you should describe in interviews rather than the job title itself.

How should I use my placement strategically?

To maximise the career benefit, be intentional during your placement. Attend knowledge events, build relationships with senior analysts, and seek feedback on your work. Document your projects carefully and maintain contact with supervisors after you leave. An OECD placement is a career accelerator, but only if you approach it with deliberate strategy. Treat the placement as a project you are running rather than an experience you are having: set your own objectives at the start, ask what success looks like in your team, and review against them at the end so that you leave with evidence rather than impressions.

OECD internships across different policy directorates

Which policy directorates can host OECD internships?

The OECD is organised into multiple policy directorates, each focusing on a different area of economic and social policy, and the nature of your internship varies significantly depending on which directorate hosts you. Understanding the different directorates helps you target your application to the area that best matches your skills and interests. Each directorate has its own vocabulary, its own publication calendar and its own idea of what a strong intern contributes, so the more precisely you can name the team you want, the more credible your application sounds.

What does the Economics department work on?

The Economics department produces flagship publications such as the OECD Economic Outlook and the Going for Growth report. Interns in this directorate work with economists on data analysis, econometric modelling and the preparation of policy documents. If you have strong quantitative skills and a background in macroeconomics or applied economics, this directorate offers rigorous and rewarding work. Expect heavy use of statistical software, tight modelling deadlines tied to the Outlook release cycle, and a lot of attention to how a chart or a scenario will be read by a policy maker.

What work is available in Education and Skills?

The Education and Skills directorate focuses on education policy, skills development and the Programme for International Student Assessment. Interns here may help with survey analysis, policy reviews or comparative education research. Survey work means cleaning imperfect data, comparing education systems across dozens of countries and writing the short summaries that end up in the tables. It suits candidates who are comfortable with both quantitative work and careful descriptive writing, and who want their placement to sit close to a sector they care about.

What does the Environment directorate cover?

The Environment directorate works on climate policy, biodiversity, green growth and environmental regulation. Interns contribute to environmental performance reviews, work on sustainability indicators and help prepare policy recommendations for member governments. Country reviews follow a predictable structure, which means interns quickly learn to assemble evidence, draft findings and support recommendations with citations. The work is often fast when a review deadline lands, and the output is aimed squarely at helping governments implement commitments they have already made.

What is the Trade and Agriculture directorate like?

The Trade and Agriculture directorate focuses on international trade policy, agricultural subsidies and market access, and offers opportunities for students with backgrounds in economics, law or political science. Tasks range from tariff data analysis and market monitoring to legal summaries of trade agreements and preparation for negotiating rounds. If you are coming from a law or political science background without heavy quantitative training, this is often the most accessible entry point into OECD internships, provided you can demonstrate careful reading and precise drafting.

What about the Directorate for Science, Technology and Innovation?

The Directorate for Science, Technology and Innovation works on innovation policy, digital transformation and intellectual property. Interns here may work on technology assessments, innovation surveys or policy analysis related to artificial intelligence and data governance. The tooling is often more technical than elsewhere in the organisation, and the topics move quickly, so an intern who can work with data pipelines and produce clear visualisations tends to be in demand. The regulatory questions are also live, which means your analysis can have consequences well beyond the organisation.

Do OECD internships vary a lot by directorate?

Each directorate has its own culture, pace and focus, so research the specific directorate you are applying to and tailor your application accordingly. Read the directorate's recent publications before you interview, and be able to name one piece of work you found genuinely interesting and explain why. Regardless of the directorate, the common thread is policy relevance. Every OECD intern contributes to work that directly informs government decisions, and the quality of your analysis and writing must meet the standards of a publication that will be read by policy makers around the world. The OECD internship is not a passive learning experience: it is an opportunity to contribute meaningfully to global policy while developing skills that will serve you throughout your career.

Frequently asked questions

Who can apply for an OECD internship?+

Students enrolled in a full-time degree programme or graduates within the past two years, of any nationality, with a good command of an OECD working language such as English.

Is the OECD internship paid?+

Yes. Interns receive a monthly allowance intended to cover living costs in Paris; confirm the current amount on the official programme page.

What is the OECD Junior Professional Programme?+

It is a paid, two-year fixed-term junior analyst role open to recent graduates from OECD member and partner countries, run through the OECD careers site.

How do I apply to the OECD?+

Applications go through oecd.org/careers, with internship vacancies posted throughout the year and the JPP running its own annual recruitment cycle.

Do OECD internships lead to permanent jobs?+

Not automatically, but they build strong networks and policy skills, and JPP roles are a recognised entry route into the organisation.
