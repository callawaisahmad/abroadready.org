Summer Study Abroad Programs 2026

🗓️ Aug 5, 2026⏱️ 9 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

Summer study abroad programs are the fastest-growing corner of international education and the easiest to fit into a US degree. Instead of giving up a semester, you spend three to eight weeks abroad, usually earn transferable college credit, and return in time for fall classes. Because they are shorter, they cost a fraction of a full term and they stay open to students who cannot leave campus for an entire semester.

What is a summer study abroad program?

A summer study abroad program is a short, credit-bearing course of study run outside the United States during the summer break, usually three to eight weeks long. You enrol with a US university, a specialist provider or a foreign university, you take classes (often alongside a field placement, language immersion or research project), and you come home with credits your home institution has already agreed to accept. This guide covers how these programs work, how the credit transfers, what they really cost, and which funding can cover them.

How long is a summer study abroad program, and how much credit do you earn?

Lengths run from a single intensive week to eight full weeks, but the sweet spot for most students is three to six weeks. Shorter programs are cheaper and easier to combine with a job or internship; longer programs unlock bigger credit loads and deeper language gains. A typical three-week course awards three US credits, a six-week course usually awards six, and an eight-week intensive can award six to nine. Every one of those credit amounts has to be approved by your home university in advance, which is why the transfer conversation comes before the shopping conversation.

Is a summer abroad cheaper than a semester abroad?

Almost always. A summer abroad is a fraction of the price of a semester abroad because you are paying for accommodation and living costs for weeks rather than months, and because you keep your housing, your job and your routine at home. Where a semester abroad might run $18,000 to $30,000, a summer study abroad program usually lands between $3,000 and $8,000 all-in. That single fact is the reason summer programs have grown faster than any other segment of study abroad.

Key takeaways

  • Summer programs run 3 to 8 weeks and typically award 3 to 9 US college credits.
  • Three types exist: faculty-led, provider-run, and direct enrollment at a foreign university.
  • Credit transfers through pre-approval from your home study abroad office, so arrange it before you go.
  • Costs typically range from about $3,000 to $8,000 including tuition, housing and excursions.
  • Federal aid, Gilman summer cycles and provider grants all apply to summer study abroad.
  • Apply by late winter for the following summer; scholarships close even earlier.

What are the most important things to know before you apply?

Six things matter more than everything else combined: the length of the program, the type of program, the credit pre-approval, the true all-in price, the funding stack, and the deadline you are actually working against. Get those six right and the rest is logistics. Get any one of them wrong and you can end up with credits your university will not accept, or a bill you did not plan for, or a missed scholarship window that was open for exactly four weeks.

Why summer study abroad works

The biggest advantage is structural. A summer term does not delay your degree, so it works for students in rigid majors, student athletes, and anyone balancing work or internships.

Does a summer abroad delay my graduation?

No. Because the program runs inside the summer break, you return in time for fall classes on the same schedule as everybody else. Some students even use the summer to catch up on credits, which means a summer study abroad program can pull your graduation date forward rather than push it back. The only way it slows you down is if the credit fails to transfer, which is entirely avoidable with pre-approval.

Is summer study abroad good for first-time travelers?

It is the recommended first trip. Shorter programs carry lighter language requirements, smaller group sizes and far more hand-holding than a semester abroad, and the financial risk is capped because you are only out of pocket for a few weeks. Many students treat a summer course as the trial run for a longer exchange later, and study abroad offices usually count it toward first-time-abroad requirements.

What are the summer study abroad programs requirements?

The range of destinations is also wider than for a full semester. Summer terms run in big capital cities, small coastal towns, and everything in between, and because they are short, you can even pair two programs back to back in different countries in a single summer. Entry requirements are usually lighter too: a minimum GPA (often 2.5 or 3.0), a language level that matches the course, sometimes an application essay, and in some cases proof of a medical clearance. A few elite programmes ask for a portfolio or a writing sample, but the majority of summer study abroad programs want nothing more than a transcript and a passport.

Can I use financial aid for a summer program?

Yes, and this surprises people. Federal aid applies when you enrol in the required number of summer credits, Pell-eligible students can access the Gilman summer cycles, and provider grants and early-booking discounts cover much of the gap. Because most of that money would have been spent on campus living costs anyway, many students fund a summer abroad almost entirely with money they were going to spend regardless.

The three types of summer programs

What are the three types of summer study abroad programs?

There are exactly three: faculty-led programs run by your own university, provider programs run by specialist organisations, and direct enrollment where you join a foreign university yourself. All three can earn transferable credit, all three have different cost structures, and all three suit different students. The format you pick determines how much support you get, how much freedom you have, and how much you pay.

How do faculty-led programs work?

Your own department or university runs a two-to-six-week course with a professor you already know. Credit transfers cleanly because the course was designed for your catalogue, the group travels together, and the price is the lowest of the three routes at roughly $3,000 to $6,000 including travel. This is where most first-time study abroad students start, and it doubles as a chance to build a real relationship with a faculty mentor who can write you a recommendation later.

What do provider programs include?

CIEE, SIT Study Abroad, API Abroad, IES Abroad and Cultural Experiences Abroad all run summer terms in dozens of countries, from language and culture courses to environmental science and global health. You get housing, excursions, on-site staff, airport pickup and a transcript that usually transfers once your university approves the provider. Expect fees in the $4,000 to $8,000 range for four to six weeks, and expect the package to be all-inclusive rather than à la carte.

What does direct enrollment involve?

Many European and Asian universities run international summer schools you can join directly. German public universities keep summer fees very low, which fits neatly with our guide to studying in Germany for free. You handle the logistics yourself: applications, housing, visas, transport. In exchange the tuition is often the cheapest of all three routes, sometimes a few hundred euros for the whole course.

Which format should I choose?

Choose faculty-led if you want the lowest cost, the safest credit transfer and built-in academic support. Choose a provider if you want a specific city or subject that your own university does not teach, and you want the logistics handled. Choose direct enrollment if you are independent, comfortable with a foreign registration system, and hunting for the lowest tuition. Many students combine routes, taking provider coursework while arranging housing privately.

How college credit transfers

Never assume the credit will transfer. Get the syllabus approved by your home study abroad office and registrar before you pay a deposit, and request a detailed transcript from the provider or host university once the course is complete.

How do I make sure the credit will transfer?

Three things, in this order. First, get the syllabus or official course description approved in writing by your registrar or study abroad office. Second, confirm the credit value and the category it will satisfy, general elective versus a specific requirement. Third, request a detailed transcript from the provider or host university at the end of the program. Most summer courses convert to 3 to 9 US credits depending on contact hours, and a pre-approved course keeps your scholarship and aid eligibility intact. A pre-approved course is also the difference between a credit that appears on your transcript and a credit you end up paying for twice.

Do summer credits affect my financial aid or GPA?

A pre-approved course protects both. Because the home institution treats the abroad credit as credit earned at your own school, your enrolment status, your aid package and your graduation standing all continue uninterrupted. If you skip pre-approval, the credit can be treated as transfer credit from an unlisted institution, which sometimes means no aid, no GPA and no progress toward your degree.

Costs and how to fund them

A typical summer program runs $3,000 to $8,000 including tuition, housing and excursions, with flights and food on top. Provider fees are usually all-inclusive, bundling tuition, housing, some meals, excursions and insurance, so compare them against a faculty-led trip where you may pay for flights and activities separately. Federal aid can apply when you enrol in the required number of summer credits, Pell-eligible students can use Gilman's summer cycles, and the Fund for Education Abroad plus provider grants, including early-booking discounts, cover much of the gap. Stacking two or three awards is common, and our scholarship list tracks the deadlines.

How much does summer study abroad programs cost?

Break the price into five categories and the total becomes predictable. Programme fees are the headline figure and typically include tuition, housing, some excursions and on-site support; provider-run programmes run $4,000 for a three-week session to $8,000 or more for an eight-week intensive, while faculty-led programmes through your home university usually sit between $3,000 and $6,000 with the same components bundled in. Direct enrollment can be dramatically cheaper, because summer tuition at a German public university might cost less than 500 euros and a student dormitory runs 300 to 600 euros per month. Flights are the second largest cost: round-trip airfare to Europe in summer typically ranges from $800 to $1,500 depending on how early you book and which city you fly into, and flights to Asia, Latin America or Africa tend to cost more, though deals exist for students flexible on dates and airports. Personal expenses including food, local transport, phone plans and leisure are the third category, and budgeting $30 to $50 per day is reasonable for Western Europe, less for Eastern Europe and Latin America, and considerably more for expensive cities like London, Paris or Tokyo. A prepaid local SIM card for data and calls typically costs $15 to $30 for the duration of a summer programme. Visa fees are the fourth, and insurance the fifth.

What does summer study abroad programs cover?

Provider and faculty-led programmes usually bundle tuition, housing, a stated number of excursions, some meals, on-site staff and basic emergency coverage. What you almost always pay extra for is international airfare, optional excursion upgrades, meals outside the included plan, and a full international health insurance policy. Visa fees, if required, are a variable cost that depends on your destination: US citizens can enter most European countries for up to ninety days without a visa, which covers most summer programmes, while students going to the UK for more than six months need a Student Visitor visa costing approximately 189 British pounds at the time of writing. Always check the current fee and processing time for your specific destination well before you travel, because consulate processing times change without notice.

How should I compare an all-inclusive fee with a self-build budget?

Add up everything you would pay yourself and compare that number to the advertised provider price rather than comparing tuition to tuition. A provider fee that looks $1,500 higher often works out cheaper once you price a hotel for six weeks, airport transfers, three excursions, a SIM card, an insurance policy and the admin cost of a student visa application. Faculty-led trips are frequently cheaper on paper but push the flights, activities and housing logistics onto you.

What happens to my budget if the exchange rate moves?

The exchange rate between the US dollar and your destination country's currency affects your budget directly. If the dollar weakens against the euro while you are abroad, your purchasing power drops and a fixed-price programme fee can quietly become more expensive. Building a 5 to 10 percent buffer into your budget for currency fluctuations is prudent financial planning, and withdrawing cash in small amounts rather than one large lump reduces the pain of a bad rate.

You submit an application to the program, not to your home university, and the process is usually shorter than a semester abroad application. What is the summer study abroad programs visa process?

The visa process depends entirely on your nationality and your destination, and it is the single most common reason students miss a start date. Start the paperwork the week you are accepted. That means applying for a student visa or residence permit where one is required, gathering bank statements, enrolment letters and accommodation proof, booking the earliest available embassy appointment, and tracking processing times against your departure date. Summer embassy slots fill up fast because the whole northern-hemisphere summer rush competes for the same June and July appointments, and slots routinely run six to ten weeks out. If your programme is three weeks or shorter and your nationality allows visa-free short stays, you may not need a visa at all, but you should verify that on an official government source rather than a forum, and you should still check the maximum permitted length of stay.

Choosing Between Faculty-Led, Provider and Direct Enrollment Formats

The three main types of summer study abroad programmes differ in cost, independence and academic rigour, and choosing the right format depends on your budget, your academic goals and how comfortable you are navigating a foreign environment on your own.

How independent am I on each format?

On a faculty-led program you are the least independent: a professor sets the syllabus, grades the work, schedules the travel and guarantees the credit. On a provider program you are semi-independent, choosing courses from a catalogue and handling your own daily life with staff support nearby. On direct enrollment you are fully independent, applying, registering, housing and problem-solving entirely on your own. Match the format to your comfort, not to what sounds impressive on an application.

Which format is the best value for money?

Direct enrollment at a foreign university is the most independent and often the cheapest option. Tuition at European public universities can be very low, sometimes a few hundred euros for a summer course, and living costs outside major capital cities are manageable. The trade-off is that you are responsible for everything from finding accommodation to navigating public transport, and credit transfer depends entirely on your home university's willingness to accept foreign coursework.

Can I mix formats in one summer?

Yes, and combining formats is increasingly common. Some students enrol directly at a foreign university for coursework while arranging their own housing through a provider or privately. This hybrid approach can offer the academic authenticity of direct enrollment with some of the support infrastructure of a provider programme. Discussing this option with your study abroad office before committing ensures it meets your home university's requirements, and pairing two short programs in two countries in one summer is also possible because the calendar allows it.

How College Credit Transfers After a Summer Programme

Credit transfer is the most important practical question in summer study abroad, and it is the one that causes the most problems when it is not addressed before you leave. The basic principle is straightforward: your home university decides whether to accept the credits you earn abroad, and the decision is governed by your university's transfer credit policy, not by the programme you attend.

What documents do you need for summer study abroad programs?

Before enrolling in any summer programme, get written confirmation from your registrar's office or study abroad office that the credits will transfer. This usually involves submitting the programme's course syllabus or description for approval. Some universities have pre-approved lists of providers and partner institutions; if your chosen programme is on the list, credit transfer is essentially guaranteed. If it is not on the list, you will need to petition for approval, which is possible but requires documentation and lead time. Keep the approval email, the syllabus, the enrolment letter and the final transcript in one folder, because you will be asked for those same documents again if anything goes wrong after you return.

Does the type of credit matter as much as the number?

Yes, the type of credit matters as much as the number of credits. Some summer programmes offer credits that transfer as general electives, while others are mapped to specific requirements in your major. A summer course in marine biology offered by a provider in the Caribbean might transfer as a science elective, but it may not count toward a biology major requirement if the course content does not match your department's standards. Clarifying both the credit value and the category before you enrol prevents unpleasant surprises when you return.

Will the grade appear on my transcript?

Grades earned abroad may or may not appear on your home transcript. Some universities record the grade and count it toward your GPA; others record only the credit as pass or fail. This distinction matters if you are using summer study abroad to raise your GPA or to demonstrate proficiency in a subject for graduate school applications. Check with your registrar before you go.

Does the host university's accreditation matter?

It can decide the outcome. Credits from universities that are not recognised by your home country's accreditation body may not transfer, regardless of the quality of the education you received. Checking the accreditation status of your target institution through your university's registrar is a step that many students skip, sometimes with costly consequences. Do it before you pay a deposit, not after you return.

Funding Sources Specifically for Summer Study Abroad

Summer study abroad qualifies for several funding sources that students often assume are limited to semester-length programmes.

What funding and benefits does summer study abroad programs provide?

The Gilman Scholarship, the largest US federal study abroad award, explicitly covers summer programmes of at least three weeks. Students receiving Pell Grants can apply for up to $5,000 in Gilman funding, with an additional $3,000 for critical language study. Because the Gilman application pool for summer is smaller than for semester programmes, the odds of winning can be slightly better for summer applicants. The Fund for Education Abroad also funds summer programmes of at least four weeks, with awards of up to $10,000, and FEA particularly values applicants who plan to study in countries that are underrepresented in US study abroad statistics, which means destinations outside the traditional Western Europe circuit may strengthen your application.

Can I get a scholarship from the provider itself?

Provider-specific scholarships are another significant source of summer funding. IES Abroad, CIEE, ISA and SFS all run their own scholarship programmes with awards ranging from $1,000 to $5,000. These are often easier to win than national awards because the applicant pool is limited to students enrolled in that specific provider's programmes. Always check with your provider's financial aid office before assuming you cannot afford the programme, and ask about early-application discounts at the same time.

What should you write in your summer study abroad programs essays?

Write about a specific, unresolved question, not a generic statement about wanting to see the world. Strong summer essays name the subject you want to study, explain what you have already tried at home, and describe what you intend to do with the credit. University study abroad offices typically maintain a list of summer scholarships funded by alumni donations, departmental budgets and institutional partnerships. These are often small, $500 to $2,000, but they are frequently less competitive and require less paperwork than national awards. Applying for five or six small scholarships is a realistic way to raise $3,000 to $5,000 without writing a dozen lengthy essays.

Can my employer help pay for a summer program?

Possibly. Some employers also offer study abroad sponsorship or tuition assistance for employees pursuing education that enhances their professional skills. If you have a part-time job during the academic year, asking your employer about education benefits that might apply to summer study abroad is worth the conversation. Internships, especially with employers that have an international presence, sometimes carry a tuition-assistance clause that nobody advertises until you ask.

Summer Study Abroad Programs: Credit Formats and What They Mean for Your Degree

Summer programmes award credit in different formats, and understanding these formats helps you plan how the credits will fit into your degree requirements.

What is US credit and how much is it worth?

US college credits are the most common format for programmes run by US universities and providers. A typical three-week summer course awards three credits, while an eight-week programme may award six to nine credits. These credits are generally understood by US registrars and transfer relatively smoothly, which makes them the safest option if you want the credit to count toward your graduation timeline.

How do ECTS credits convert?

The European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS) is used by universities across Europe. One ECTS credit equals roughly half a US credit, so a course worth six ECTS credits translates to approximately three US credits. When enrolling at a European university directly, pay attention to the ECTS value and confirm with your home registrar how it converts before you enrol, not after.

What are contact hours, and do they count as credit?

Contact hours are sometimes used instead of credit hours, particularly for intensive language programmes. A programme offering 100 contact hours of instruction might or might not carry formal credit, depending on whether the provider has arranged accreditation. If credit transfer is important to you, ensure the programme offers credit through an accredited US institution or a recognised foreign university. A certificate of completion is not a substitute for credit, however valuable it looks on a CV.

Is a certificate of completion worth anything?

Some summer programmes offer certificates of completion rather than academic credit. These certificates may have value for your CV or for graduate school applications, but they will not count toward your degree. If earning transferable credit is a priority, choose a programme that awards credit from an accredited institution, not a certificate from a commercial provider.

Does lower-division versus upper-division credit change anything?

It can. Some summer courses transfer as introductory-level credit, which may not count toward major requirements for juniors and seniors. Confirming the level at which the credit will transfer before you enrol prevents frustration when you return, and it is a one-minute question for your advisor that can save you a whole wasted summer.

Making the Most of Your Summer Abroad Experience

A summer study abroad programme is short, typically three to eight weeks, which means every day counts. Making the most of the experience requires intentionality from the moment you arrive.

How do I immerse myself from day one?

Engage with the local culture from day one. Shop at local markets, eat at neighbourhood restaurants rather than tourist-oriented ones, use public transport instead of taxis and attend local events, festivals and free lectures. These interactions create the kind of cultural immersion that makes study abroad genuinely transformative, and they are the experiences that distinguish a meaningful summer abroad from an expensive vacation. The habit that helps most is learning the first twenty words of the local language before you land, because it changes how strangers respond to you.

Which summer study abroad programs tips actually work?

The tips that consistently work are unglamorous. Keep a daily journal, because it creates a personal record of your time abroad and it also provides material for future applications, essays and interviews. Scholarship applications, graduate school applications and job interviews all benefit from specific, reflective examples of international experience. Build relationships with local students and professionals by joining a university sports club, attending a meetup group or volunteering with a local organisation, because these relationships often lead to future opportunities, from study recommendations to job referrals to invitations to return. Manage your time carefully, since the temptation to travel every weekend is strong, but spending every weekend in a different city means you never develop a deep familiarity with your host city. Finally, be honest about the challenges: culture shock, homesickness and academic adjustment are normal even on a short programme, and addressing them through conversation with peers, mentors or counselling services is healthier than pretending they do not exist. A challenging experience is often the one that teaches you the most.

What practical prep should I finish before departure?

Sorting out these details before you leave prevents disruptions that can derail your experience. Health insurance is a non-negotiable requirement: most universities and providers require proof of international health insurance that covers medical treatment, emergency evacuation and repatriation, your domestic plan may not cover you abroad, and a short-term international policy typically costs $50 to $150. Notify your bank and credit card companies that you will be travelling, because cards that are not flagged for international use may be frozen when you try to make a purchase abroad; set up travel notification on every card and carry a backup card from a different issuer. Register with the US Embassy through the Smart Traveller Enrollment Programme (STEP), which takes five minutes, is free, and lets the embassy contact you in an emergency. Arrange your housing early, because if you are enrolling directly at a foreign university you will need to secure your own, and student dormitories, homestays and private rentals all fill up months before the summer session begins, so starting in January or February for a June or July programme is not too early. Finally, obtain an international driving permit if you plan to drive, carry copies of your passport and key documents both physically and digitally, and download offline maps and translation apps before you land.

Planning Your Course Selection to Maximise Degree Progress

The strategic value of a summer study abroad programme extends beyond the cultural experience; if you choose your courses carefully, a summer abroad can advance your degree progress significantly.

How do I choose courses that count toward my degree?

The key is selecting courses that satisfy requirements you would otherwise need to complete at your home university, which means you are earning credit toward your degree while gaining an international perspective that you would not get from a domestic course. Start by reviewing your degree audit and identifying the requirements that remain unfilled, whether they are general education courses, major electives or minor requirements. Then check which of those requirements could be satisfied by courses offered at your target study abroad destination. A summer course in environmental science at a marine biology station in the Caribbean might satisfy a science elective requirement, while a course in European history at a British university might satisfy a humanities requirement.

What is the payoff of planning my courses strategically?

The benefit is twofold: you complete a degree requirement in an enriching international setting, and you potentially free up space in your future semesters for more advanced coursework, independent research or an additional minor. Students who plan their summer study abroad courses strategically often find that they graduate on time or even early, despite spending a summer abroad, because the credits they earn replace courses they would otherwise have taken in a later semester. This kind of long-term academic planning, combined with the financial aid and scholarship strategies in this guide, makes summer study abroad not just an enriching experience but a sound investment in your degree.

Dealing with Credit Transfer Problems After You Return

Even when you have done everything right, credit transfer problems can arise after you return. The most common issue is a course that your home university refuses to accept, either because the content does not match any existing requirement or because the foreign institution's accreditation is not recognised by your registrar. If this happens, do not panic: there are several steps you can take to resolve the situation.

What do I do if a course is rejected?

The first step is to request a detailed syllabus or course description from the foreign university and submit it to your registrar along with a written appeal explaining why the course should be accepted. Many registrars have a formal appeals process, and a well-documented appeal that includes the syllabus, a comparison to a similar course at your home university and a statement from your department chair or academic advisor can be successful. If the appeal fails, the course may still count as a general elective, even if it does not satisfy a specific requirement. While this is less ideal than having the course satisfy a specific requirement, it still contributes to your total credit count and your graduation timeline. The second step is to explore whether you can petition your department to create a special requirement that the course satisfies, particularly if the course content is genuinely relevant to your major. Department chairs have some discretion in how requirements are interpreted, and a strong case that the foreign course covers similar material to a domestic equivalent can be persuasive.

What if I cannot fix the credit gap?

The third step, if all else fails, is to accept the loss and plan accordingly. If you need the credit to graduate on time, taking an additional course in a future semester or during a winter or summer session at your home university can fill the gap. The key is to address the problem immediately upon your return, rather than discovering it months later when you are applying for graduation. Keep copies of everything you submitted, because the appeal file is what makes the difference in a second review.

Summer Congestion: Why Early Application Pays Off

Summer study abroad programmes are the most popular and fastest-filling corner of international education, and submitting your application as early as possible is one of the highest-leverage moves you can make.

How far in advance should I apply?

Many programmes fill their cohorts eight to twelve months before the programme start date, because popular destinations and host institutions have limited capacity. The University of London's summer session, for example, regularly fills its most popular courses within weeks of opening enrolment, and field-based programmes in biology, ecology and environmental science often cap at fifteen to twenty students and close once the cohort is full. The summer programme application cycle typically begins twelve to eighteen months before the start date, so the strongest strategy is to research programmes in the autumn, apply in the late autumn or early winter, and finalise your funding and logistics in the spring.

What do I gain by applying early?

Early application gives you access to early-bird discounts, which several providers offer for applications submitted before a specific date, and it ensures that your housing choices are available, since the best student dorms and homestays are allocated on a first-come basis. From a financial aid perspective, applying early also means you can apply for scholarships that require proof of programme enrolment, and you will know your costs well in advance so you can budget accurately instead of discovering them in May.

How do I prepare for health and safety abroad?

Preparing for the health and safety aspects of summer study abroad is a responsibility that begins long before you step on the plane, and the students who prepare thoroughly are the ones who have the smoothest experiences. The first step is researching the health and safety situation in your destination country: government websites like the US State Department's country information pages and the travel advisories they publish provide current, reliable information about safety conditions, required vaccinations and local laws, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention provides destination-specific health guidance including recommended vaccines and precautions. Registering with your university's travel office or your study abroad provider's safety programme means you receive alerts and guidance if the security situation changes. Vaccinations, if required or recommended, must be completed at least several weeks before departure, because some vaccines require a series of doses spread over time. Endemic diseases in some summer destinations, including mosquito-borne illnesses in tropical regions, can be managed through precautions like insect repellent, protective clothing and, where appropriate, preventive medication. While in country, keeping your passport secure, remaining aware of your surroundings in unfamiliar areas and using reputable transportation options prevent most problems. Sharing your itinerary with family or friends at home and checking in regularly gives people at home the information they need to help if something goes wrong. A summer abroad is overwhelmingly safe for students who prepare reasonably and exercise commonsense caution, and the preparation described above is entirely sufficient for a memorable and trouble-free experience.

Frequently asked questions

Do summer study abroad programs give college credit?+

Yes. Most award 3 to 9 US credits. Get the syllabus pre-approved by your study abroad office and registrar to guarantee the credit transfers to your degree.

How much do summer programs cost?+

Typically $3,000 to $8,000 including tuition, housing and excursions, with flights and food on top. Federal aid, Gilman and provider grants can cover much of this.

Can I use federal financial aid in the summer?+

Yes, if the program is approved for credit by your home university and you remain enrolled in the required number of credits for the summer term.

Which providers are best for summer study abroad?+

CIEE, SIT, API Abroad, IES Abroad and Cultural Experiences Abroad all run large summer portfolios. Choose by destination, academic focus and housing style rather than brand alone.

When should I apply for next summer?+

Research in October and November, confirm credit transfer by December, apply for scholarships in their winter cycles, and submit your program application by February or March.
