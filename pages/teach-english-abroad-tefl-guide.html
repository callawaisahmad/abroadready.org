Teach English Abroad: TEFL Guide & Destinations

🗓️ Aug 5, 2026⏱️ 10 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

What is teach English abroad, and why does it still work?

Teaching English abroad is one of the few jobs you can land straight out of university with no prior experience, and demand remains strong across Asia, Europe, the Middle East and Latin America. For American, Canadian, British and Australian graduates, and for Pakistani graduates who can document native-level English, it is a realistic way to live overseas for a year or two, build savings in some destinations, and return with a genuinely international CV, or simply stay longer than you planned.

That is why teach English abroad is one of the most searched career questions from people who want an international move without spending a decade building a career first. The demand side is straightforward: English is the working language of international business, aviation, tourism and higher education, and dozens of countries actively recruit native-speaker teachers every single year.

What does this guide actually cover?

This guide covers the requirements, the certificates and what they cost, the destinations that actually pay well, and the visa reality behind every teaching job. Every section below is written as a direct question, so you can jump straight to the answer you need: whether you qualify, which certificate to buy, where the money is, how the visa works, and what happens after your first year.

Key takeaways

  • What is the minimum I need to qualify? A bachelor's degree and native-level English; most employers want a TEFL or TESOL certificate too.
  • Which certificate should I buy first? A 120-hour online TEFL course ($300 to $600) is enough for many jobs; CELTA ($1,500 to $2,500) matters most for career teachers.
  • What are the easiest sponsored routes? The JET Programme in Japan and Spain's auxiliar program are the most popular sponsored routes for Western graduates.
  • Will I actually save money? Salaries range from enough to break even (Spain) to genuine savings (Japan, South Korea, China, parts of Vietnam).
  • Can I work on a tourist visa? No. Employers must sponsor your work visa; working on a tourist visa is illegal everywhere.
  • How early should I start applying? Plan six to twelve months ahead, including background checks and authenticated documents.

The basic requirements

Do I need a degree to teach English abroad?

The universal baseline is a four-year bachelor's degree and native-level English. Employers usually verify this with a passport or an interview rather than a formal test, which means the strength of your passport and the clarity of your accent often matter more than any score you could achieve on an English proficiency exam.

Do employers also want a TEFL certificate and a background check?

Beyond the degree, a TEFL certificate is expected almost everywhere, and a clean criminal background check is required in most countries before a work visa can be issued to you. The background check is usually the slowest part of the whole process, so request it before you accept an offer rather than after you have turned down other options.

What extra paperwork might a government ask for?

Many governments also require documents such as apostilled degree certificates, certified translations of your transcript and a medium-of-instruction letter, so start gathering paperwork early. Age limits, teaching experience requirements and accent preferences vary by destination, so check each program's fine print rather than assuming one set of rules covers Asia, Europe and the Gulf at the same time.

TEFL, TESOL or CELTA: what to choose

Which certificate do most teach English abroad jobs ask for?

A 120-hour TEFL or TESOL course from a reputable provider, usually $300 to $600 online, satisfies employers across Asia, Latin America and the Middle East. It is the fastest route from zero experience to a shortlisted application, and it is the certificate that most first-time teachers genuinely start with.

When is a CELTA worth the extra money?

If you want a career in teaching or a position in a competitive European market, a CELTA (or Trinity CertTESOL) is the gold standard: an intensive four-week course costing roughly $1,500 to $2,500 that serious employers and government programs accept and often require. It is assessed, moderated and practical, so it doubles as real preparation for the classroom rather than a certificate you simply own.

Should I pick the certificate or the country first?

Choose the destination first, because your destination decides your certificate. Destinations in Asia and the Middle East will rarely ask you to hold a CELTA, while European language schools and government programs frequently do. Researching that requirement before you pay for a course saves you from ending up with a certificate your target employers do not recognise.

Best destinations and what they pay

Which countries hire the most first-time English teachers?

  • Japan (JET Programme): what do I actually get? A monthly salary around 280,000 yen, subsidized housing, and visa sponsorship; applications open in the fall through jetprogramme.org.
  • Spain (auxiliar program): what is the reality? US and Canadian citizens teach 12 to 16 hours a week for a modest monthly stipend, enough to live on in many regions, with excellent travel access.
  • South Korea (EPIK): is it worth it for a newcomer? Paid accommodation and strong salaries for new and experienced teachers alike, plus tax exemptions that applicants often overlook when comparing offers.
  • China: where are the highest salaries? The highest salaries in the industry, though program choice matters more than ever; teach only for accredited, vetted schools.
  • Vietnam and Thailand: can I really break even? Lower salaries but very low living costs, great for breaking even while enjoying Southeast Asia and often saving more than the headline pay suggests.
  • Czech Republic and the UAE: who are they right for? Interesting options for certified teachers, though entry requirements are higher than the mainstream Asia routes.

Visas, contracts and legalities

Can I legally teach English abroad on a tourist visa?

Legality is non-negotiable: a work visa, not a tourist visa, is required to teach, and schools that offer to "sort it out later" should be avoided. Working on the wrong immigration status can end in deportation, a ban on future entry and forfeited income, and no legitimate employer will ever ask you to do it.

Which routes handle sponsorship and paperwork most reliably?

The safest routes are government programs like JET, EPIK and Spain's auxiliares, because they handle sponsorship, health care and contracts properly. For direct jobs, ask for the contract in writing, check the employer's accreditation, and never pay a recruiter before verifying the school independently through your own research.

Do working holiday visas make moving abroad simpler?

British, Canadian and Australian citizens also have working holiday visas with several countries that make moving abroad much simpler, and in some destinations those permits cover teaching work. Check the conditions attached to your own passport, because the rules differ for every nationality and doing the wrong job on a working holiday is still illegal.

How much it costs to get started

What is the total cost of getting TEFL ready?

Getting ready costs less than most people think: roughly $400 to $700 for an online TEFL, or $1,500 to $2,500 for CELTA, plus around $1,000 to $2,000 for flights and a startup buffer. Add the cost of a police clearance certificate, passport renewal and any apostilling or certified translation fees your destination requires, and your realistic starting budget sits comfortably under $4,000.

Will I have to pay for housing before I arrive?

Most reputable employers provide or help with housing, and government programs cover or subsidize accommodation. Ask whether the apartment is deducted from your salary, what the deposit is, and whether utilities and internet are included, because that single detail can be worth several hundred dollars every month.

Is teach English abroad a good financial move?

Teaching abroad is genuinely a net-positive money move in destinations like Japan, Korea and China, and a break-even adventure in Europe. Salary is only half of the calculation; the other half is your cost of living, the accommodation package, tax treatment and how disciplined you are about saving while you are there.

How to Teach English Abroad Without Prior Teaching Experience

Can I teach English abroad with no teaching background at all?

Teaching English abroad is one of the few international career paths that does not require prior teaching experience, but understanding how to position yourself without a teaching background is essential. Most employers hiring English teachers abroad are looking for a bachelor degree in any field, native or near-native English proficiency and a TEFL, TESOL or CELTA certificate.

Why is the certificate the key differentiator?

The certificate is the key differentiator: without it, your application will generally not be considered, regardless of your degree or language skills. The certificate demonstrates that you have learned the fundamentals of lesson planning, classroom management, language analysis and the communicative teaching methods that employers expect. If you do not yet hold a certificate, obtaining one before you begin applying is the single most effective step you can take.

What documents do you need for teach English abroad?

The bachelor degree requirement is firm for most reputable employers, particularly in Asia and the Middle East. The degree does not need to be in education or English: employers in countries like South Korea, Japan, China and Saudi Arabia accept degrees in any discipline, as long as you hold a valid passport from a country designated as English-speaking or can demonstrate native-level proficiency.

For graduates who do not hold a degree from an English-medium institution, additional documentation such as a medium-of-instruction letter or a high IELTS or TOEFL score may be required. If you are a Pakistani graduate with a degree from an Urdu-medium university, you should be prepared to provide supplementary English proficiency evidence, as some employers may not automatically consider Pakistani degrees as meeting the native-speaker requirement.

How do I apply for teach English abroad?

Once you have the basic qualifications, the practical question is where to begin. The most accessible entry points for first-time English teachers are South Korea, Japan, China and Vietnam, where demand is consistently high, employers sponsor work visas and the application process is structured. In these countries, you can realistically secure a teaching position within two to three months of beginning your search, provided you have your TEFL certificate and degree ready.

European destinations like Spain, the Czech Republic and Poland are also accessible, though the visa process for non-EU citizens can be more complex and the pay is generally lower relative to the cost of living. The Middle East, particularly Saudi Arabia and the UAE, offers the highest salaries but typically requires some experience, making it a better target after your first year of teaching.

What is the first year of teaching English abroad really like?

Your first year of teaching abroad is as much about learning as it is about earning. Classroom management, adapting lessons to different proficiency levels and navigating a foreign education system are skills that develop through practice, not through your TEFL course alone. Be realistic about the learning curve: your first month in the classroom will be challenging, and it is normal to feel uncertain.

Employers who hire first-time teachers typically provide orientation, mentoring and ongoing support, so choose employers with a track record of investing in new teachers. Reading reviews from former teachers on forums and social media groups gives you a practical sense of what each employer and location is actually like, and it is one of the most valuable research steps you can take before accepting a position.

TEFL vs TESOL vs CELTA: Which Certificate to Choose

Are TEFL, TESOL and CELTA the same thing?

The three main English teaching certificates, TEFL, TESOL and CELTA, are often used interchangeably, but they differ in content, recognition and suitability for different teaching contexts. TEFL (Teaching English as a Foreign Language) is the most widely used certificate for teaching English abroad, particularly in countries where English is not the primary language.

TESOL (Teaching English to Speakers of Other Languages) is a broader term that encompasses both foreign and second language teaching, and TESOL certificates are accepted in most of the same contexts as TEFL. CELTA (Certificate in English Language Teaching to Adults) is administered by Cambridge University and is considered the most prestigious of the three, with particular recognition in Europe and among employers who value the Cambridge brand.

What does a TEFL, TESOL or CELTA course actually cover?

The content overlap between the three certificates is substantial. All three cover lesson planning, classroom management, language analysis (grammar, pronunciation, vocabulary), the communicative approach and practical teaching practice with real students. The main difference is in the intensity and the standardisation of assessment. CELTA is a four-week full-time course or an extended part-time course, assessed by Cambridge University moderators, and it includes a minimum of six hours of assessed teaching practice with adult learners.

TEFL and TESOL courses vary widely in quality: some are four-week intensive courses comparable to CELTA, while others are entirely online and can be completed in a few weeks at your own pace. The quality of your certificate depends on the provider, the course content and whether it includes supervised teaching practice.

How much does each certificate cost?

For practical purposes, the certificate you should choose depends on where you want to teach and what your budget allows. CELTA is the best choice if you plan to teach in Europe or if you want the strongest certificate for competitive positions, but it is also the most expensive, typically costing between 1,500 and 2,500 US dollars. A reputable TEFL or TESOL certificate from an accredited provider is sufficient for most positions in Asia, Latin America and the Middle East, and it costs significantly less.

When choosing a provider, look for courses that include at least 100 hours of instruction and at least six hours of observed teaching practice. Online-only certificates without teaching practice are widely available but are viewed less favourably by employers, particularly for positions that involve classroom teaching rather than online instruction.

What are the real requirements for a TEFL certificate?

A common mistake is choosing the cheapest certificate available without considering the course quality or the employer expectations. Some online TEFL certificates can be obtained for as little as 50 dollars in a few days, but these are not recognised by most reputable employers and may not prepare you adequately for the classroom. Conversely, some employers do not distinguish between TEFL, TESOL and CELTA and will accept any certificate that meets their minimum requirements.

Research the specific requirements of the countries and employers you are targeting before you invest in a certificate. If you are unsure, a 120-hour TEFL certificate from an accredited provider with teaching practice is a safe and practical choice that opens doors in most markets. For Pakistani graduates, the certificate also strengthens your application by providing internationally recognised evidence of your English teaching ability, which is particularly valuable when your degree is from a non-English-medium institution.

Teaching English Abroad Salary and Cost of Living by Destination

How do salaries compare across the main destinations?

Salaries for English teachers abroad vary enormously by destination, and understanding the relationship between salary and cost of living is more important than looking at the salary figure alone.

South Korea offers some of the best packages for first-time teachers: a typical salary ranges from 1,800 to 2,500 US dollars per month, with free or subsidised housing provided by the employer, a flight reimbursement and a completion bonus. Japan offers slightly higher salaries, typically 2,000 to 3,000 US dollars per month, but the cost of living in Tokyo and Osaka is significantly higher, so savings potential is lower. China offers a wide salary range, from 1,200 to 3,500 US dollars per month depending on the city and the employer, and the cost of living in tier-two and tier-three cities is low enough to allow substantial savings.

What do the Gulf and European destinations pay?

Middle Eastern destinations offer the highest salaries but have higher entry requirements. Saudi Arabia and the UAE typically offer 2,500 to 4,500 US dollars per month, often with housing, flights and health insurance included. However, these positions usually require at least two years of teaching experience and a TEFL or CELTA certificate, making them less accessible for first-time teachers.

European destinations like Spain, the Czech Republic and Poland offer lower salaries, typically 800 to 1,500 US dollars per month, and housing is rarely included. The cost of living varies across Europe, and teachers in these countries often supplement their income with private tutoring or online teaching. Latin American destinations like Mexico, Colombia and Brazil offer salaries that range from 600 to 1,500 US dollars per month, but the cost of living is low and the cultural experience is a major draw.

What hidden costs and tax rules reduce my take-home pay?

The hidden financial factors are as important as the headline salary. Some countries tax foreign teachers heavily, while others offer tax exemptions for a limited period. South Korea, for example, provides a tax-free salary for the first two years for most E-2 visa holders, which significantly increases your effective income. Japan taxes foreign teachers from their first year, but the rate is relatively low for lower incomes.

Health insurance contributions vary by country: in some destinations, the employer covers the full cost, while in others, you must pay a monthly contribution. Housing is the largest single expense in most destinations, and an employer-provided apartment or housing allowance can be worth 300 to 800 US dollars per month, depending on the city. Always ask for a complete compensation package breakdown before accepting a position, not just the monthly salary.

Can I save money and send it home while teaching English abroad?

For Pakistani graduates considering teaching abroad, the financial equation also includes remittance potential. Teaching in a high-salary destination and remitting money home can be a powerful wealth-building strategy, but it requires discipline and planning. Countries with strong currencies relative to the Pakistani rupee, such as the UAE, Saudi Arabia and South Korea, offer the best remittance value.

Opening a foreign currency account in Pakistan and using regulated remittance channels ensures that your transfers are secure and that you benefit from favourable exchange rates. Teachers who plan to save should also consider the cost of initial setup in their destination country, including visa fees, flights, accommodation deposits and the first month living expenses before their first salary payment. Having three to six months of living expenses saved before you depart prevents financial stress during the transition period.

Teaching English Abroad: Visa and Legal Requirements by Country

Do I need a work visa to teach English abroad?

Every country where you teach English abroad requires a specific work visa or permit, and understanding the legal requirements for each destination is essential before you accept a position. Working on a tourist visa is illegal in virtually every country and can result in deportation, a ban on future entry and the loss of any income you have earned.

Legitimate employers will sponsor your work visa as part of the hiring process, and you should never accept a position that asks you to work on a tourist visa or without a formal contract. The visa process varies by country, but the general sequence is: receive a job offer, have the employer initiate the visa application, submit the required documents and attend an interview if necessary.

Which visa do South Korea, Japan and China require?

South Korea requires an E-2 foreign language teaching visa, which is sponsored by your employer. The process involves submitting your degree, TEFL certificate, criminal background check and passport to the Korean consulate in your country. The employer handles the Certificate of Eligibility, which is submitted to the consulate along with your application. Processing typically takes two to four weeks.

Japan requires an instructor visa, which follows a similar process: the employer applies for a Certificate of Eligibility from the Japan immigration office, and you then use this certificate to apply for the visa at the Japanese consulate. China requires a Z visa (work visa), which also requires a background check, a health examination and a degree verification. Each country has its own timeline and document requirements, so begin the visa process as soon as you receive a job offer.

What about teaching English in Europe as a non-EU citizen?

European countries have more complex visa landscapes for non-EU citizens. Spain, the Czech Republic and Poland all require work permits for non-EU teachers, and the process can be slower and more bureaucratic than in Asian destinations. Some European countries offer freelancer visas or self-employment visas that allow you to teach English independently, but these require you to register as a self-employed person and manage your own tax and social security contributions.

The EU Blue Card is available for highly qualified employment, but English teaching typically does not meet the salary threshold for a Blue Card. For non-EU graduates who want to teach in Europe, the most practical route is often to find an employer who will sponsor a work permit, though this limits your options to larger language schools and international institutions.

What extra documents do Pakistani graduates need to prepare?

For Pakistani graduates, the visa process has additional practical considerations. The criminal background check (police clearance certificate) must be obtained from the Pakistani police authorities, and processing times can vary. The degree verification process may require your degree to be attested by the Higher Education Commission of Pakistan and, in some cases, by the relevant foreign embassy.

Passport validity is another factor: most countries require your passport to be valid for at least six to twelve months beyond your intended stay, so renew your passport well in advance if it is close to expiry. Some countries also require a health examination, and the panel physician must be approved by the relevant consulate. Begin gathering these documents as soon as you begin your job search, as delays in document procurement are one of the most common reasons for missed start dates.

Long-Term Career Development for English Teachers Abroad

Can teaching English abroad become a long-term career?

Teaching English abroad can be more than a short-term adventure: it can develop into a sustainable career with meaningful progression. The career path for English teachers typically moves from entry-level classroom teaching to senior teacher, teacher trainer, curriculum developer, academic manager or examiner. Each of these steps requires additional qualifications, experience or both, and planning your progression from the beginning of your teaching career gives you a strategic advantage.

Should I take the DELTA after my CELTA?

The most common next step after two to three years of classroom teaching is the DELTA (Diploma in English Language Teaching to Adults), a Cambridge University qualification that is recognised worldwide and opens doors to senior teaching and training roles. The DELTA is more demanding and expensive than the CELTA, but it is the standard qualification for career progression in English language teaching.

Which career paths exist outside the classroom?

Beyond classroom teaching, English teachers can move into related fields such as educational publishing, language assessment, teacher training and programme management. International examination bodies like Cambridge Assessment, IELTS and ETS hire examiners and test developers who have classroom experience, and these roles often come with higher salaries and greater job security than classroom teaching.

Educational publishers hire content developers and materials writers who understand the needs of English language learners, and teacher training organisations hire trainers who can deliver TEFL, TESOL and CELTA courses to new teachers. Each of these paths builds on your classroom experience, and the network you develop during your teaching years is often the route through which these opportunities arise.

Which specialisations pay the most?

For teachers who want to remain in the classroom, specialisation can increase both your satisfaction and your earning potential. Business English teaching is one of the most lucrative specialisations, with rates significantly higher than general English teaching, particularly in corporate and professional contexts. IELTS preparation is another high-demand specialisation, particularly in countries like Pakistan, India and China where students are preparing for English proficiency exams for study abroad.

Young learner teaching, English for specific purposes (ESP) and English for academic purposes (EAP) are other specialisations that can differentiate you in the job market and command higher rates. Each specialisation requires additional knowledge and sometimes additional qualifications, but the investment is repaid through higher pay and more interesting work.

When should I plan to return home?

The practical question of when to return home is one that every English teacher abroad eventually faces. Some teachers build a career abroad and never return to their home country, while others teach for a few years and then use the experience as a springboard to a different career at home.

For Pakistani graduates, teaching English abroad provides skills that are directly transferable to the Pakistani education sector, including curriculum development, classroom management, assessment design and teacher training. The international experience and language skills you develop abroad are valued by Pakistani schools, universities, language centres and educational NGOs. Returning to Pakistan with teaching experience and an international perspective positions you for roles in educational leadership, programme management and educational consulting that would be difficult to access without overseas experience.

The decision of when and how to return is personal, but planning for it from the beginning of your teaching career ensures that your time abroad contributes to your long-term professional goals.

Teaching English Abroad: Cultural Adaptation and Classroom Challenges

What cultural differences should I expect as an English teacher abroad?

Cultural adaptation is one of the most significant challenges for English teachers abroad, and understanding the cultural dynamics of your host country before you arrive helps you avoid misunderstandings and build stronger relationships with students, colleagues and employers. Teaching styles, student behaviour and classroom expectations vary enormously across cultures.

In South Korea and Japan, for example, students may be reluctant to speak up in class, ask questions or disagree with the teacher, as the educational culture emphasises respect for authority and group harmony. In contrast, students in Latin America or the Middle East may be more vocal and expressive in the classroom, which can be a positive dynamic but may also require different classroom management strategies. Understanding these cultural norms allows you to adapt your teaching approach to the students you are actually teaching, rather than applying a one-size-fits-all method.

How do I manage a class of mixed-ability students?

Classroom management is the area where first-time English teachers abroad face the steepest learning curve. Managing a class of twenty to thirty students who may have different English proficiency levels, different learning styles and different levels of motivation requires flexibility, creativity and patience. Establish clear routines from the first day: how students should get your attention, how group work is organised, how questions are asked and how behaviour expectations are communicated.

Use a variety of activities to maintain engagement, including pair work, group discussions, games, songs, role-plays and real-world tasks. When students are disengaged or disruptive, address the behaviour calmly and privately rather than creating a public confrontation. Building rapport with students through genuine interest in their lives and learning goals is one of the most effective classroom management strategies, as students who feel respected and valued are more likely to engage positively.

How do I handle homesickness and culture shock?

Dealing with homesickness, culture shock and the emotional challenges of living abroad is an aspect of teaching English that is rarely discussed in TEFL guides but affects many teachers. The first few weeks in a new country can be overwhelming: the language barrier, the unfamiliar food, the distance from family and friends and the pressure of starting a new job all contribute to stress and isolation. Recognising that these feelings are normal and temporary is the first step.

Building a support network of fellow teachers, both local and international, provides emotional support and practical advice. Staying connected with family and friends through regular video calls helps maintain your sense of identity and belonging. Physical activity, exploring your new city and maintaining hobbies and routines from home all contribute to your wellbeing. Most teachers find that after the initial adjustment period, the experience becomes increasingly rewarding and enjoyable.

How do I build a good relationship with my employer?

The relationship with your employer and colleagues is another dimension of cultural adaptation that requires attention. In some countries, the relationship between foreign teachers and school management is formal and hierarchical, while in others it is more collaborative and informal. Understanding the power dynamics, communication norms and expectations of your workplace helps you navigate professional relationships effectively. Attend staff meetings, participate in school events and show interest in the broader work of the school, not just your own classes.

This demonstrates professionalism and commitment, and it builds relationships that can lead to opportunities for professional development, contract renewal and career advancement. For Pakistani teachers abroad, maintaining your cultural identity while adapting to a new professional environment is a balance that requires thoughtfulness and flexibility, but it is also one of the most enriching aspects of the experience.

Teaching English Online as a Supplement to In-Person Work

Can I teach English online while living abroad?

Teaching English online has become a significant supplement to in-person teaching, and for teachers abroad it provides an additional income stream that can be pursued alongside a classroom position. Online teaching platforms connect teachers with students worldwide, and the flexibility of scheduling means that you can teach before or after your in-person classes, at weekends or during holidays.

Which online teaching platforms should I choose?

The major platforms include those that provide structured curricula and students, such as VIPKid, Cambly and italki, and those that allow you to build your own client base, such as Preply and your own website. Each platform has its own requirements, pay structure and scheduling model, so researching the options before you commit is important.

How much do online English lessons pay?

The pay for online English teaching varies significantly by platform, student location and your qualifications. Teaching Chinese students through platforms like VIPKid has historically paid between 15 and 25 US dollars per hour, though this market has changed in recent years due to regulatory shifts. Teaching European and Latin American students through platforms like italki or Preply typically pays between 10 and 20 US dollars per hour, depending on your qualifications and experience.

Private tutoring, which you can arrange through your own network or through word of mouth, often commands the highest rates, as there is no platform commission. For teachers already abroad, the combination of a full-time classroom position and part-time online teaching can significantly increase your income, particularly if you are teaching in a destination where the cost of living is high.

What equipment do I need to teach online?

The technical requirements for online teaching are straightforward: a reliable internet connection, a computer or tablet with a webcam and microphone, a quiet teaching space and good lighting. Investing in a headset with a noise-cancelling microphone improves audio quality and makes the teaching experience more professional. A stable internet connection is essential: if your home connection is unreliable, consider using a co-working space or a location with better connectivity for your online sessions.

Some platforms require specific technical setups, such as a wired internet connection rather than Wi-Fi, so check the requirements before you commit. For teachers in countries with unreliable internet, having a backup plan, such as a mobile hotspot or a nearby location with reliable connectivity, prevents missed sessions and frustrated students.

How do I avoid burnout while building an online income?

Building a sustainable online teaching practice alongside your in-person work requires time management and boundaries. It is tempting to fill every available hour with online sessions, but burnout is a real risk, particularly if you are already teaching full-time during the day. Set clear boundaries around your online teaching hours, and protect your rest and personal time. Use a scheduling tool to manage your availability, and communicate clearly with students about your time zone and your available hours.

Over time, you can develop a regular client base of students who book recurring sessions, which provides more predictable income and a more satisfying teaching experience than sporadic one-off lessons. For teachers who eventually want to transition from classroom teaching to full-time online work, building a client base while you are still employed provides a safety net during the transition.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a degree to teach English abroad?+

In almost every country, yes, a four-year bachelor's degree is the standard requirement for a legal work visa. The degree subject rarely matters.

What is the difference between TEFL, TESOL and CELTA?+

TEFL and TESOL are general certificates, and a 120-hour online course is the common baseline. CELTA is a more rigorous, widely recognized qualification that serious employers and competitive markets prefer.

Which country pays the most for teaching English?+

China typically offers the highest salaries, with Japan, South Korea and parts of the Middle East close behind. Europe, especially Spain, pays less but offers unmatched travel access.

Do I need a TEFL certificate before applying to JET or EPIK?+

No. Government programs like JET and EPIK do not require TEFL, but having one makes you more competitive for other jobs and for the destinations that do require it.

Is teaching English abroad worth the cost of getting certified?+

Yes. A $400 online TEFL pays for itself quickly, and a $2,000 CELTA opens better-paying, longer-term positions in markets that require recognized qualifications.
