Study in Germany: The Complete 2026 Guide (Admissions, Costs, Visa & Scholarships)

🗓️ Aug 17, 2026⏱️ 35 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

Germany is one of the most popular study-abroad destinations in the world, and for good reason. Public universities charge almost no tuition. The country is home to some of the highest-ranked universities on the planet. There are over 1,800 English-taught programmes available. And after graduation, you get an 18-month job-seeker visa to stay and find work.

More than 400,000 international students are currently studying in Germany. They come from every continent, drawn by the promise of world-class education without crippling student debt. But the path from deciding to study in Germany to actually landing in a lecture hall is complicated. You need to understand the German education system, navigate the visa process, open a blocked account, get health insurance, and meet language requirements.

This guide covers everything. It is designed to be the only page you need. Whether you are a high school student exploring bachelor's options, a graduate considering a master's, or a parent helping your child plan — this is your complete roadmap to studying in Germany in 2026 and beyond.

Key Takeaways

  • Most public universities in Germany charge zero tuition for international students — you only pay a semester contribution of EUR 150–350.
  • Students from China, India, Vietnam, and Mongolia need an APS certificate before applying.
  • You need EUR 11,904 in a blocked account to prove you can cover living costs for one year.
  • English-taught programmes typically require IELTS 6.0–6.5 or TOEFL iBT 79–100. Many universities waive IELTS if your prior degree was in English.
  • After graduation, you can stay for 18 months on a job-seeker visa, then switch to a work permit or EU Blue Card.
  • DAAD scholarships, the Deutschlandstipendium, and Erasmus Mundus can fully fund your studies.

1. Why Study in Germany?

Every year, hundreds of thousands of international students choose Germany over the US, UK, Australia, and Canada. Here is why.

Free or Nearly Free Tuition

The single biggest reason is cost. Germany abolished tuition fees at public universities for undergraduate programmes and most consecutive master's programmes. This applies to international students, not just EU citizens. You pay only a semester contribution of EUR 150 to 350, which typically includes a public transport ticket. Compare that to USD 20,000–60,000 per year at American universities, and the financial advantage is enormous.

World-Class Universities

Germany has 12 universities in the global top 150 (QS World University Rankings 2026). The Technical University of Munich, Heidelberg University, and Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich consistently rank among the best in the world. German universities are especially strong in engineering, computer science, medicine, natural sciences, and philosophy.

English-Taught Programmes

You do not need to speak German to study in Germany. There are 800+ English-taught bachelor's programmes and 1,800+ English-taught master's programmes available across the country. These programmes are designed for international students and use English as the language of instruction.

Strong Economy and Job Market

Germany has the largest economy in Europe and the fourth-largest in the world. It faces a significant skilled worker shortage, especially in engineering, IT, healthcare, and STEM fields. International graduates are actively recruited, and the post-study work visa gives you ample time to find a job.

Central European Location

Germany sits in the heart of Europe. You can reach Paris, Amsterdam, Prague, Vienna, and Zurich by train in a few hours. Budget airlines connect you to virtually every European city for under EUR 50. Studying in Germany means Europe is your playground during weekends and semester breaks.

Large International Community

With over 400,000 international students from more than 170 countries, Germany is one of the most diverse study destinations globally. Most German universities have international offices, buddy programmes, and orientation weeks designed to help you settle in.

2. The German Education System

Before you apply, you need to understand how the German higher education system is structured. It is different from most English-speaking countries.

Types of Institutions

Universitäten (Universities) — These are traditional research-oriented universities. They offer the full range of subjects including humanities, natural sciences, medicine, law, and theology. They award doctoral degrees and focus heavily on theoretical knowledge. If you want to pursue a PhD or a research-focused career, a Universität is usually the right choice.

Fachhochschulen (Universities of Applied Sciences) — These institutions focus on practical, industry-oriented education. Programmes in engineering, business, IT, and design are especially popular. Fachhochschulen have strong connections to industry, mandatory internship semesters, and smaller class sizes. They do not typically award doctoral degrees, but they produce graduates who are immediately employable. Many international students mistakenly overlook Fachhochschulen — they should not. A degree from a Fachhochschule is highly respected by German employers.

Kunsthochschulen (Academies of Art and Music) — These are specialised institutions for fine arts, music, design, film, and performing arts. Admission typically requires a portfolio or audition, and tuition may be charged even at public Kunsthochschulen.

Degree Structure

Bachelor's Degree — 3 years (6 semesters), 180 ECTS credits. This is the standard first degree. Programmes are either "consecutive" (meaning a related master's builds on them) or "non-consecutive."

Master's Degree — 2 years (4 semesters), 120 ECTS credits. You need a completed bachelor's degree to enrol. Master's programmes are either research-focused (academic career path) or professionally oriented (industry career path).

PhD (Promotion) — 3 to 4 years. You need a master's degree (or in some cases an excellent bachelor's). You can do a PhD at a university or at a non-university research institute like the Max Planck Society or Fraunhofer Society.

The ECTS System

Germany uses the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS). One ECTS credit represents 25 to 30 hours of student work. A full academic year equals 60 ECTS. This system makes it straightforward to compare and transfer credits between European universities.

Semester Structure

The German academic year has two semesters:

  • Winter Semester (Wintersemester) — October to March. This is the main semester. Most programmes start in October.
  • Summer Semester (Summersemester) — April to September. Fewer programmes start in April, but it is still an option.

Lectures typically run from mid-October to mid-February and mid-April to mid-July. Exams take place in the weeks following each lecture period. Semester breaks are not "holidays" — they are study and exam periods.

3. Tuition Costs — The Real Numbers

Let us break down exactly what studying in Germany costs. The headline number is "free tuition," but you need to understand the full picture.

Public University Tuition

At most public universities, tuition is EUR 0 for both German and international students. This applies to bachelor's programmes and most consecutive master's programmes at universities in all states except Baden-Württemberg.

Baden-Württemberg Exception: Non-EU international students at public universities in Baden-Württemberg pay EUR 1,500 per semester in tuition. This affects universities like the University of Freiburg, Heidelberg University, University of Stuttgart, and TU Karlsruhe. Even with this fee, studying in Baden-Württemberg is far cheaper than most countries.

Semester Contribution

Every student pays a semester contribution (Semesterbeitrag) of EUR 150 to 350, depending on the university. This is not tuition. It covers:

  • Studentenwerk fee — student services including canteen subsidies, counselling, and housing support
  • AStA fee — the student body government that funds clubs, events, and advocacy
  • Semester ticket — a public transport ticket valid for buses, trams, and trains in your region (some tickets cover the entire state)
  • Administration fee — a small processing charge

The semester ticket alone is worth EUR 200–400 if you purchased it separately, so the contribution is genuinely good value.

Private University Tuition

Private universities in Germany do charge tuition. Annual fees typically range from EUR 5,000 to EUR 20,000. Some elite private institutions like WHU, ESMT Berlin, and Jacobs University charge up to EUR 30,000 per year. Private universities are accredited and offer strong programmes, but they are not the reason most people choose Germany.

Living Costs — City by City

The German government estimates international students need EUR 11,904 per year (EUR 992 per month) to cover living costs. This is the amount required in your blocked account. Actual costs vary significantly by city:

Expensive Cities (EUR 1,000–1,200/month):

  • Munich — the most expensive city in Germany. Rent for a shared flat is EUR 500–700. Total monthly costs: EUR 1,000–1,200.
  • Frankfurt — high rents due to the financial sector. Total: EUR 950–1,100.
  • Hamburg — port city with a high quality of life. Total: EUR 900–1,100.

Moderate Cities (EUR 800–1,000/month):

  • Berlin — the capital. Rents have risen but remain moderate. Total: EUR 850–1,000.
  • Cologne — vibrant cultural scene. Total: EUR 800–950.
  • Düsseldorf — business hub. Total: EUR 850–1,000.

Affordable Cities (EUR 600–800/month):

  • Leipzig — rapidly growing, very affordable. Total: EUR 600–750.
  • Dresden — beautiful city, low cost of living. Total: EUR 600–750.
  • Chemnitz — one of the cheapest cities in Germany. Total: EUR 550–700.
  • Jena — university city with low rents. Total: EUR 600–750.

5. Admission Requirements — Bachelor's Degree

Getting into a German bachelor's programme has specific requirements that differ from other countries.

Academic Qualifications

You need a school-leaving qualification that is equivalent to the German Abitur. This typically means:

  • 12 years of schooling with strong grades (varies by country)
  • A-levels, IB Diploma, or equivalent qualification
  • Students from India usually need a minimum of 60% in Class 12 (some universities require 70%+)
  • Students from Pakistan need a Higher Secondary School Certificate (HSSC) with strong grades

Check the anabin database (anabin.kmk.org) to verify whether your qualification is recognised. Your qualification is classified as "direkt" (direct entry), "bedingt" (conditional — requires Studienkolleg), or "nicht" (not recognised).

Numerus Clausus (NC)

Some programmes have a numerus clausus (NC), meaning there are more applicants than places. Your GPA determines whether you get a spot. NC varies by programme and university — it can range from 1.5 (excellent) to 3.0 (average). Many programmes, especially at Fachhochschulen, have open admission (zulassungsfrei) — if you meet the basic requirements, you get in.

Application via uni-assist

uni-assist (uni-assist.de) is a central application portal used by most German universities. It processes and evaluates your documents before forwarding them to the university. Not all universities use uni-assist — some accept direct applications. Always check the university's website to know which method they require.

uni-assist costs:

  • First application: EUR 75
  • Each additional university: EUR 30

Required Documents

  • Higher secondary school certificate (certified copy)
  • Transcript of marks
  • Passport copy
  • Language certificate (IELTS, TOEFL, TestDaF, or DSH)
  • APS certificate (if required — see Section 7)
  • CV (some universities require a specific format)
  • Motivation letter (some programmes)

Application Deadlines

  • Winter Semester: Apply by July 15 (for non-EU students). Some universities have earlier deadlines.
  • Summer Semester: Apply by January 15. Fewer programmes are available.

Studienkolleg

If your school-leaving qualification is not equivalent to the Abitur, you may need to attend a Studienkolleg — a one-year preparatory course. It costs approximately EUR 1,200–1,500 per semester and ends with the Feststellungsprüfung (assessment test). Passing this exam qualifies you for university admission in your chosen subject area. Studienkollegs are offered in different tracks:

  • T-Kurs — for technical, mathematical, and science programmes
  • M-Kurs — for medicine, biology, and pharmacy
  • W-Kurs — for business and economics
  • G-Kurs — for humanities and social sciences
  • S-Kurs — for German studies

6. Admission Requirements — Master's Degree

Master's admission is generally more straightforward than bachelor's admission, but it is also more competitive.

Academic Requirements

  • A completed bachelor's degree (or equivalent) in a relevant field
  • Your degree must be recognised in Germany — check the anabin database for your university and degree
  • Typical GPA requirement: 2.5 to 3.0 on the German grading scale (where 1.0 is best and 5.0 is failing). Many top programmes require 1.5–2.0.

German grades work inversely: 1.0 is the best, 4.0 is the minimum pass, and 5.0 is a fail. Use a conversion tool like the DAAD grade converter to translate your GPA.

Standardised Tests

  • GRE — required or recommended for some STEM and economics programmes (e.g., TU Munich, RWTH Aachen)
  • GMAT — required for MBA and some business programmes
  • Not all programmes require these — always check the specific programme page

Work Experience

Some master's programmes, especially MBAs, require 1–3 years of professional work experience. Academic master's programmes generally do not require work experience.

Required Documents

  • Bachelor's degree certificate (certified copy)
  • Full transcript of records
  • CV ( Europass or tabular format)
  • Motivation letter (1–2 pages explaining your goals)
  • Language certificate (IELTS/TOEFL for English-taught, TestDaF/DSH for German-taught)
  • APS certificate (if applicable)
  • Letters of recommendation (1–2, academic or professional)
  • Copy of passport

Application Process

Many master's programmes accept applications through uni-assist. However, an increasing number of universities accept direct applications through their own portals. Some use the platform myStipendium or Hochschulkompass.de for centralised searching. Always start your search on the DAAD programme database.

7. The APS Certificate — Complete Guide

The APS certificate is one of the most important — and most misunderstood — requirements for studying in Germany. If you are from China, India, Vietnam, or Mongolia, you cannot apply to German universities without it.

What Is APS?

APS stands for Akademische Prüfstelle (Academic Evaluation Centre). It is an assessment body run by the German embassies in Beijing, New Delhi, Hanoi, and Ulaanbaatar. Its purpose is to verify that your academic documents are genuine and that you have the knowledge you claim to have. The APS system was introduced to prevent document fraud.

Who Needs APS?

  • China — all students applying to German universities
  • India — all students applying to German universities
  • Vietnam — all students applying to German universities
  • Mongolia — all students applying to German universities

If you are from any other country, you do not need APS. This includes Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nigeria, Brazil, and all other nations.

APS Process Step by Step

Step 1: Gather Documents

  • All degree certificates (10th, 12th, bachelor's, master's — whatever you have)
  • Full transcripts of marks
  • Language certificates (IELTS, TestDaF, etc.)
  • Passport copy
  • Two passport-sized photographs

Step 2: Submit Application

Apply online through the APS website for your country (aps-india.org, aps-china.de, etc.). Pay the application fee — approximately EUR 25,000 in local currency (the fee is set in local currency and varies slightly).

Step 3: Document Review

APS reviews your documents for authenticity. This takes 2 to 4 weeks. If there are issues with your documents, they may request additional information.

Step 4: Interview

You will be invited for a 15–20 minute interview at the APS office. The interview is conducted in German or English (or a mix, depending on your programme). The interviewer will ask about your academic background, your subject knowledge, and your motivation for studying in Germany. This is not an oral exam — they want to verify that you actually studied what your transcript says you studied.

Step 5: Receive APS Certificate

If you pass, you receive the APS certificate (a physical document). You need this certificate for every university application and for your visa interview. Keep it safe — you may need multiple copies.

Tips for the APS Interview

  • Be honest — do not exaggerate your knowledge or achievements
  • Review your bachelor's coursework, especially core subjects
  • Be able to explain your final-year project or thesis clearly
  • Prepare a 2-minute introduction about yourself and your study plans
  • If the interview is in English, practise speaking clearly about your academic subjects
  • Know why you chose Germany and your specific programme

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Submitting documents that are not certified or translated into English/German
  • Not preparing for the interview — even native speakers can struggle if they do not review their coursework
  • Applying too late — start the APS process at least 3–4 months before your university application deadline
  • Providing inconsistent information between your APS application and university application

Timeline and Cost

  • Processing time: 4 to 8 weeks total (document review + interview scheduling)
  • Cost: Approximately EUR 25,000 (paid in local currency)
  • Validity: The APS certificate does not expire, but you should apply within 6 months of receiving it

8. Language Requirements

Language is the gatekeeper for studying in Germany. You need to prove your proficiency in either English or German, depending on the language of instruction.

English-Taught Programmes

For English-taught programmes, accepted tests and typical minimum scores include:

  • IELTS Academic: 6.0 to 6.5 overall (some programmes require 7.0)
  • TOEFL iBT: 79 to 100 (varies by programme)
  • PTE Academic: 55 to 65
  • Cambridge C1 Advanced (CAE): Grade C or above
  • Duolingo English Test: 100 to 110 (growing acceptance, but not universal)

Many German universities do not require IELTS if your previous degree was taught entirely in English. You will need a medium-of-instruction letter from your university as proof. See our guide to studying abroad without IELTS for more options.

German-Taught Programmes

For German-taught programmes, you need one of these:

  • TestDaF: TDN 4 in all four sections (reading, listening, writing, speaking)
  • DSH-2 or DSH-3: Taken at the university itself. DSH-2 is the standard requirement.
  • Goethe-Zertifikat C2: The highest level German language certificate
  • telc Deutsch C1 or C2: Accepted by many universities
  • ÖSD C2: Austrian German certificate, also accepted

Even for English-taught programmes, learning German is strongly recommended for daily life, part-time work, and post-graduation career prospects. A B1 level is enough for everyday life; B2 or C1 opens up significantly more job opportunities.

How to Study Without IELTS

If you do not have IELTS, check these alternatives:

  • Medium of instruction letter: If your bachelor's was in English, many German universities waive the IELTS requirement
  • TOEFL or PTE: Universally accepted alternatives
  • Internal language test: Some universities offer their own English assessment
  • Duolingo: Accepted at a growing number of universities

9. Application Process — Step by Step

Here is the complete application process from start to finish.

Step 1: Research Programmes

Use these databases to find programmes:

  • DAAD programme database — daad.de/en/study-and-research-in-germany/find-programme/
  • Hochschulkompass.de — official German university database
  • Study in Germany — study-in-germany.de

Filter by subject, language of instruction, degree level, and city. Create a shortlist of 5–10 programmes.

Step 2: Check APS Requirement

If you are from China, India, Vietnam, or Mongolia, start the APS process immediately. This can take 1–2 months and must be completed before you can submit applications.

Step 3: Take Language Test

Schedule your IELTS, TOEFL, TestDaF, or other language test at least 3 months before your application deadline. Book early — test slots fill up fast.

Step 4: Prepare Documents

  • All academic certificates and transcripts
  • Certified translations (into English or German by a sworn translator)
  • Apostille or legalisation of documents (check your country's requirements)
  • APS certificate (if applicable)
  • Language test results
  • Passport copy
  • CV and motivation letter
  • Passport-sized photos

Step 5: Submit Applications

Apply through uni-assist or directly to the university, depending on their requirements. Submit to multiple universities — there is no limit. Pay the uni-assist fee (EUR 75 for the first, EUR 30 for each additional university).

Step 6: Wait for Admission

Processing takes 4 to 8 weeks. You will receive an admission letter (Zulassungsbescheid) or a rejection. Some universities send a "conditional" admission if you still need to complete a language course.

Step 7: Accept Offer

Accept the offer by the stated deadline. Pay the semester contribution (EUR 150–350). This confirms your place and enrols you.

Step 8: Open a Blocked Account

You need EUR 11,904 in a blocked account for your visa application. See Section 11 for detailed instructions.

Step 9: Get Health Insurance

German law requires all students to have health insurance. Public insurance for students costs EUR 110–120 per month. See Section 12 for details.

Step 10: Book Visa Appointment

Contact the German embassy or consulate in your country. Book your appointment as early as possible — wait times can be 2–8 weeks depending on location and season. See our student visa guide for a complete walkthrough.

Step 11: Attend Visa Interview

Bring all documents in both original and copy. Be prepared to answer questions about your study plans, finances, and motivation. The interview typically lasts 10–15 minutes.

Step 12: Receive Visa and Travel

Processing takes 4 to 12 weeks. Once you receive your visa, you can travel to Germany. Most student visas allow entry 1–2 weeks before the semester starts.

Application Deadlines Summary

  • Winter Semester (starting October): Apply by July 15
  • Summer Semester (starting April): Apply by January 15
  • Some universities and uni-assist have earlier deadlines — check individually

10. DAAD Scholarships — Complete Guide

The German Academic Exchange Service (DAAD) is the world's largest funding organisation for international academic exchange. It offers a wide range of scholarships for international students.

Types of DAAD Scholarships

Study Scholarships for Foreign Graduates

For students pursuing a master's degree or a postgraduate programme at a German university. The scholarship provides a monthly stipend of EUR 934 to EUR 1,200, depending on your academic level, plus travel allowance and health insurance. Deadline: typically October each year for the following academic year.

Research Grants for Doctoral Candidates

For PhD candidates and young researchers who want to conduct research at a German university. Monthly stipend of EUR 1,200. Can last 1 to 4 years depending on the programme.

Development-Related Postgraduate Courses (EPOS)

For graduates from developing countries who want to pursue a postgraduate programme related to development. Covers tuition, living costs, travel, and insurance. The programmes are specifically designed for professionals with work experience.

Helmut Schmidt Programme (Public Policy and Good Governance)

A fully funded master's programme in public policy, economic development, or human rights. Offered at eight German universities. Includes full tuition, monthly stipend of EUR 861, travel costs, and health insurance. Designed for future leaders from developing countries.

One-Year Research Grants

For graduate students and young academics who want to conduct a research project at a German university. Duration: 7 to 12 months. Monthly stipend of EUR 1,200.

DAAD Eligibility Criteria

General requirements vary by programme, but typically include:

  • Excellent academic record (usually top 10% of your class)
  • Completed bachelor's degree (for master's scholarships) or master's degree (for PhD scholarships)
  • Language proficiency (English and/or German, depending on the programme)
  • Strong motivation letter and academic references
  • Relevant work experience (for some programmes like EPOS)
  • Citizenship of a country eligible for the specific scholarship

DAAD Benefits

  • Monthly stipend of EUR 861 to EUR 1,200
  • Travel allowance (flight tickets or reimbursement)
  • Health insurance coverage
  • Study and research allowance
  • German language course funding (in some programmes)
  • Network of DAAD alumni worldwide

Tips for Winning a DAAD Scholarship

  • Start early: Prepare your application 3–4 months before the deadline
  • Strong motivation letter: Explain specifically why you want to study in Germany, why that programme, and how it connects to your career goals
  • Academic excellence: Highlight research, publications, awards, and high grades
  • Relevance: Show how your studies will benefit your home country
  • German language skills: Even for English-taught programmes, showing basic German (A1–B1) strengthens your application
  • Strong references: Get recommendation letters from professors who know you well and can speak to your abilities specifically

Other Scholarships in Germany

  • Deutschlandstipendium: EUR 300 per month, merit-based, available at most universities
  • Erasmus Mundus Joint Masters: Fully funded EU scholarship for specific master's programmes
  • Heinrich Böll Foundation: For students committed to ecology, democracy, and human rights
  • Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung: For politically and socially engaged students
  • Friedrich Ebert Stiftung: For students committed to social democracy
  • University-specific scholarships: Many German universities offer their own scholarships for international students

11. Blocked Account (Sperrkonto) — Complete Guide

The blocked account (Sperrkonto) is a special German bank account where you deposit money that proves you can support yourself financially during your studies. It is a mandatory requirement for the student visa.

What Is a Blocked Account?

A blocked account is a bank account where a fixed amount of money is "blocked" — meaning you cannot withdraw it freely. Instead, the bank releases a set monthly amount to you (currently EUR 992 per month). This system ensures that international students have enough money to cover living expenses for their first year in Germany.

Why Is It Required?

The German government wants assurance that international students will not become a financial burden. The blocked account is the most common way to demonstrate financial self-sufficiency. Without it, your visa application will be rejected.

Amount Required for 2026

For 2026, you need to deposit EUR 11,904 (EUR 992 × 12 months). This amount is reviewed annually and may increase in future years.

How to Open a Blocked Account

There are three main providers:

Expatrio

  • 100% online process — fastest option
  • Account opening fee: approximately EUR 49
  • Monthly fee: EUR 5
  • Deposit via bank transfer or credit card
  • Recognition by most German embassies
  • Provides insurance packages as add-ons
  • Website: expatrio.com

Fintiba

  • Online process, recommended by many German embassies
  • Account opening fee: approximately EUR 89
  • Monthly fee: EUR 4.90
  • Deposit via bank transfer or credit card
  • Well-established and widely recognised
  • Provides bundled health insurance options
  • Website: fintiba.com

Deutsche Bank

  • Traditional option — requires visiting a branch or using their online portal
  • Account opening fee: approximately EUR 50–150
  • No monthly fee
  • Slower processing time
  • Most widely accepted by all embassies
  • Requires more paperwork

Documents Needed

  • Passport copy
  • University admission letter (or proof of application)
  • Completed application form
  • Proof of address (your home country address)

Monthly Release

After you arrive in Germany and register your address (Anmeldung), you can activate the monthly release. The bank will transfer EUR 992 per month to your regular German bank account. You need a standard German bank account (e.g., Commerzbank, N26, ING) to receive these transfers.

Processing Time

Online providers (Expatrio, Fintiba) typically process your account within 1–3 business days. Deutsche Bank may take 1–2 weeks. Start this process at least 6 weeks before your visa appointment.

12. Health Insurance

Health insurance is mandatory for all students in Germany. You cannot enrol at a university without it, and you cannot get a visa either.

Public Health Insurance (Gesetzliche Krankenversicherung)

Public insurance is the most common option for students. Major providers include:

  • TK (Techniker Krankenkasse) — the largest and most popular
  • AOK — regional providers across Germany
  • Barmer — another large provider
  • DAK — widely available

Monthly cost for students: EUR 110 to EUR 120 (this includes a supplementary care contribution of approximately EUR 12–15). Public insurance covers doctor visits, hospital stays, dental emergencies, mental health treatment, and basic vision care.

Private Health Insurance

Private insurance can be cheaper (EUR 30–60 per month) but has limitations. It may not cover pre-existing conditions, may require co-payments, and may not be accepted by all doctors. Private insurance is generally only recommended for students over 30 years old, since public insurance becomes more expensive after age 30.

Under 30 vs Over 30

  • Under 30: Public insurance is recommended and affordable. You are automatically enrolled in public insurance at the student rate.
  • Over 30: Public insurance costs more (adult rate, EUR 200+/month). Many over-30 students opt for private insurance, but check that it meets university requirements.

Public insurance from providers like TK can be arranged online before you arrive in Germany.

13. Student Visa Process

This is often the most stressful part of the process. Here is everything you need to know.

Visa Type

Non-EU students need a National (Type D) Visa for the purpose of studying. This visa is valid for the duration of your studies and allows you to work part-time.

Where to Apply

Apply at the German embassy or consulate in your country of residence. Processing is handled by the visa section of the embassy. You must apply in person.

Required Documents

  • Valid passport — at least 6 months validity beyond your planned stay, with at least 2 blank pages
  • APS certificate — if you are from China, India, Vietnam, or Mongolia
  • University admission letter (Zulassungsbescheid) — the original or certified copy
  • Blocked account proof — confirmation from Expatrio, Fintiba, or Deutsche Bank showing EUR 11,904 deposited
  • Health insurance — confirmation from a German public or approved private insurer
  • Motivation letter — explaining why you chose Germany and your programme
  • CV — chronological, in Europass or tabular format
  • Language certificate — IELTS, TOEFL, TestDaF, DSH, or other accepted test
  • Academic transcripts and certificates — original and copies
  • Biometric passport photos — 2 recent photos meeting German biometric standards
  • Visa application form — filled out and signed
  • Visa fee: EUR 75 (paid in local currency)

Processing Time

Processing times vary significantly by embassy:

  • Pakistan: 6 to 10 weeks (German Embassy Islamabad)
  • India: 4 to 8 weeks (German Embassy New Delhi, Consulates Mumbai, Bangalore, Chennai, Kolkata)
  • Bangladesh: 6 to 10 weeks (German Embassy Dhaka)
  • Nigeria: 6 to 12 weeks
  • General estimate: 4 to 12 weeks

Apply at least 3 months before your programme starts. Summer months (June–August) are the busiest — apply even earlier if possible.

Visa Interview Tips

  • Be clear about your study plans — know your programme name, university, and why you chose them
  • Know your finances — how much is in your blocked account, who is funding you
  • Explain your post-graduation plans — Germany values students who intend to contribute to the economy
  • Be honest and concise — do not give long, rambling answers
  • Bring all documents in order — organised documents show you are serious

Common Rejection Reasons

  • Insufficient financial proof (blocked account not properly funded)
  • Missing APS certificate
  • Language certificate does not meet requirements
  • Documents not properly certified or translated
  • Suspicion that the applicant does not genuinely intend to study
  • Criminal record or previous immigration violations

If Your Visa Is Delayed

If processing takes longer than expected and your semester is about to start:

  • Contact the embassy to check the status
  • Ask your university if you can start late (many allow arrival within the first 4–6 weeks)
  • Some universities allow you to enrol remotely and attend online classes until you arrive

14. Living in Germany — Practical Guide

Moving to a new country involves a lot of practical adjustments. Here is what you need to know.

Finding Accommodation

Studentenwohnheim (Student Halls of Residence)

The cheapest option at EUR 200 to EUR 400 per month. Run by the Studentenwerk (student services). Rooms are typically shared (2–3 people share a kitchen and bathroom). Apply early — there is often a waiting list. Apply through the Studentenwerk of your university as soon as you receive your admission letter.

WG (Wohngemeinschaft — Shared Flat)

A shared flat with other students. Cost: EUR 300 to EUR 600 per month for a room. The most common housing type for students. Find WGs on:

  • WG-Gesucht.de — the largest WG platform
  • eBay Kleinanzeigen (now Kleinanzeigen.de) — classified ads
  • Facebook groups — search for "WG [city name]"
  • University notice boards — physical and online

Private Flat

Your own apartment: EUR 500 to EUR 900 per month depending on city and size. More expensive and harder to find. Requires a SCHUFA credit check and usually a deposit of 2–3 months' rent (Kaution).

Tip: Start looking for accommodation 3–4 months before you arrive. The housing market in popular university cities (especially Munich, Berlin, and Hamburg) is competitive.

Food and Daily Expenses

  • Food: EUR 200–300 per month. Cooking at home is much cheaper than eating out. Student canteens (Mensen) offer meals for EUR 3–5.
  • Mobile phone: EUR 10–20 per month for a prepaid SIM card. Providers: Aldi Talk, Lebara, Lycamobile, Telekom.
  • Internet: EUR 20–30 per month for a home connection (often included in WG rent).
  • Books and supplies: EUR 30–50 per semester. Many textbooks are available in the library or as PDFs.
  • Entertainment: EUR 50–100 per month. Students get discounts at cinemas, museums, and cultural events.

Banking

Open a German bank account as soon as you arrive. Recommended options:

  • N26 — fully online, English-language app, free basic account
  • Commerzbank — physical branches, good student account with free debit card
  • ING — online bank with good rates
  • Deutsche Bank — traditional, widely accepted

You need your Anmeldung (address registration) to open most German bank accounts. Do the Anmeldung within 14 days of arriving at your address.

Public Transport

The semester ticket included in your semester contribution gives you free or heavily discounted public transport. In most cities, this covers buses, trams, U-Bahn (underground), and S-Bahn (suburban trains). Some semester tickets cover the entire state (e.g., Brandenburg, Saxony).

15. Working While Studying

Most international students in Germany work part-time. Here are the rules and your options.

Work Limits

  • During the semester: You can work up to 120 full days or 240 half days per year. A "full day" is any day where you work more than 4 hours.
  • During semester breaks: You can work full-time (up to 120 days equivalent).
  • Research assistants (HiWi/Wissenschaftliche Hilfskraft): Work at your university as a research or teaching assistant does not count toward the 120-day limit. This is the best option for most students.

Types of Student Jobs

Mini-Job

A mini-job pays up to EUR 538 per month tax-free. Employers pay a flat tax on mini-jobs. This is the simplest way to earn pocket money without complex tax obligations.

Werkstudent (Working Student)

The best type of student job. A Werkstudent position is a paid job in your field of study. Typical wages: EUR 12 to EUR 18 per hour, which translates to EUR 800–1,600 per month for part-time work. Many Werkstudent positions offer relevant work experience that strengthens your CV. Popular employers include BMW, Siemens, Bosch, SAP, and Deutsche Bank.

Tutoring

Teaching other students or school pupils. Pay: EUR 12–20 per hour. You can tutor at the university or privately.

Student Assistant (HiWi)

Work at your university — assisting professors with research, grading, or administrative tasks. Pay: EUR 12–15 per hour. Does not count toward the work limit. Excellent for academic CVs.

Service Industry

Working in restaurants, cafes, bars, or shops. Pay: EUR 12–15 per hour. Common but less relevant for your career.

Tax Implications

  • Mini-job earnings (under EUR 538/month) are tax-free
  • Regular student jobs are taxed, but students with low incomes can get a tax refund at the end of the year
  • Check with the Finanzamt (tax office) about your specific situation

How to Find Student Jobs

  • University job boards — check the career centre and departmental notice boards
  • Job portals: Indeed.de, StepStone.de, Jobvector.de
  • Company websites: Direct application to companies in your field
  • Networking: Attend career fairs, join student organisations, and talk to professors

16. After Graduation — Staying in Germany

One of the biggest advantages of studying in Germany is the clear pathway to staying and working after graduation.

18-Month Job-Seeker Visa

After completing your degree, you can convert your student residence permit to an 18-month job-seeker visa (or residence permit for job seeking). During this period:

  • You can work any job (even unskilled work) to support yourself while job searching
  • You must have health insurance throughout
  • You must have sufficient funds (or be employed)
  • Once you find a qualified job related to your degree, you switch to a work permit

Work Permit and EU Blue Card

Once you find a job in Germany, you can apply for:

  • Work permit (Arbeitserlaubnis): For any qualified job in your field
  • EU Blue Card: For highly qualified jobs with a minimum salary of EUR 45,300 per year (or EUR 41,040 for shortage occupations like IT, engineering, mathematics, and natural sciences)

The EU Blue Card is a special work permit for highly skilled professionals and offers additional benefits like easier family reunification and a faster path to permanent residence.

Permanent Residence

  • Standard route: After 4 years of legal residence in Germany (including study time)
  • Fast track: After 21 months if you have a B1 German language certificate and a qualified job

Citizenship

Germany allows dual citizenship since 2024 reforms. You can apply for German citizenship after:

  • 8 years of legal residence (standard)
  • 6 years with exceptional integration (B2 German, civic engagement, etc.)

In-Tech Startup Visa

Germany also offers a start-up visa for international graduates who want to found a company. You need a viable business plan and backing from a recognised incubator or accelerator.

Job Market Tips for International Graduates

  • In-demand fields: Engineering (mechanical, electrical, automotive), IT/computer science, data science, healthcare, business/finance, and natural sciences
  • German language: While many tech companies operate in English, learning German to B2/C1 level dramatically increases your job prospects
  • Networking: Attend career fairs (Absolventenkongress), join alumni networks, and use LinkedIn actively
  • Working student positions: Many Werkstudent roles convert to full-time positions after graduation

17. Common Mistakes to Avoid

These are the most frequent errors that derail study-in-Germany plans:

1. Applying too late. Start your preparation at least 12 months before your intended start date. The APS process, language testing, university applications, and visa process all take time.

2. Not getting APS when required. Students from China, India, Vietnam, and Mongolia who skip the APS step are automatically rejected. Start this early.

3. Confusing Fachhochschule with Universität. Both are excellent, but they serve different purposes. Fachhochschulen are not "lesser" — they are more practical and often more directly employable.

4. Insufficient blocked account balance. The amount must be exactly EUR 11,904 (or whatever the current year requires). Partial deposits are not accepted.

5. Ignoring language requirements. Even for English-taught programmes, basic German (A1–B1) makes daily life dramatically easier and is essential for part-time work.

6. Not using uni-assist. Applying directly to universities that require uni-assist results in automatic rejection. Always check which application channel the university requires.

7. Choosing an expensive city without budgeting. Munich is wonderful, but living costs are 50–70% higher than in Leipzig or Dresden. Choose a city you can afford.

8. Delaying health insurance. You need health insurance confirmed before your visa appointment. Do not leave this to the last minute.

9. Missing scholarship deadlines. DAAD and other scholarships have fixed annual deadlines. Missing them means waiting another year. See our common scholarship mistakes guide.

10. Not learning German at all. Even if your programme is in English, not speaking German limits your social life, job prospects, and ability to navigate bureaucracy. Start learning before you arrive.

Frequently asked questions

Can I study in Germany for free?+

Yes. Most public universities in Germany charge no tuition for international students, including for bachelor's and many master's programmes. You only pay a semester contribution of EUR 150 to 350, which covers student services and a public transport ticket. The only exception is Baden-Württemberg, which charges EUR 1,500 per semester for non-EU students.

What is the APS certificate and do I need it?+

The APS (Akademische Prüfstelle) certificate is required for students from China, India, Vietnam and Mongolia. It verifies that your academic documents are genuine and that you are qualified for university study in Germany. You must get APS before applying to German universities. The process includes document verification and an interview.

How much money do I need in a blocked account for Germany?+

For 2026, you need EUR 11,904 in a blocked account (Sperrkonto). This proves you can cover living expenses for one year. The money is released in monthly instalments of EUR 992 while you live in Germany. You can open a blocked account with Expatrio, Fintiba, or Deutsche Bank.

Do I need IELTS to study in Germany?+

For English-taught programmes, most universities accept IELTS (typically 6.0-6.5), TOEFL iBT (79-100), or PTE Academic (55-65). Some universities accept Duolingo. For German-taught programmes, you need TestDaF 4 or DSH-2. Many German universities do not require IELTS if your previous degree was taught in English.

What are the best universities in Germany for international students?+

Top universities include Technical University of Munich (TUM), Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich (LMU), Heidelberg University, RWTH Aachen, Humboldt University of Berlin, University of Freiburg, University of Göttingen, and Free University of Berlin. For English-taught programmes, look at University of Mannheim, TU Berlin, University of Cologne, and Jacobs University Bremen.

Can I work while studying in Germany?+

Yes. International students can work up to 120 full days or 240 half days per year. During semester breaks, you can work full-time. Working as a research assistant at your university does not count toward this limit. Many students earn EUR 400-800 per month through part-time work.

How long does the German student visa take?+

Processing times vary by embassy: typically 4 to 12 weeks. Apply at least 3 months before your programme starts. The visa fee is EUR 75. You need the admission letter, blocked account proof, health insurance, and APS certificate (if applicable).

Can I stay in Germany after graduation?+

Yes. After graduation, you can convert your student residence permit to an 18-month job-seeker visa. During this period, you can work any job while looking for employment in your field. Once you find a qualified job, you can switch to a work permit or EU Blue Card. After 4 years of legal residence, you can apply for permanent residence.

What scholarships are available for studying in Germany?+

Major scholarships include DAAD scholarships (various programmes), Deutschlandstipendium (EUR 300/month), Erasmus Mundus Joint Masters, Heinrich Böll Foundation, Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung, and university-specific scholarships. Many universities also offer tuition fee waivers and merit awards.

What documents do I need for a German student visa?+

Required documents: valid passport, APS certificate (for Chinese/Indian/Vietnamese/Mongolian students), university admission letter, blocked account proof, health insurance, motivation letter, CV, language certificate, and proof of academic qualifications. Some embassies also require a biometric photo and visa application form.
