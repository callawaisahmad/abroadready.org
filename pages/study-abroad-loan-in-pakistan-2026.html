Study Abroad Loan in Pakistan: Banks and Guide 2026

🗓️ Sep 14, 2026⏱️ 30 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

When a Pakistani family decides their child will study abroad, the first question is never "which university?" — it is "how do we pay for it?" If scholarships fall through and savings are not enough, the obvious next step is a study abroad loan in Pakistan. The idea is simple: borrow the dollars now, earn them back after graduation. The practice is harder, because Pakistani banks price education financing against a benchmark that moves, demand strict documentation, and rarely fund the full cost of a foreign degree. This guide is a plain-language handbook to getting a study abroad loan in Pakistan in 2026 — which banks offer what, how markup and repayment actually work, what MPOWER-style international loans change, why Islamic financing structures behave differently, and the alternatives that sometimes rescue families from a bad loan.

Reading this guide will not make you a banker. What it will do is arm you to walk into any branch without being mystified by the jargon, and — more importantly — to walk out politely if the deal on the table is worse than what you can build yourself. A study loan, used well, is a bridge between two income curves. Used badly, it is a 20% drag on a family's finances for the next decade. Everything below is written to make sure yours is the former.

Who actually offers a study abroad loan in Pakistan?

The honest map of lending for overseas education in Pakistan is thinner than most families hope. The big commercial banks — Habib Bank (HBL), United Bank (UBL), MCB, Faysal Bank, Bank Alfalah and others — do not run a dedicated "overseas study loan" product with published terms the way the Indian banks do. What they offer in practice is consumer and personal finance (personal loans, "consumer" loan accounts) at a markup linked to the central bank's policy benchmark, plus secured financing against property, gold or bank deposits, and, for some customers, an education-oriented personal loan routed through consumer-banking teams. That reality is not a scandal; it simply means the "education loan" from a Pakistani bank is usually a general-purpose loan dressed for the study abroad case.

The second, genuinely different source is international education lenders. MPOWER Financing, which operates in Pakistan, lends to Pakistani students for postgraduate study in the US and Canada without any collateral or co-signer, basing approval on academic potential and charging a fixed rate (roughly 10% in 2026 marketing) with a free application and no prepayment penalty. Prodigy Finance plays a similar role for professional graduate programmes in a wider set of countries. These lenders matter because they solve the two problems Pakistani banks cannot: you have no local credit history and no collateral. The catch is their university whitelists — the college must be on their eligible list — and their funding windows open and close, so a student should check availability early.

The State Bank context every borrower should know

Borrowing in Rupees to pay a dollar tuition bill comes with a hidden third actor: the State Bank of Pakistan's exchange-rate and remittance framework. When your loan is disbursed in PKR and sent abroad, the bank handling the transfer must follow SBP letter-of-credit and education-remittance rules, which require the university's fee schedule, your admission documents, and proper TTS documentation. The practical effect is that you cannot simply hand the loan cash to a cousin to carry; the money must move through a bank with evidence that it is for education. The proof of funds guide on this site explains the documentation reality for families, and the same discipline applies the day your bank disburses your loan.

How the markup works, in plain language

Every conventional loan in Pakistan is priced as a "markup" rather than an "interest rate," but they are economically the same thing, and the cost is quoted as a percentage built from two numbers: the benchmark (usually the policy rate or KIBOR — the interbank lending rate published daily) and the bank's spread on top of it. In 2026, with the policy rate around 11 to 12%, a personal loan at KIBOR plus a spread commonly prices at 14 to 20% effective, and secured loans against property price several points cheaper. Your "EMI" is the monthly repayment that pays both the principal and that markup over the tenure.

Three features of Pakistani loans surprise families most. First, the markup is floating unless you fix it — if the policy rate rises, your EMI rises. Second, early repayment usually attracts a penalty or pre-payment charges, so "I'll just pay it off when I land a job" is expensive. Third, the tenure squeezes: consumer personal loans run two to four years, not a decade, which means the monthly payment is huge relative to the amount borrowed. A PKR 5 million loan at 18% over 3 years costs around PKR 181,000 a month — a figure most households cannot sustain unless the student is already earning. That, more than the terminology, is why this guide constantly pushes scholarships and savings from the front.

Collateral, guarantors and the documents banks demand

Pakistani banks will finance a study abroad loan when you can show them repayment exit: collateral, a guarantor, or a stable salary. The documents list below is the floor in almost every branch, whether you walk in for a personal loan or secured education financing.

  • CNIC and passport copies of the student and the guarantor/co-borrower.
  • The university admission letter and the official fee schedule for the full programme.
  • Bank statements of the account in which the fees will be shown, ideally one year of history.
  • Salary slips or income proof, tax returns, and — for secured loans — property valuation or title documents, or a fixed-deposit pledge.
  • For international transfers, the completed education remittance and foreign-exchange forms.
  • A credit-bureau check against the (often limited) credit file of a young borrower.

Banks like property as collateral, value it conservatively (typically 50 to 60% of market), and disburse against the fee milestone rather than in one lump. The practical consequence: your loan size is capped by the value of the family's secured assets far more than by the university's bill, which is why families should size the loan need honestly before promising the top tier of a degree.

Islamic financing structures when interest matters to you

For observant families, conventional bank markup is a deal-breaker, and the correct response is not "no loan" but "the right kind of financing." Islamic banks in Pakistan — Meezan Bank being the largest and most visible — structure the same economic need through Shariah-compliant contracts. The two you will see on an education-financing file are diminishing musharakah (declining partnership: the bank buys the asset with you, rents its share, and you buy it back in instalments, so the bank earns rent/profit rather than interest) and Ijarah (lease), with takaful (Islamic insurance) replacing conventional insurance. The practical result is a profit rate quoted rather than a markup percentage, collateral that often takes the shape of the financed asset, and a genuinely different legal spine, even though the monthly cash numbers land in a similar band.

Shariah-compliance is a professional matter, not a slogan, so before signing any "Islamic" study-financing agreement, ask the bank's Shariah board for the contract structure in writing and have a scholar you trust read it. A bank wearing an Islamic label that structures a plain mark-up loan is selling compliance theatre, and the correct response is the same financial sense you apply to any contract: price it, compare it, then decide.

The repayment trap and the exchange-rate risk

Two risks destroy borrowers who took a study loan in Pakistan, and both are worth a paragraph each. The first is the timing trap: you borrow at 18%, leave, graduate, and land a junior salary — and only then realise the loan's total cost was 40 to 60% above the headline amount once markup over tenure is counted. The second is the currency trap: you borrowed rupees, but the dollar cost of your studies is what drives the fee; if the rupee weakens against the dollar during your programme, the additional rupee cost of future fees lands on you regardless of your loan. Families insulate themselves from both by borrowing the minimum, fixing tenures they can actually service from Pakistan, and layering the loan only on top of a core of scholarship and savings rather than as the whole bridge.

If you find yourself at the point where the loan IS the whole bridge, this article's honest recommendation is to stop and reconsider the destination and the degree, because one extra year of savings or one additional funded-scholarship application (the full menu is in our scholarships for Pakistani students guide) is worth more to your family than any lender's marketing can claim. A study loan is a tool, not a strategy.

Which degrees and countries actually repay a study loan?

A study loan is only sound if the degree converts into an income that outruns the debt. The honest ranking of repayment curves for Pakistani students, based on the market reality Pakistani graduates actually meet, runs like this: fully funded or near-fully-funded degrees (Germany public universities, HEC overseas scholarship, CSC, Commonwealth) have no loan at all and win by default; elite professional degrees in the US, UK, Canada, Australia and the Gulf repay a loan quickly because their starting salaries are high; degrees from mid-tier universities without a clear employment pathway repay slowly precisely when the loan is largest. The mistake families make is borrowing against the dream of a foreign internship that was never guaranteed. Use the cost guide to price the true total, and ask a brutal question before choosing the plan: does the employment reality in this country for this specific degree comfortably exceed this specific monthly EMI reachable from abroad? If the honest answer is no, either fix the plan (scholarship, cheaper destination) or fix the degree.

Destinations where Pakistani graduates genuinely convert to earnings quickly — the UK post-study route for STEM and health, the Gulf's immediate job market, Australia's skilled pathway for health/engineering, Germany's engineering internships — are the ones worth financing with borrowed money. Destinations or degrees with weak post-study demand should be funded only by savings or scholarships, never by a loan that keeps accruing while the graduate hunts.

What convinces a bank, and what gets you rejected

Bankers approve or decline a study-abroad request on roughly five signals. The first is stability of income: a salaried guarantor or co-borrower with at least a year of history beats a freelancer's variable income every time. The second is collateral: a property file with clean title, recent valuation and no encumbrances transforms the deal. The third is repayment capacity: the bank computes your full debt-to-income position, so undisclosed existing obligations surface on the bureau report. The fourth is the quality of the education file: a visible tuition schedule, a published university fee page and a clean admission letter beat a typed "custom quotation". The fifth — and the place most applicants fail — is documentation discipline: mismatched names on CNIC and sponsor, photocopies instead of attested statements, or an uncleared credit-bureau record. Prepare the file as if you were the banker, and you will be treated like a serious client rather than a walk-in.

Notice that the admission letter itself is both the most important and the least costly item in the file: universities issue it free, and its presence is what separates "personal consumption loan" from "education financing" in a bank's mind. The practical version of this tip is to obtain the official fee schedule PDF and the university's bank-account details from the university directly, because those two documents also happen to be exactly what the Pakistani proof-of-funds system wants to see for the visa file.

The co-borrower, the guarantor and the family dimension

Very few young Pakistani applicants have a credit file or a salary history strong enough to borrow alone, which is why the co-borrower/guarantor structure matters. In practice the parent (usually the father, sometimes a sibling) becomes the co-borrower: the loan is sanctioned against their income, their bureau record is checked, and their signature carries the legal weight. This is entirely normal, but the emotional economics deserve a paragraph: this loan now sits on the parent's balance sheet at the same time the family may be helping with living costs, and the parent's own retirement and emergency buffer usually stand right behind it. Banks approve such structures precisely because they collect on the parent's income first, not the student's future. Sign with full awareness of whose salary is actually at stake, and size the amount so that the parent's essential obligations are never crowded out — because if the family cannot carry a six-month emergency without this loan money, the whole house of cards is too tight.

What MPOWER and Prodigy really require

International no-collateral lenders have become genuine options for Pakistani students, and their requirements are different enough to matter. MPOWER lends to Pakistani nationals for postgraduate study in the US and Canada at exactly the eligible universities it lists, approves without collateral or co-signer, prices a fixed rate (roughly 10% in its 2026 marketing with automatic-pay discount), and is built around your future earning potential rather than your parent's. Its constraints: eligible university whitelist, the degree level (postgraduate, generally), and funding windows that open and close (in 2026 it was temporarily at capacity and running a waitlist). Prodigy Finance serves a wider set of countries and degrees but concentrates on professional schools (MBA, public health, engineering masters, law) and prices variably against its fund. Both are real, both charge in USD with no cosigner, and both should be compared to a Pakistani secured loan using the effective rate on the full tenure — because a fixed 10% international loan is usually cheaper than a 16–20% Pakistani consumer loan even before you count the inability of a Pakistani family to borrow USD at all.

Refinancing, top-ups and managing the loan once approved

A loan is a relationship, not a one-time event, and the silently powerful questions are what happens after approval. The repayment schedule should ladder with your earning timeline: Pakistani banks that offer a grace period during study charge the markup capitalising in that window, so "deferred" repayment is expensive even when it feels painless. If your circumstances change, the options are a top-up (a second sanction against the same collateral, usually available only while the original is performing well), a refinance (moving the balance to a bank with a lower rate, worth doing whenever rates fall materially), or restructuring under hardship policies. The relationship managers who helped you borrow are rarely the ones who help you restructure, so keep every document, every clause and every rate letter in one folder. And the strategic note: the moment you have income in a foreign currency, compare a full early settlement against the forward exchange rate — because paying off a PKR loan with strengthened dollars is often the smartest single move a graduate makes.

Alternatives that out-rank the loan

The final funding section is the one this guide hopes you remember longest: the fully funded scholarship is the superior instrument in almost every case, and Pakistani households dramatically under-exploit it. The scholarships for Pakistani students to study abroad guide documents the full menu — HEC overseas awards, CSC for China, Commonwealth, Chevening, DAAD, Turkiye Burslari, Fulbright, and dozens of country pairs — and the study abroad without a full scholarship guide builds the realistic hybrid plan of scholarship plus savings plus modest part-time income. The honest ranking for a Pakistani family is: fully funded scholarship first; savings and family support second; a hybrid of scholarship and a modest secured loan third; and a full-cost loan against income-only expectations last. Every step you take up that ranking is a step toward the family whose children study abroad without the graduate's first salary being already spent before it arrives.

The six-step funding decision, in one screen

Before any family acts on a single quote, run these six steps in order: (1) price the true total cost of the degree, including living costs and the forecast and contingency; (2) exhaust the funded-scholarship list for your exact case; (3) size the family's real, sustainable contribution; (4) only then compute the gap; (5) shop the gap across a Pakistani secured loan, an international no-collateral loan where eligible, and a Shariah-compliant structure if required — obtaining written quotes and computing the effective rate and total repayable yourself; and (6) sign only when the monthly burden is serviceable from abroad, the total-cost riddle is understood in writing, and the exit (repayment, early settlement, refinance) is planned in advance. A study abroad loan in Pakistan is a legitimate, widely used, and occasionally brilliant engine for a family's future — provided it is the last link in a funding chain built on scholarships and savings, not the first.

Reading a loan agreement line by line

The difference between a fair and a punishing study loan is rarely the headline number; it is the small print stacked around it. The five lines you must read twice in any Pakistani loan agreement are the rate basis (floating KIBOR-linked review frequency — quarterly, six-monthly, annual — because each review moves your EMI), the capitalisation rule (whether unpaid markup is added to the principal, which turns deferred repayment into compounding), the early-settlement clause (the penalty that makes "I'll repay when I'm earning" expensive), the insurance/takaful premiums bundled into the EMI, and the foreclosure/default and late-payment penalties, which in Pakistan are charged at punitive multiples. Tick each one against the written quote before you sign; the bank's relationship manager will not volunteer a spreadsheet, so bring your own and ask the questions directly.

Also read the security document, not just the loan one. A secured loan is registered against the collateral, and the difference between "equitable mortgage" and "sale-deed mortgage" matters at the point you want to sell the property or transfer the loan. Less glamorous but equally important: confirm who the account's signatories are, because a joint co-borrower who dies or leaves the country changes the whole administration. The discipline equals what you would apply to any contract worth millions of rupees — and a study loan, with its markup, tenure and collateral, is exactly that.

If the exchange rate moves against your family

Families borrow rupees, universities invoice and tuition is paid in dollars, and the gap between those two currencies is a risk the loan paperwork never mentions. Picture the mechanics: your loan was sanctioned and the first tranche converted at one rate, but your second-year fees convert at whatever the market rate is when you pay them. If the rupee weakens by 10% across your programme, you have effectively borrowed 10% more in rupees for the same dollar tuition. The partial defences are the same ones a treasurer would use: convert and hold currency when the rate is favourable (through the banks' customers' foreign currency accounts), write your fee plan in dollars and your budget in rupees so the family sees the exposure, and keep an exchange-rate buffer in the funding model — the cost and budget guide shows exactly how the buffer line works. A family whose plan assumes a stable rate and no buffer has no plan, only a hope; and hope is not a hedging instrument.

Two families, one decision: a case study in loan sizing

A pair of anonymised cases from the guidance art teaches more than a hundred warnings, so here are two. Family A borrowed the full cost of a four-year UK bachelor's — PKR 12 million with a three-to-five-year personal-loan amortisation at 18% effective — because the fee scare came late and scholarships seemed "too much work". The monthly pressure at that tenure made the family's accountant beg; the student's whole degree ran under the shadow of the bill; and the graduate now finances the EMI against a junior salary. Family B, same destination, refused the full-cost loan and borrowed only the gap after a scholarship, savings and a cheap ten-month allowance: PKR 3 million over five years at a lower secured rate. Both sailed on the same professional future; one clears the debt in four years, the other is still unwinding the interest eight years later. The difference was not which bank offered the better rate — it was the size of the decision, made before signing, with the no-full-scholarship funding guide open on the screen.

Banks, fintech and Islamic wings: who serves students best

The Pakistani financing market for educations is layered, and the right counter for each family is not the loudest brand but the structure that fits. The big commercial banks win on branch network, secured-product depth and the ability to move money abroad cleanly; their personal loans are standardised, priced on KIBOR plus spread, and reliable. The Islamic wings — most visibly Meezan Bank — solve the compliance question with diminishing-musharakah and Ijarah structures at comparable profit rates, which matters to the observant families who refuse conventional markup. The international no-collateral fintech lenders (MPOWER, Prodigy) operate from abroad, lend in dollars, and serve the whitelisted-university postgraduate market nobody in Pakistan can service. There is no single champion; there is only a fit, and the fit is decided by collateral available, family income, religious constraint, destination and degree level. Read the comparison table earlier in this guide one more time with your actual numbers in hand, and you will find the answer is already there.

Your credit file in Pakistan and abroad

Borrowing for study creates a credit trail on both sides of your future. In Pakistan, the loan appears in the system (the debt-bureau record shared by banks), and a clean repayment record becomes the foundation for your first car, home and business financing — the same record is immediately damaged by late payments, so set the automatic debit on day one. Abroad, the Pakistani credit history usually does not travel, which is why the first years in a new country rebuild a fresh file; the discipline of paying the study loan on time from abroad, with statements you can show, becomes the evidence base for your new-country credit. The practical habit that serves both: keep every bank statement, every repayment receipt and every written rate review in one folder — because it is simultaneously your proof-of-funds exhibit, your credit evidence and your tax record. The proof of funds guide documents how the same folder serves the visa side of the story.

Talking to your bank: the ten questions worth asking

Before any family signs, put these ten questions directly to the relationship manager and write the answers down: (1) what is the exact effective rate including every fee; (2) how often is it reviewed and by which benchmark; (3) how much is the total repayable on this figure; (4) what happens if I want to repay early; (5) what security is registered against this; (6) who are the co-borrowers and what happens if one leaves the country; (7) does the schedule have a study-period grace and what does capitalisation do to it; (8) which insurance/takaful is bundled and can the family decline it; (9) how is the money moved abroad and what documentation covers the transfer; (10) what are the default, restructuring and rescheduling options if income collapses. A bank that answers all ten in writing, alongside the spreadsheet you built yourself, is a bank that deserves the family's business. A bank that waves its hand at question four is telling you, in its own advertising, exactly how painful the exit will be.

The hidden costs inside a quoted EMI

The monthly EMI a banker quotes is rarely "just" markup. Stripped apart, the bits that sit inside the number are the base profit/markup on the outstanding principal, the bunded insurance or takaful premium (often mandatory for the guarantor's life coverage), administrative and processing charges recouped across the tenure, and any account-maintenance or late-payment margins. The family that signs without decomposing the figure cannot compare two banks' offers honestly, because the quoted rates rarely cover the same bundle. The way to compare properly is identical to comparing electricity tariffs: put the full repayment schedule of each offer beside the other, line by line, and subtract the fees. In practice a 17% "all-inclusive" quote can be cheaper than a 14% quote that loads six extra charges, and only the written breakdown reveals which. Keep both schedules in the family folder; the budget discipline guide is the companion that turns them into a one-page decision.

Opportunity cost, stress and the family buffer

Hidden beneath every study loan's rupee cost is a quieter price: what the money stops doing. The loan serviced from a parent's salary is salary that no longer tops up the retirement fund, the younger sibling's fees or the family's emergency cushion, and a Pakistani extended-family economy tends to flow through exactly those same lines. Stress compounds the arithmetic — a family that has borrowed to the limit is fragile to a job loss, an illness or another child's admission, and the researcher knows that financial-stress families make worse tuition decisions, not better ones. The mature plan therefore sizes the loan as one pillar of a four-pillar structure — scholarship, savings, family help, and a modest loan — never as the single bridge, and always paired with a visible emergency buffer line. That single habit, the buffer, separates the families who shrug at a bad month from the ones whose whole plan collapses in one.

Decision rules: when the loan is right, when it is not

Distilled to rules, the loan decision is simpler than the emotion around it. Take the loan when: the degree's realistic first-job earnings comfortably exceed the monthly EMI reachable from abroad; the total borrow is under half the family's sustainable assets; and the security does not risk the family home being the only asset standing behind the loan. Do not take the loan when: the plan depends on the student financing the EMI from income that does not yet exist; the borrow strips the family's emergency buffer; or the destination degree lacks a demonstrated employment pathway. Every loan rejection story on the WhatsApp groups is one of these rules violated — usually the first. Apply the rules with the scholarship search running in parallel, and the family keeps both doors open until the last signature.

A financial-literacy planner for the young borrower

Finally, the loan is the young borrower's first real lesson in money, and that is not a footnote — it is the point. Use the study-loan paperwork to learn the terms the rest of your financial life will reuse: effective rate versus headline rate, capitalisation, amortisation, security, default and the spread between a savings and a loan rate. Keep a personal monthly budget, in the currency of the country of study, from semester one; track it against the plan; and build the habit of paying the loan's scheduled amount on automatic debit so the record stays clean in every system it touches. The reader who finishes this guide and can explain markup, exchange-rate risk and effective rate to a relative has already extracted more value from the journey than most borrowers ever will — and that is the truest return on the whole venture. Study aboard loans from Pakistan are a powerful, legitimate tool when sized honestly; the borrower who understand them is the borrower who uses them well and clears them on schedule.

The student side of the funding: income, discipline and the top-up

The loan belongs to the family, but the funding plan is a joint document, and the student's own contribution — paid work where the visa permits, scholarship top-ups, and a strict monthly budget — is what turns a stretched loan into a manageable one. The reader abroad should never treat the loan as an unlimited account; every month's withdrawal goes on the family's bill, and the wisest students draw the budgeted amount on schedule and nothing more. The genuinely diligent students also track part-time income against the plan, keep every payslip, and bring the semester's surplus home at the end — the surplus habit alone can strip a year off the repayment schedule. The part-time job guide explains exactly which visa regimes allow the work the plan depends on, and the no-scholarship funding guide shows the full hierarchy — grants, need-based aid, cheap-budget countries and loans in that order — so that the loan is never the first or the only door. The student who treats the loan as a professional relationship, budgeted, documented and repaid early, converts the family's trust into a win on both sides of the arrangement.

One-page loan math: a worksheet for the kitchen table

Condensed to a single page, the whole loan decision fits on a sheet most families could write in an afternoon. Column one: the total borrow in rupees. Column two: the effective rate after the fee breakdown. Column three: the ten-year total you will actually repay. Column four: the monthly EMI in the first, third and fifth years assuming the KIBOR corridor moves as the historical band suggests. Column five: the family's debt-to-income ratio after the loan (a ratio under 35% is the comfort zone; above 45% the calculator is lying). Column six: the exchange-rate impact if the rupee moves 10% against the dollar across the degree. And column seven: the trigger line — the emergency buffer that, if breached, triggers the early-settlement discussion with the bank. When those seven columns are written and the decision still survives them, the loan is doing its job: expanding a family's reach, not shrinking it. When any column turns red, the red column is the honest advisor the brochure never hired. Pair the worksheet with the proof-of-funds checklist and the family has both halves of the money story: what it costs and what it proves.

Protecting yourself: documents, scams and the fine print

Predatory behaviour in study-abroad financing takes two forms in Pakistan. The first is informal "agents" who lend dollars at family-friend terms with terrifying unwritten guarantees — avoid entirely, since there is no regulator to help you if the terms change. The second is banks whose relationship managers understate the total cost, capitalise interest, or attach insurance and processing fees you never approved. Both are neutralised by the same habits: get the full schedule of fees and the total payable in writing, compute the effective rate yourself, refuse to sign any document you have not read, and treat any promised "processing in days" with suspicion — the patterns are the same as those described in our scholarship scam guide, adapted to finance.

The single most valuable document you can carry into any bank is the equivalent of a fee-schedule photocopy and a written repayment projection you made yourself. When you can tell the manager the effective rate you expect and the total you will repay before he does, you have already won half the negotiation. And before you borrow a single rupee, revisit the options in how to find scholarships you are actually eligible for — because every fully funded scholarship a Pakistani student wins is a loan they never had to take.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get a study abroad loan in Pakistan?+

Yes, several Pakistani banks provide personal or education financing for overseas study, usually as an unsecured personal loan or a secured loan against property, salary or a guarantor, priced at a markup above the KIBOR benchmark. International lenders such as MPOWER also lend to Pakistani students for US and Canada study without collateral.

What is the interest rate on education loans from Pakistan?+

Rates follow Pakistan's policy benchmark (KIBOR was around 11.5 to 12% per annum in 2026), and banks add a spread, so the effective markup on an education-related loan typically lands between 14% and 22% depending on the bank, tenure, collateral and your credit profile.

Is a study loan halal?+

Conventional mark-up loans are interest-based, so if that matters to you, Islamic banks such as Meezan Bank structure the same financing through Shariah-compliant contracts like diminishing musharakah or Ijarah, with profit rates replacing interest and takaful replacing insurance.

Do I need collateral for a study abroad loan?+

Most bank loans for study abroad from Pakistan need either collateral (property or savings) or a guarantor and a salaried co-borrower. International no-collateral lenders like MPOWER finance solely on academic potential, but only for their eligible universities in the US and Canada.

What are alternatives to a study abroad loan in Pakistan?+

The order most families should follow: fully funded scholarships first, family savings and informal support second, bank financing third, and income during study last. The funded-scholarship programs for Pakistani students are documented in the scholarships guide on this site.
