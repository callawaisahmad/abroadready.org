Apply for Masters in Germany Step 1 Intake

🗓️ Sep 26, 2026⏱️ 30 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

Apply for masters in germany step 1 intake: the decision that comes before every document

Most students who search for "apply for masters in germany step 1 intake" want a form or a portal link. What they need first is a decision, because the intake you choose sets every deadline, document and appointment that follows: when portals open, which translations you must commission, and which universities are still accepting applications when you submit. Get it right and the rest is administration. Get it vague and you lose a full cycle to programmes that closed while you were deciding.

Germany is also a federal system, which means 16 states and hundreds of universities, each with its own portal, deadline habits and transcript expectations. There is no national admissions office, only the shortlist you build yourself from public databases, and that shortlist is the step most applicants give a week when it deserves a month.

This guide covers that month: winter versus summer, the real deadlines, searching the DAAD international programmes database and Hochschulkompass, building a five to eight university shortlist in three tiers, and the spreadsheet that holds it together. It is written for students applying from Pakistan and comparable countries, but the method works anywhere.

What Step 1 covers, and how long it should take

What exactly is Step 1 of a German masters application?

Step 1 has three deliverables and nothing else. An intake decision: winter semester, summer semester, or a small number of rolling programmes. A longlist of roughly fifteen to thirty programmes meeting your subject requirements. And a tiered shortlist of five to eight you will genuinely submit, split so you know which offers would surprise you. It does not include writing a letter, gathering transcripts, booking a test or applying for a visa. If you are writing a motivation letter in February for a programme that closed the previous January, you did not do Step 1. You skipped it.

How long should Step 1 take, and why do people rush it?

Budget three to four weeks of real work across two or three months: week one for intake research and database setup, week two for the longlist, week three for eligibility screening against official pages, week four for tiering and the tracking sheet. Add a week of slack, because you will find a programme in week four whose deadline you have not checked.

People rush because the search surface is enormous, one common subject returning hundreds of results needing individual judgement, and because they start in the wrong month, finding in August that competitive programmes closed between January and May. The twelve-month timeline further down shows the correct order of operations for a winter intake.

Winter or summer: making the apply for masters in germany step 1 intake decision

Can I apply for the summer intake?

Yes, but know what you are choosing. Germany runs two semesters: the winter semester, abbreviated WS or Wintersemester, and the summer semester, SS or Sommersemester. Winter is the main intake. Summer is real, but far fewer master's programmes run in English, and in many subjects nothing in English starts in April.

What exists in summer tends to be German-taught continuation degrees, teacher training programmes, part-time formats, and a few English-taught courses in business and some STEM fields. If summer is your only realistic option, that is fine. But choosing between an English-taught winter programme at a strong university and a German-taught summer programme at a similar one deserves more thought: language is a hard gate, and German-taught master's programmes usually want C1, not B2.

What are the actual semester dates?

The winter semester runs officially from 1 October to 31 March, the summer semester from 1 April to 30 September. Lecture periods sit inside them and vary by university: winter lectures usually start in the second half of October and finish in mid-February, summer lectures begin around mid-April and end in mid-July. Plan to arrive a few days before lectures rather than weeks, because standard accommodation contracts begin with the semester, not the calendar day.

How many programmes start in each intake?

Roughly three quarters of English-taught master's programmes in Germany start only in the winter semester. Summer starts are a minority, and rolling programmes a minority of those. Which intake should I choose if I want a DAAD scholarship?

Target the winter semester, almost certainly. Most DAAD funding lines, including the popular development-related postgraduate courses and university-administered scholarships, are built around the winter cycle, and fewer routes exist for an April start. Because scholarship deadlines fall earlier than admission deadlines, not later, treat the scholarship deadline as your real deadline. What about year-round and every-other-year programmes?

Both exist in small numbers, and both are misunderstood. Some professional master's programmes, particularly part-time and continuing-education formats, accept applications year-round with several start dates, and a few joint degrees run on a rolling basis. "Applications accepted year-round" often means only that the university may accept them year-round, not that a place is waiting for you. For a full-time student, rolling is usually worse, because places fill continuously and early applicants are assessed first.

More disruptive is the every-other-year programme, offered by a few specialist degrees in fields with heavy infrastructure plus some part-time variants. The page says "applications for winter 2027 are accepted" and nothing about 2028. If that is your target, Step 1 changes completely: you are planning around a fixed launch year. Decide whether a gap year is acceptable, check whether an equivalent course runs the following year, and if not, treat the launch year as a hard anchor and build backwards from it.

When do German application deadlines actually fall?

Is the 15 July deadline real, or just a guideline?

It is real for the large majority of programmes with no earlier date of their own, and in most states binding for courses with restricted admission. But calling it "the" German deadline is the most common error applicants make: 15 July is a framework, not a fact about your programme. Faculties publish earlier dates and are entitled to, so you will meet 15 May, 31 March and 1 December on real programme pages, plus rolling admissions where the only deadline is "when the programme is full". The rule for apply for masters in germany step 1 intake planning is simple: the deadline printed on the programme page overrides everything else, including this article.

How do I work backwards from a deadline?

Work backwards from your earliest deadline, not your target one, because the earliest one decides whether you have time to fix a problem. What happens if I miss 15 July?

For restricted-admission programmes you are usually finished for that cycle. For open-admission programmes, called "zulassungsfrei" or "nc-frei" in German, you can often still apply, because the course is not capacity-constrained and admits anyone meeting the formal requirements. Many universities keep those portals open through late summer, and some run a waiting list or lottery for oversubscribed courses. Missing the deadline is survivable only for programmes that were never competitive anyway.

What are the most common German deadline mistakes?

In order of frequency: assuming a time zone you are not in, since German portals usually close at 23:59 or sometimes 12:00 CET and a late-night Karachi submission can land a day behind; treating a scholarship deadline as identical to an admission deadline; and forgetting that some portals require account activation by email. All three are solved by submitting early.

How do I search for programmes with DAAD and Hochschulkompass?

Which portal should I start with, and how do I use the DAAD database?

Start with Hochschulkompass for the authoritative list, then use the DAAD international programmes database for the international-student view and funding information. The DAAD database, reachable from daad.de under "Studying in Germany", is built for applicants from outside Germany. It is narrower than Hochschulkompass, but it shows what you need on one card: language, intake, deadline, application process and entry requirements. That layout makes it the fastest triage tool, and it is where the apply for masters in germany step 1 intake search usually begins. Always finish on the university's own page, which is what counts when a deadline is disputed.

How do I use Hochschulkompass to build the official longlist?

Hochschulkompass is the official portal for German higher education and holds the complete set of degree programmes in the country. At hochschulkompass.de you can filter by degree type, subject, state, language of instruction, admission type and start of the degree programme, which makes it the right place to answer factual questions such as whether a German-taught master's in your field exists in a given state at all.

Use the advanced filters rather than free text, because free-text search in German will not find an English description of a German-taught programme. Set the degree type to master's, set the subject using the discipline you want rather than the department name you assume exists, set language to English if you are not German fluent, and set the start of the degree programme to the winter semester. Record three fields for every result immediately: the programme name exactly as written, the university, and the direct link.

Which third-party tools help, and how long should the longlist be?

Aggregators and ranking sites are useful for one thing: showing you what else exists. They are dangerous for three. Rankings of German master's programmes are weak, because most are built on research output rather than teaching, and a highly ranked research university can have a mediocre master's programme. Aggregator listings go out of date, showing last year's deadline or a discontinued course. And "no tuition fees" pages rarely distinguish states that charge international tuition from those that do not.

As for length, aim for fifteen to thirty programmes. If a niche subject yields only ten, that is itself information: a field with few programmes has few places too. Write a short brief first, covering subject, degree type, language, budget ceiling and two or three deal-breakers, then let the brief decide what you add.

How do I build a shortlist of 5 to 8 universities in three tiers?

Why five to eight applications, and not twenty?

Because each serious German application costs hours. A motivation letter written properly is a focused afternoon, a customised certified and translated document pack is a week of logistics, and reference management adds more. Twenty applications means twenty mediocre letters, and mediocre letters perform badly everywhere, so the extra applications add cost without adding probability. Five to eight well-executed applications, aimed at programmes where your profile sits inside the admitted range, beat twenty scattershot ones, while a list of three leaves you with nothing if all three refuse.

What goes in the dream tier?

One or two programmes where you sit below the typical admitted range, or where competition is fierce, but the fit is so good the payoff justifies the odds. Apply expecting rejection, and never let more than two of your total applications sit here. The danger is mislabelling: a dream university is one where you are a plausible applicant with a low probability, while a programme whose stated entry requirements exclude you is a distraction, not a dream.

What goes in the realistic tier?

Three or four programmes, and this is the heart of the list. These are the ones where the average admitted applicant's profile looks like yours, language and degree requirements are clearly satisfied, the deadline is achievable with your document pipeline, and the funding works without a miracle. A realistic-tier programme is not a compromise; it is where most applicants should end up.

Test each candidate honestly. If the university publishes its admitted range, are you inside the lower bound, the middle, or above it? Is the required subject credit actually on your transcript?

What goes in the safety tier?

One or two programmes where admission is close to certain and the budget works. In Germany this has a particular flavour: open-admission programmes, often at smaller or less internationally known universities, where the main academic test is whether you hold a relevant bachelor's degree with a suitable grade and can prove language proficiency. They are not consolation prizes, and a degree from a German public university is a degree. But such a programme must not be a money trap: open admission does not mean affordable if the course sits in a high-cost state or city.

How do I decide between two programmes I both want?

Score them on the same five criteria, in the same order, with the same weights, and let the total break the tie. Never break a tie on prestige alone, because prestige is the criterion that most reliably turns a good application into a wasted one. The deciding criteria are usually subject fit, cost after funding, language of instruction reality, meaning whether taught modules, exam and thesis are genuinely in English, project access, and city. If two still tie, ask which you would attend if money were free. A useful backup is a second programme at the same institution, and some faculties will consider an application to one programme for another if you ask early.

How do I match your background to the admission requirements?

How are my grades assessed, and what is an aptitude assessment?

Many programmes convert your grade onto the German scale, where 1.0 is best and 5.0 is worst, using a published formula, and some use the raw percentage against a stated minimum. Where a minimum is set, it means it. Pakistani students should expect a degree certificate, a transcript showing subjects and credit hours, and often a professor's statement describing relative performance, because there is no universal national scale to convert from. If your result sits near a threshold, ask the admissions office in writing before investing in the application.

Many programmes also run a formal suitability assessment, the Eignungsfeststellungsverfahren, combining a grade-based score with a short aptitude questionnaire and sometimes an interview. Some publish a graded formula showing exactly how much your transcript counts, which tells you what decides the outcome. If your grade is borderline and a written component exists, that is where you differentiate yourself.

Do I need work experience, and does my degree field have to match?

For a straight-from-undergraduate applicant, relevant work experience is a differentiator, not a gate. What matters is specificity, not length: what you did, with what data or equipment, and what changed because of it. An internship, research assistantship, a final-year project that produced something, or documented volunteering all count. Our resume versus CV guide covers the format, and the motivation letter guide shows how to turn a thin experience section into an argument.

On subject match, Germany is stricter than the US or UK. Programme pages often distinguish degrees that must be "fachlich verwandt" (subject-related) from those that must be "fachlich einschlägig" (subject-specifically relevant), and those are not the same requirement. If your background is adjacent rather than identical, use a logical transition story and demonstrate subject knowledge through coursework or thesis. Some universities will examine your existing credits for equivalence when you ask, which sometimes converts a rejection into an offer for the price of one email.

How do I check the language of instruction properly?

If the programme says English, is it really taught in English?

Usually, but not always, and the difference matters more than students expect. Some officially English-taught programmes hold examinations in German or offer modules in German. Some master's programmes are German-taught with a minority of English modules, which is fine if you plan a life in Germany and a serious problem if you do not. And in a German-taught programme, your thesis supervisor, your internship and your first job will all be in German.

Check three things: the language of the taught modules, the language of examination, and the language expected for the master's thesis. If any of the three is German and you are not fluent, either plan to become fluent through a structured language course, or drop the programme.

Which German level or English test do I need, and when do I book it?

For German-taught master's programmes, C1 is the usual expectation and B2 is occasionally accepted where a language component is built in. The certificate normally must be a recognised one, such as a Goethe-Zertifikat, a telc certificate or a university language certificate, and no older than two years at application. If you are starting from zero, an A1 or A2 course is a better use of six months than a desperate application. Studying abroad without IELTS covers the German route, and IELTS scores by country sets out what Germany expects.

Book the English test in your first month of Step 1, not after you have decided where to apply, because availability in Pakistan can be tight and a result you already hold turns a shortlist from a projection into facts. Typical requirements are IELTS 6.5 overall with 6.0 or 6.5 per component, and TOEFL iBT 80 to 95, with competitive programmes asking for 7.0 or above; take the target from the programme page, not a country table. Our TOEFL versus IELTS guide explains the differences in how speaking and writing are assessed. Many universities now also accept the Duolingo English Test, but the list is institution-specific, and a test a university accepts is not always one the visa authority accepts.

What other filters should decide your German shortlist?

Is tuition really free, and what does the semester contribution include?

Public universities in most states charge no tuition for standard degree programmes, and the country's free-study reputation rests on that. The important exception is Baden-Württemberg, which charges non-EU and non-EEA international students a semester contribution, and a few public institutions elsewhere also levy international tuition, particularly in art, music and some professional fields. Verify fee status on the programme page, because "Germany is free" is a generalisation, not a fact about your application. Our cost and budget guide sets out what else to budget, and study abroad loans in Pakistan covers financing a gap.

The semester contribution, or Semesterbeitrag, is what every enrolled student pays each semester, and it usually bundles administration with the semester ticket, the local transport pass. In most states that pass covers the regional transport association, which across a large state can mean unlimited trains, buses, trams and metro for the full semester. The amount varies by university and state, is typically a few hundred euros, and can differ slightly between winter and summer. Budget it as compulsory, because enrolment is not complete until you have paid it.

Do the city, the state and my funding options change the choice?

More than students admit, because you will spend two years there: rent, transport, whether university accommodation has places, how easy part-time work is, and whether the international office actually helps with residence registration and bank accounts. That last point sounds trivial and is not, because support varies enormously between states. If part-time work is part of your plan, see part-time jobs for international students, and for the longer view the post-study work visa comparison.

Scholarships change the ranking of your options more than the list itself. Before tiering, note which realistic-tier programmes are scholarship-eligible, which have dedicated funding lines, and which sit where self-funding is comfortable, because a slightly weaker academic fit that is fully funded can beat a stronger one you cannot afford. Some German funding is not a scholarship at all but an assistantship tied to a research group. The DAAD scholarship guide and our scholarships for Pakistani students overview both belong here.

How do I set alerts, and what if a new programme appears mid-search?

Enter every deadline as a date, then set reminders at thirty days, fourteen days, seven days and forty-eight hours before the official cut-off, and treat your real submission date as a week earlier than the deadline. For competitive programmes, add the portal opening date as a milestone with an alert two days before it opens, and subscribe to the faculty mailing list, because German faculties announce deadline extensions there first.

When a new programme appears mid-search, apply the brief you wrote for the longlist, not a new and more enthusiastic one. Check the deadline first, then academic requirements, then language, then cost. If it passes all four and the deadline is at least six weeks away, add it and tier it properly; if the deadline is closer, add it only if it is open-admission. Set a hard date for the shortlist to be final, or you will never stop adding.

Mistakes to avoid in apply for masters in germany step 1 intake

Is 15 July a target or a cliff?

A cliff. Applications are assessed in rounds, and in restricted subjects the later you apply the worse your chances, because better-prepared candidates apply first. If your goal is a competitive programme, your personal deadline is the day applications open, or within a fortnight. Write that date into the spreadsheet as your submission date and treat the published deadline as an outer limit you will never approach.

What happens if you forget uni-assist?

uni-assist is the central application service used by many universities for programmes with restricted admission, handling the preliminary review of your documents, grades and language certificates. If a programme says "application via uni-assist", you cannot apply directly to the university, and a direct application there is a no-op. Registration is at uni-assist.de, and you submit separately to each university, each with its own handling fee, so a six-programme list means six submissions and six fees. Your documents must also reach uni-assist before the university's cut-off, so early registration is not optional.

What are the other classic mistakes?

Confusing the course start date with the application deadline. Students read "winter semester 2027" and assume they can apply in 2027. They cannot: a winter 2027 intake has an application deadline in the first half of 2026, often earlier, because the university needs time to decide and issue confirmations so students can apply for the visa. Record the deadline next to the start date, never separately.

Shortlisting by ranking and nothing else. Ranking reflects research reputation, which is not teaching quality, student support or fit with your career. A mid-ranked programme with a live industry placement and an international office that answers emails may suit you far better than a top-ranked one with a department where nobody replies. And the subtler fifth mistake is letting the list grow indefinitely, since every extra programme is 10 to 20 more hours of work and a weaker letter for all of them.

What happens after Step 1?

Which documents come next, and how long does the visa process take?

Assume you will need your bachelor's certificate and full transcript, a passport valid beyond your course, a detailed module handbook for the target programme, a language certificate, a motivation letter, one or two academic references, a CV in the German academic format, a passport photograph, and certified plus translated documents where required. For a full-time degree you will also need proof of funds for the residence permit, usually a blocked account covering the study period plus a buffer, with the amount published by the German federal government and updated annually.

For Pakistani applicants, plan around a document verification process run by the academic verification office, which must be booked as an appointment rather than walked into and usually completed before the visa can be considered. Add a visa processing period measured in weeks, and weeks for the university to issue admission and enrolment confirmations. A perfect application submitted in April realistically still lands you in Germany in September or October, not July. Frequently asked questions about the German masters intake

What intakes do German universities have for masters programmes?

Germany has two standard intakes: winter semester starting in October, and summer semester starting in April. Winter is the main intake and carries the large majority of programmes, especially the English-taught degrees international applicants want. Summer intake is real but smaller, with far fewer English-taught options, concentrated in business, teacher education and some STEM fields. A small number of programmes admit every other year. For anyone counting on DAAD funding, the apply for masters in germany step 1 intake choice comes down to a wide October market versus a narrow April one.

When are the application deadlines for German universities?

The standard framework is 15 July for the following winter semester and 15 January for the summer semester. But many competitive programmes close three to six months earlier than that, and some publish entirely different dates such as 15 May, 31 March or 1 February. Restricted-admission programmes running through uni-assist add their own earlier cut-off on top. Always treat the deadline printed on the individual programme page as the only one that counts, and plan to submit in the first weeks applications are open.

How do I find masters programmes in Germany?

Use two official sources together. The DAAD international programmes database at daad.de is built for international applicants and shows language, intake, deadline and application process in one place, which makes it fast for triage. Hochschulkompass at hochschulkompass.de is the complete official list of degree programmes in Germany, with the most reliable filters by subject, state, degree type, language and start of programme. Build the longlist in DAAD, verify every survivor in Hochschulkompass, then confirm on the university's own faculty page, because that is what the admissions officer will use.

How many German universities should I apply to?

Five to eight, structured in three tiers: one or two dream options where you are plausible but low probability, three or four realistic options where your profile sits comfortably inside the admitted range, and one or two safety options that are open-admission or near-certain and that you would genuinely be happy to attend. More than eight usually means weaker documents across the board, and fewer than five leaves you exposed if several decisions go against you.

Your Step 1 checklist before you move to Step 2

You are done with Step 1 when you can answer all of the following without hesitation. You know whether you are targeting a winter or summer intake, and why. You have a longlist of fifteen to thirty programmes and a tiered shortlist of five to eight. Every shortlisted programme has a verified deadline, language requirement, academic requirement, cost figure and saved direct link. Your spreadsheet is live with alerts set and a next action dated, and your language test is booked or deliberately skipped. And you know your document checklist, because that is what Step 2 will ask for.

Close anything still open before you write a single sentence of motivation letter. A focused application to a programme you have genuinely researched will beat a rushed application to a better name every time, and the research you do now is what makes the writing fast later. Step 1 is not the boring administrative part of a German masters application. It is the part that decides what the rest of the application is even possible.
