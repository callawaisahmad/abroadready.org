OECD Internships & Junior Professional Programme

🗓️ Aug 5, 2026⏱️ 9 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

The OECD offers two distinct ways to build an international career in economic and social policy: a well-known internship programme and a more advanced entry route called the Junior Professional Programme (JPP). Both are open to candidates of all nationalities, which makes them genuinely different from many government organisations, and both are funded. The OECD, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, is based in Paris and works on policy across nearly forty member countries plus many partner economies, so interns and junior professionals join a genuinely global policy environment.

This guide explains how OECD internships work, what the Junior Professional Programme is, who is eligible for each, and how to make your application competitive.

Key takeaways

  • OECD internships are open to candidates of all nationalities who are enrolled in a degree or have recently graduated.
  • Interns receive a monthly allowance designed to cover living costs in Paris.
  • The Junior Professional Programme is a paid two-year fixed-term role for recent graduates, open to candidates of all OECD member and partner countries.
  • Both routes are based mainly in Paris, though some assignments are hybrid or in field offices.
  • Applications go through oecd.org/careers, and competitive candidates show strong analytical and language skills.

How OECD internships work

The OECD internship programme places students in its policy directorates, where they contribute to research, analysis, event support and communications. Interns work with experienced analysts on projects that feed into real OECD output, such as country reviews, working papers and statistical reports. Assignments typically run from one to six months and can sometimes be renewed, with many interns joining for three months over the summer.

Eligibility is broad. You need to be enrolled in a full-time degree programme or to have graduated within the past two years, and you should have a good command of one of the OECD's working languages, most often English, with French a strong advantage. There is no age limit and no citizenship requirement.

Is the OECD internship paid?

Yes. OECD interns receive a monthly allowance intended to cover the cost of living in Paris, which is widely reported to be around one thousand euros per month, though you should confirm the current figure on the official programme page. Interns are also expected to arrange their own accommodation and travel, so the allowance is a contribution rather than a full salary. You are not an employee of the OECD, and the internship does not automatically lead to a staff contract, though it is an excellent entry signal.

What is the Junior Professional Programme?

The Junior Professional Programme is the OECD's structured route for recent graduates who want to work at the organisation. It is a paid, two-year fixed-term appointment at a junior analyst level, and JPP participants carry real responsibility from the start, contributing to directorate work, country reviews and policy research. The salary is a proper OECD salary with benefits, not an intern allowance.

The programme is open to candidates from all OECD member countries and partner economies, and to both university graduates and those with a few years of early-career experience. Selection is competitive and usually involves an online assessment and interviews, and participants are expected to relocate to Paris unless the assignment is elsewhere.

How the application process works

Both routes are managed through the OECD careers website at oecd.org/careers, and the process for each is different.

Applying for an internship

Internship vacancies are posted on the OECD careers site, often with rolling deadlines. Create a profile, upload a tailored CV and cover letter, and apply to specific openings rather than submitting a general application. Because demand for OECD internships is high, applications that connect the applicant's coursework to a specific directorate's priorities stand out.

Applying for the JPP

The Junior Professional Programme has its own recruitment cycle, with applications opening for a defined period each year. The process typically includes an online application with motivation questions, a technical assessment, and structured interviews. Prepare by studying the OECD's current priorities, from climate and digitalisation to education and employment, and be ready to discuss how your skills map to real OECD projects.

Making your application competitive

The OECD works in policy analysis, so your application needs to demonstrate analytical writing. Highlight coursework, theses or prior work where you handled data, wrote policy briefs or produced research reports, and name the relevant OECD work streams you would like to contribute to. Language skills matter, so list every language you can work in, not just the ones you are fluent in.

Your cover letter should read like a short statement of purpose, explaining why the OECD, why this team and why you. Our guide to writing a winning statement of purpose walks through the same structure, and our advice on building a competitive profile applies directly to international applications like this one.

Practical considerations

Paris is an expensive city, so budget carefully before accepting an internship, and start your visa research early if you need one. The OECD can provide documentation to support visa applications, but the responsibility for obtaining a visa sits with you. For a general walkthrough, see our student visa guide.

Both the internship and the JPP reward persistence. The OECD receives thousands of applications for a limited number of places, so it is normal not to be selected on the first attempt, and many successful candidates applied across several cycles.

How to apply: next steps

Create your profile on oecd.org/careers this week and set up a calendar reminder to check new internship postings. Identify two or three directorates that fit your interests, tailor your CV and cover letter to each, and apply early in the cycle. If you meet the JPP eligibility window, prepare your application materials well before the deadline and practise for the assessment.

While you wait, keep building the skills the OECD values. For more funded policy and research opportunities, browse our internships hub and our guide to funded summer research internships abroad.

What you actually do as an OECD intern day to day

Understanding the day-to-day work of an OECD intern helps you decide whether the programme fits your career goals and gives you material for a more convincing application. OECD internships are placed within specific directorates or divisions, and the work varies significantly depending on which team hosts you. In the Economics department, an intern might assist with data collection and analysis for flagship publications such as the OECD Economic Outlook. In the Education and Skills directorate, you might help prepare survey instruments or analyse results from the Programme for International Student Assessment. In the Environment directorate, you might contribute to policy reviews or draft sections of working papers on climate policy.

The common thread across all placements is policy relevance. The OECD does not conduct purely academic research; everything it produces is intended to inform government policy across member and partner countries. As an intern, you will typically be assigned a specific project or a set of tasks within an ongoing project, supervised by a professional staff member. The work is real and contributes to published outputs. You are not fetching coffee or filing papers. Interns are expected to produce analysis, draft text and contribute meaningfully to the team's workload.

Internships at the OECD generally last between two and six months, with the most common duration being around three to four months. The pace is professional and expectations are high, but the environment is also supportive. Interns are included in team meetings, invited to seminars and lectures, and encouraged to attend internal events. The working language is English or French depending on the division, and many teams operate bilingually. If you are fluent in both languages, that is a significant advantage.

The experience varies by division. Economics and finance placements tend to be highly quantitative, involving econometric analysis, data manipulation and statistical modelling. Policy-oriented placements in areas such as education, health or environment may involve more writing, literature review and stakeholder consultation. Technology and data science placements may involve programming, data visualisation and database management. Regardless of the specific tasks, the common expectation is that you produce work of professional quality that your team can use.

The Junior Professional Programme and the internship programme serve different purposes and target candidates at different stages, and confusing the two is a common mistake. The internship programme is aimed at current students or recent graduates who are still building their professional skills. It is a training and exposure opportunity, typically lasting a few months, and it is open to a wide range of candidates with appropriate academic backgrounds.

The Junior Professional Programme, by contrast, is a more advanced entry point designed for candidates who already hold a graduate degree and have some professional experience. JPP positions are regular staff appointments, not temporary placements. Junior professionals are hired on fixed-term contracts, typically for one to two years, and they perform the same work as other professional staff members. The JPP is essentially a recruitment pipeline: successful JPP participants often transition into regular OECD positions or move into other international organisations with the experience and credentials gained.

The selection process for the JPP is more rigorous than for internships. It typically involves a written application, a technical assessment or test, and one or more interviews. Competition is intense because the positions are paid at professional salary levels and offer genuine career progression. At the time of writing, JPP salaries are competitive with entry-level positions in other international organisations, and the programme includes benefits such as health insurance and paid leave.

For students deciding between the two, the internship is the right choice if you are still completing your degree or have recently graduated and want to explore international policy work. The JPP is the right choice if you have a master's degree or equivalent, at least one to two years of relevant professional experience, and you are ready to commit to a full professional role at an international organisation. Both programmes are valuable, but they lead to different outcomes and require different levels of preparation. An internship at the OECD can strengthen a future JPP application significantly, so if the JPP is your long-term goal, consider the internship as a strategic stepping stone.

Building a competitive OECD application: what selection panels look for

OECD selection panels assess applicants on a combination of academic quality, relevant skills, motivation and alignment with the organisation's mission. Understanding what panels prioritise helps you allocate effort in your application materials and present yourself in the strongest possible light.

Academic quality is the baseline requirement. The OECD recruits from strong universities worldwide, and your transcripts, references and any publications or research outputs will be scrutinised. However, academic excellence alone is not sufficient. The OECD values practical skills: quantitative analysis, policy writing, data visualisation and the ability to synthesise complex information into clear, actionable summaries. If you have experience with statistical software such as Stata, R, Python or SAS, mention it prominently. If you have policy writing experience, such as briefing notes, policy briefs or government reports, highlight that as well.

Motivation and mission alignment matter enormously. The OECD is a policy organisation, not a university or a think tank, and it serves a specific constituency: governments of member and partner countries. Your application should demonstrate that you understand this role and are motivated by the prospect of contributing to evidence-based policy. Avoid framing your motivation purely in terms of personal career development. Instead, connect your interests to the OECD's current priority areas, which at the time of writing include productivity, digital transformation, green growth, inequality and skills.

Language skills are a significant advantage. English is the working language of most divisions, but French is also widely used, particularly in governance and legal affairs. Bilingual candidates are strongly preferred for many positions. If you are proficient in additional languages, especially those spoken in OECD member or partner countries, include that information in your application. Language proficiency is not just a communication tool; it reflects cultural awareness and the ability to work across linguistic boundaries, which is central to the OECD's collaborative model.

Finally, attention to detail in the application itself signals professionalism. The OECD receives applications from highly qualified candidates worldwide, and small errors, formatting inconsistencies or incomplete sections can result in immediate rejection. Proofread everything carefully, follow the application instructions exactly, and ensure that your supporting documents are complete and in the requested format. A polished application demonstrates the precision and thoroughness that the OECD expects in its professional work.

Practical logistics: living in Paris as an OECD intern

If you are selected for an OECD internship or JPP placement, you will spend your time at the OECD headquarters in Paris, France. Paris is one of the most expensive cities in Europe, so understanding the practical costs and logistics before you arrive is essential for a smooth experience.

The OECD does not provide accommodation for interns, though it may offer assistance in finding housing through its housing information service. Many interns find accommodation through university housing networks, shared apartments or temporary rental platforms. The cost of a shared room in Paris typically ranges from 600 to 900 euros per month depending on the neighbourhood and the type of accommodation. Living further from the centre reduces costs but increases commute time, so weigh the trade-off carefully.

The OECD internship stipend, at the time of writing, is modest relative to the cost of living in Paris. It is intended to cover basic expenses rather than provide a comfortable income. Budget carefully from the start: food, transport, insurance and personal expenses add up quickly. France has a strong and affordable public transport system, and a monthly Navigo pass covering the Paris region costs roughly 86 euros. Many interns cook at home rather than eating out regularly, and student-priced meals are available at university canteens if you register.

Health insurance is required for your stay in France. The OECD may provide or facilitate coverage, but verify the specifics in your offer letter and arrange supplementary insurance if needed. EU citizens may be covered through their national health system; non-EU citizens should ensure they have adequate coverage before arriving. The French health system is efficient but navigating it in a foreign language can be daunting, so ask the OECD HR team or other interns for guidance during your first week.

Paris itself is a remarkable place to spend a few months. The city's cultural, intellectual and social life is extraordinary, and even on a tight budget you can enjoy museums, parks, libraries and neighbourhoods that make the experience genuinely enriching beyond the professional development. Many museums offer free entry on the first Sunday of each month, and the city's public parks and riverside spaces provide free recreation. Plan your finances realistically so that financial stress does not undermine what should be a formative professional experience.

OECD internship and JPP application timelines and key deadlines

The OECD operates on a rolling or cyclical application system depending on the programme, and knowing the timing is crucial for planning. The internship programme typically accepts applications multiple times per year, with intake periods often aligned to quarterly start dates. At the time of writing, the main application windows open approximately three to four months before each intake period, though the exact dates vary and are published on the OECD careers portal.

The Junior Professional Programme follows a different cycle. JPP positions are posted when they become available, which may be once or twice per year depending on the division and budget. JPP applications typically close within a few weeks of posting, and the selection process can take several months from submission to offer. Monitor the OECD careers page regularly and set alerts for new postings in your area of interest.

For both programmes, the application requires a CV, a cover letter, academic transcripts and sometimes a writing sample or technical assessment. Prepare these documents well in advance so that when a posting appears, you can apply quickly without rushing. Tailor your cover letter to the specific division or position; generic applications are rarely successful because the OECD receives hundreds of strong applications for each opening.

A practical approach is to begin preparing your application materials six months before you intend to start an internship. Use that time to strengthen any skill gaps, such as improving your statistical software proficiency, taking a short online course in policy analysis or economics, or building your writing portfolio with policy-relevant pieces. The stronger your skills at the point of application, the higher your chances of selection.

After submitting your application, the OECD typically responds within four to eight weeks for internships and longer for JPP positions. If you are shortlisted, expect one or two rounds of interviews, which may be conducted online. Prepare for technical questions related to your field, as well as behavioural questions about your motivation, teamwork skills and ability to work in a multicultural environment. The OECD values candidates who can demonstrate both technical competence and the interpersonal skills needed to thrive in an international policy environment.

What OECD internships pay and how the stipend compares

The funding question is the first thing most students ask about OECD internships, and the honest answer is that the picture has changed. For many years OECD internships were unpaid, which made them inaccessible to students who could not self-fund a stay in Paris. At the time of writing, the OECD offers a paid internship programme with a monthly stipend, though the amount is modest relative to the cost of living in Paris. The stipend is intended to cover basic living costs for a student, and it is typically adjusted annually.

It is important to set expectations correctly. The OECD internship stipend is not a salary and it is not designed to produce savings. It covers rent in shared accommodation, food, transport and basic expenses, but students who plan comfortable travel or expensive hobbies will need personal resources. In practice, many interns find that the stipend covers most daily costs if they budget carefully, but that an additional personal buffer is helpful for the first weeks before the first payment arrives.

Compare this against other internship options in international organisations. UN agencies, which moved to paid internships in recent years, offer stipends that also vary by location and agency. The World Bank and IMF pay substantially more for their internship programmes. The European Commission's traineeship scheme, commonly known as the Blue Book, pays a monthly grant that is higher than the OECD stipend in most cases. Understanding this landscape helps you decide whether the OECD internship is the right financial fit for your situation.

For the Junior Professional Programme, the financial picture is different and more favourable. JPP positions are paid at professional salary levels, which at the time of writing are competitive with entry-level positions in other international organisations. Benefits typically include health insurance, paid leave and contributions toward pension or retirement arrangements depending on the contract. This is why the JPP is a genuine career entry point rather than a training placement: it compensates you at a professional level for professional work.

The practical implication for students is a straightforward division. The internship is the right choice when you are still building skills and can accept a modest stipend in exchange for world-class experience and networking. The JPP is the right choice when you have the graduate qualification and professional experience to step directly into a compensated professional role. Budget your Paris life accordingly whichever programme you pursue, and confirm the exact financial terms in your offer letter before committing.

Career paths after an OECD internship or JPP placement

The value of an OECD internship or Junior Professional Programme placement extends far beyond the months you spend in Paris, and understanding the career options that follow helps you choose the programme strategically and make the most of your time there. Both routes are recognised credentials across the international policy world, and alumni of both programmes work in government, academia, multilateral institutions, consulting, the private sector and civil society.

Within the OECD itself, the internship is a proven pathway into professional employment. Many current OECD analysts and policy advisors completed an internship earlier in their careers, and the relationships built during an internship often lead to later job offers. The JPP is an even more direct route into the OECD's professional workforce: a successful JPP placement frequently converts into a regular staff appointment, and the fixed-term contract is designed with that conversion in mind.

Beyond the OECD, the experience carries weight with other international organisations. Multilateral bodies, development banks, United Nations agencies and regional organisations all recruit from the OECD alumni pool, and the policy analysis and writing skills you develop are directly transferable. For students from Pakistan and other developing countries, an OECD credential also strengthens candidacies with national governments, central banks, research institutes and international consultancies that work on economic and social policy.

The skills you develop are the real asset. OECD interns and junior professionals learn to work in a multicultural, multilingual environment; to synthesise complex data into policy-relevant analysis; and to communicate research findings in formats that decision makers actually use. These capabilities are rare and valuable. When you apply for graduate programmes, your OECD experience differentiates you from applicants with purely academic backgrounds and provides concrete material for your statements and interviews.

To maximise the career benefit, be intentional during your placement. Attend the OECD's knowledge events and seminars, build relationships with senior analysts in fields that interest you, and seek feedback on your work. Document your projects and outputs carefully so you can point to concrete achievements in future applications. Maintain contact with supervisors and colleagues after you leave, and do not hesitate to ask for references when the time comes. An OECD placement is a career accelerator, but only if you approach it with deliberate strategy rather than passive participation.

OECD internships across different policy directorates

The OECD is organised into multiple policy directorates, each focusing on a different area of economic and social policy, and the nature of your internship varies significantly depending on which directorate hosts you. Understanding the different directorates helps you target your application to the area that best matches your skills and interests.

The Economics department produces flagship publications such as the OECD Economic Outlook and the Going for Growth report. Interns in this directorate work with economists on data analysis, econometric modelling and the preparation of policy documents. If you have strong quantitative skills and a background in macroeconomics or applied economics, this directorate offers rigorous and rewarding work. The Education and Skills directorate focuses on education policy, skills development and the Programme for International Student Assessment. Interns here may help with survey analysis, policy reviews or comparative education research.

The Environment directorate works on climate policy, biodiversity,green growth and environmental regulation. Interns contribute to environmental performance reviews, work on sustainability indicators and help prepare policy recommendations for member governments. The Trade and Agriculture directorate focuses on international trade policy, agricultural subsidies and market access, and offers opportunities for students with backgrounds in economics, law or political science.

The Directorate for Science, Technology and Innovation works on innovation policy, digital transformation and intellectual property. Interns here may work on technology assessments, innovation surveys or policy analysis related to artificial intelligence and data governance. Each directorate has its own culture, pace and focus, so research the specific directorate you are applying to and tailor your application accordingly.

Regardless of the directorate, the common thread is policy relevance. Every OECD intern contributes to work that directly informs government decisions, and the quality of your analysis and writing must meet the standards of a publication that will be read by policy makers around the world. The OECD internship is not a passive learning experience: it is an opportunity to contribute meaningfully to global policy while developing skills that will serve you throughout your career.
