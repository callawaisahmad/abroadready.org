UN Internships 2026: How to Get a Paid Internship

🗓️ Aug 12, 2026⏱️ 12 min read✍️ AbroadReady Team

What are UN internships, and who actually offers them?

UN internships are a common goal for students interested in international development, human rights and global policy, and in 2026 more of them are paid than many people assume. The United Nations runs internships across dozens of agencies and field offices, and several of the best-known bodies, UNICEF, UNDP, UNHCR and WHO, advertise paid roles year-round for students and recent graduates from anywhere in the world. The catch is that the system is decentralised: each agency posts its own vacancies, sets its own eligibility rules and decides its own pay.

Why does pay vary so much from one UN internship to the next?

Because there is no single United Nations internship scheme. Each fund, programme, office and specialised agency treats internships as a separate recruitment exercise, which means that a stipend at one duty station can be completely absent at another. Some organisations pay every intern, some pay only interns who are not already funded by a university, and some still advertise unpaid learning opportunities. Understanding this patchwork early is the difference between applying confidently and applying to the wrong advert.

What will this guide tell me about UN internships?

This guide cuts through the confusion and explains which UN internships pay in 2026, roughly how much, who is eligible, where to apply, and how to build an application that actually gets read. It also covers the less obvious routes into the system, including internship rosters, the United Nations Volunteers programme, the language and competency frameworks used for shortlisting, and what life inside a UN office is actually like.

Key takeaways

What are the headline facts about UN internships in 2026?

If you only have two minutes, these are the points that matter most when you are weighing up UN internships for 2026:

  • UN internships are open to students and recent graduates of all nationalities, with no citizenship requirement.
  • UNICEF pays a monthly stipend of around $1,000 (varies by duty station) plus a one-time travel contribution.
  • UNDP and UNHCR pay stipends to interns who are not otherwise sponsored; WHO and FAO also pay, at varying rates.
  • Parts of the UN Secretariat still advertise unpaid roles, so always read the vacancy before applying.
  • Internships typically last two to six months (UNICEF: six to 26 weeks) and can be in person, hybrid or remote.

Why do these five points matter before you start applying?

Because each one filters out a category of wasted application. The nationality point widens your pool dramatically, since there is no need to be sponsored by a member state to be considered. The stipend point stops you chasing adverts that assume you can self-fund six months abroad. The duration point lets you plan realistically against your university calendar, your visa timeline and your own savings. And the format point, in person, hybrid or remote, increasingly determines whether a given UN internship is realistic for you at all.

Which UN internships pay in 2026?

Is the honest answer simply "it depends on the agency"?

This is the question everyone asks first, and the honest answer is that it depends on the agency. The big, frequently hiring agencies mostly pay, with stipends set by duty station:

  • UNICEF: around $1,000 per month, plus a one-time lump sum toward travel and visa costs when funding is available. Roles are posted on UNICEF's careers site year-round.
  • UNDP: a monthly stipend set by duty station and work arrangement (including a remote tier), paid to interns who are not already funded by a university or other sponsor. Vacancies appear on jobs.undp.org on a rolling basis.
  • UNHCR: a mandatory stipend for interns without external funding, plus reimbursement of return travel for eligible interns travelling from abroad.
  • WHO and FAO: paid internship schemes with stipends roughly in the $700–1,800 per month range depending on location.
  • Parts of the UN Secretariat: some offices still advertise unpaid internships, so the pay status should be checked on every individual vacancy.

How do stipend levels differ between duty stations?

Stipends are not set centrally, so the same agency can pay very different amounts for very similar work. A high-cost duty station such as Geneva, New York or Paris will attract a higher monthly figure than a field office in a lower-cost country, and remote or hybrid arrangements usually sit in their own band again. This is why two UN internships advertised on the same day can carry stipends that are double one another without either advert being misleading. When you compare offers, always compare the duty station and the funding source, not just the headline figure.

Why is a self-funded UN internship still worth reading carefully?

Unpaid roles are not automatically a bad thing. Several parts of the UN system, and some national missions, still advertise learning placements with no financial support, and for a candidate who lives in the host city, holds the right residency status or is being sponsored by their university, an unpaid placement can be perfectly legitimate. The problem is ambiguity: some adverts simply do not mention money at all. Treat silence as a question to ask the hiring contact, and never accept an offer you have accepted on an assumption about funding that was never confirmed in writing.

Eligibility

Who is eligible to apply for UN internships?

Most UN agencies accept interns who are enrolled in an undergraduate or graduate degree program, or who graduated within the past two years (some agencies accept up to one year). That is the short version. In practice, eligibility is a three-part test: a minimum age, a minimum level of study, and working-language fluency. You usually need to be at least eighteen and fluent in at least one working language, most often English. Interns are not UN staff; the arrangement is an experiential learning opportunity, which is exactly why funding policies vary so much across the system.

Do you need to be a national of a UN member state to get an internship?

No. UN internships are open to students and recent graduates of all nationalities, and the largest agencies recruit deliberately from regions that are under-represented in their own staff. There is no citizenship requirement, no sponsorship requirement through a government, and no rule that you must be nominated by a ministry. What does matter is that you can legally stay in the host country for the duration of the placement and, in most cases, that you can satisfy the local labour or visa rules for interns.

How recent does my degree have to be?

Usually within two years of graduation, though the window is not standardised. Some agencies accept candidates up to one year out of university, particularly for programmes aimed at early-career professionals, while UNICEF and a number of other funds allow a slightly wider window. If you graduated three years ago, you are not automatically excluded from UN internships, but you will need evidence of continuous, relevant activity, so a postgraduate course, research assistantship or NGO role usually does more for your application than an older degree on its own.

What language level do I need for a UN internship?

Fluency in at least one UN working language, and English in the vast majority of cases, because almost all internal documentation, briefing notes and reporting run through it. A single working language is the minimum, not the target. Agencies give real weight to candidates who add French, Spanish, Arabic or Russian, and in country offices the local language often matters more than any of the official six. Treat language ability as one of the few genuine differentiators you control cheaply, and document it properly in your application.

Where and how to apply

Is there a single UN application form for all UN internships?

There is no single UN application form. Every agency, fund and office uses its own recruitment platform, its own profile and its own submission format, and the same candidate will create separate accounts on several of them. This is the single biggest source of confusion for first-time applicants, because it means there is no single place to track your progress. The practical workaround is to build one master CV, one master motivation letter and one document folder, then adapt them per agency rather than rebuilding from scratch each time.

Which portals should I check for UN internship vacancies?

UNICEF posts on unicef.org/careers, UNDP on jobs.undp.org, UNHCR on unhcr.org/careers, WHO on its careers portal, and many bodies also advertise on the central careers.un.org site. The central portal is useful for breadth, but agency sites are where the most complete duty station and funding information lives, including whether a role is remote, in person or hybrid, and whether a stipend is attached. The UN Secretariat also lists roles on the Inspira platform, which is worth monitoring even if your target agency does not use it.

How soon should I apply after a vacancy is posted?

Fast. Each vacancy has its own deadline, so the practical approach is to check the agency portals regularly and apply to roles that genuinely match your background. Vacancies close when filled, so applying within days of a posting is a real advantage. Many panels begin shortlisting while the advert is still live, which means that a strong application submitted on day three can genuinely beat a strong application submitted on day twenty. Treat postings as news, not as opportunities to plan around.

What if I am still studying when the internship starts?

Most agencies accommodate this, and a large share of UN internships are designed specifically for candidates who are enrolled. The practical requirements are usually a minimum number of completed years of study and a realistic availability window. You will typically need to show that you can commit to the full length of the placement, and where there is a conflict with your examinations, agencies are usually flexible about exact start dates. Flag the constraint early in your application rather than hiding it, because it is a scheduling problem, not a disqualification.

Building a strong UN application

What actually makes a UN internship application stand out?

UN recruiters read a lot of generic applications, and specificity wins. The panels are looking for evidence that you could do this specific job, in this specific team, on this specific mandate. The two levers that consistently work are relevance and quantification. Relevance means every claim you make can be traced to a line in the vacancy description. Quantification means the scale, duration and result of your work is stated in numbers. A third lever, which applicants underestimate, is naming the agency's current programmes accurately, because it shows you have read something other than the mission statement.

How specific should my examples be?

Far more specific than feels natural. Instead of "assisted with events", write "coordinated logistics for a fifty-participant workshop". The first version could have been written by anyone who has ever been near an event; the second tells a panel what you owned, at what scale, and therefore what you could be trusted to do in a UN office. Apply the same test to your CV bullets, your motivation letter and your interview answers. If a sentence would still make sense in an application to a bank, rewrite it until it only makes sense here.

Which transferable writing skills should I polish first?

The two that matter most are structured argument and evidence use, and both are teachable. Our guides on writing a winning statement of purpose and building a competitive scholarship profile cover the same skills, and they transfer directly to UN applications. Everything you learn about framing a personal narrative under a word limit, about supporting a claim with evidence, and about cutting filler is useful in a UN internship application, because the hiring panel reads for exactly those things.

Practical considerations

Will a UN internship stipend cover my living costs?

Usually not entirely. Even with a stipend, intern pay rarely covers the full cost of living in cities like Geneva or New York, so plan your budget before you accept an offer. Build a simple monthly model: stipend, plus any travel contribution, minus rent, local transport, insurance, food and the fee differential for a private health policy. Run that model in your host city before you sign, and if the numbers do not work, ask the agency directly what support is available rather than assuming.

Should I clarify visa support and work format before accepting?

Yes, and do it early, because agencies vary in how much they can help with paperwork. Ask whether the role is in person, hybrid or remote, and clarify visa support early, since the answer determines whether you need a sponsor, a notification letter, or nothing at all beyond a standard employment visa in your own name. Some agencies provide a formal request letter for a visa application; others contribute nothing and expect you to handle it. Getting this in writing before you travel is far better than discovering it in your second week abroad.

Does a UN internship guarantee a job afterwards?

No, and anyone who promises otherwise is overselling. A UN internship is a strong credential and a real networking opportunity, but it does not guarantee a UN job afterwards, so treat it as one step in a longer plan rather than a destination. What it does give you is system knowledge, references, a network across agencies and a demonstrated track record, and those compound over the following years. Candidates who convert an internship into a longer-term path usually do so by being deliberate about contacts, portfolios and roster registration during the placement.

How to apply: next steps

What should I do in the next two weeks?

Build a clean, tailored CV and a cover letter template now, then start scanning the UNICEF, UNDP, UNHCR and WHO portals. Here is the sequence that works for most applicants:

  1. Create accounts on the central UN portal and on the two or three agency sites that match your target field.
  2. Identify three or four roles that fit your profile, and read the full vacancy description and competency list for each.
  3. Write each application individually, mirroring the language and competencies of that specific advert.
  4. Have one person outside your field proofread the motivation letter, since grammar errors are noticed.
  5. Submit within the first week of each vacancy being posted, and record the submission date in a tracker.

What should I do if I am not selected?

Reapply for the next cycle, as many successful UN interns applied several times. Treat every rejection as a data point: check whether the gap was in your documents, in your evidence, or in your targeting, and adjust the specific weakness rather than rewriting everything from scratch. Ask for feedback where the agency offers it, keep your profiles and documents current, and re-register on rosters, because the same shortlist is often reused. Persistence is genuinely one of the competencies the UN assesses, so a second and third attempt is not a sign of failure.

Where can I find more funded international opportunities?

For more funded international opportunities, browse our internships hub. Funding bodies, development NGOs, research institutes and some intergovernmental bodies outside the UN system also run paid placements, and combining those with your UN applications reduces the risk of an unfunded year. Keep in mind that the recruitment calendar differs by organisation, so a non-UN placement may be available in the months when UN rosters are quiet.

UN Internship Roster Systems and How They Actually Work

What is an internship roster and how does it work?

Many UN agencies maintain internship rosters that function as talent pools, allowing them to match candidates to positions as they arise rather than advertising each vacancy individually. Understanding how these rosters work is essential for applicants who want to maximise their chances of securing a placement. A roster is essentially a database of pre-screened candidates who have applied and been assessed as potentially suitable for internships within a particular agency or department. When an internship opportunity becomes available, the agency draws from the roster to identify candidates whose skills and qualifications match the position requirements, and these candidates are then contacted directly with an offer or invited to complete a shorter, targeted application process.

Which agencies use rosters, and how do I get on one?

The roster approach is used by several major UN agencies, including UNICEF, UNDP, UNHCR, WHO and the World Bank. To get on a roster, you typically need to complete an online profile, submit your academic credentials, work samples and a motivation statement, and pass a preliminary screening. Some agencies conduct interviews for roster inclusion, while others assess candidates based on their written submissions alone. The workload is heavier than a single application because the profile has to work across future vacancies, so budget several days for the first version rather than a single evening.

How long does roster membership last?

Once on the roster, you remain in the pool for a set period, usually two to three years, during which you may be contacted for internships that match your profile. Being on a roster does not guarantee an internship: it means you are in a shorter queue when a position opens, and the agency may still conduct additional interviews or assessments before making a final selection. There is also usually a clause that requires you to update your details or your registration can be closed, so set a calendar reminder for eleven months after you are added.

What do rosters offer candidates based in Pakistan?

For Pakistani applicants, roster-based internships offer a practical advantage because they allow you to express interest in multiple types of positions within a single agency and to be considered for opportunities across different locations. The UN presence in Pakistan is substantial, with agencies like UNDP, UNICEF, UNHCR, WHO and the FAO all operating field offices, which means that roster candidates in Pakistan may be considered for both headquarters and field-based positions. A candidate who registers for both categories is effectively doubling the number of possible matches from a single application.

How specific should my roster profile be?

Very. When completing your roster profile, be specific about the types of roles you are interested in, the skills you bring and the locations you are willing to work in. Generic profiles that do not specify a field or role are less likely to be matched to specific opportunities, because the matching is largely automated and keyword-driven. If your academic background is in a technical field, such as public health, environmental science or data analysis, highlight these skills prominently, as technical internships are often filled through rosters rather than open advertisements.

What are the weaknesses of relying on a roster alone?

The practical challenge with rosters is that they are opaque: you will not typically receive feedback on your application, and you may not hear back for months or even years, if at all. Do not treat a roster application as your only strategy. Apply to advertised vacancies, reach out to UN staff in your network for referrals and attend UN recruitment events. A roster gives you a shorter queue, but it is still a queue, and a candidate with no other activity on their file can sit in it indefinitely.

How do I keep a roster profile competitive?

The roster is one tool among many, and it works best when combined with active networking and direct applications. Keep your roster profile updated with new skills, languages, work experience and publications, as agencies may review existing roster candidates before advertising a new position externally. If you have not been contacted from a roster within two years, consider updating your profile or reapplying with stronger documentation. Log every new qualification, certificate and language upgrade against the profile the moment you gain it, so that the record a recruiter sees is current rather than two years old.

UN Internship Application Tips for Pakistani and Developing Country Candidates

What makes Pakistani candidates competitive for UN internships?

Pakistani applicants face a specific set of challenges when applying for UN internships, and understanding these challenges allows you to address them proactively in your application. The first is competition: UN internships receive applications from candidates worldwide, and Pakistani applicants are competing against graduates from universities in Europe, North America and other parts of Asia. Your advantage lies in demonstrating knowledge of the context in which the UN operates in Pakistan, including development challenges, humanitarian needs and governance issues. If you have academic or professional experience working on issues that UN agencies address in Pakistan, such as climate adaptation, education access, gender equality or public health, this gives you a concrete foundation that many international applicants lack.

Is being a Pakistani candidate seen as a disadvantage?

The second challenge is the perception of UN internships as being inaccessible to candidates from developing countries. This perception is partly outdated: many UN agencies now actively seek candidates from developing countries, and several agencies have target quotas for regional representation. The paid internship trend, which has been adopted by UNICEF, UNDP and other agencies, is partly driven by a recognition that unpaid internships exclude candidates who cannot afford to work without pay. When you apply, do not assume that your Pakistani background is a disadvantage: frame it as evidence that you understand the operational context the UN works in, that you have firsthand knowledge of the communities the UN serves and that your perspective as a Pakistani candidate adds diversity to the team.

Which languages should Pakistani candidates list on an application?

A third consideration is the language profile that UN agencies value. English is a working language of the UN, and fluency is required for most positions, but additional languages can be a significant advantage. Urdu is a valuable language for UN work in Pakistan, and if you speak Pashto, Sindhi, Punjabi or Balochi, these are assets for field-based positions that involve community engagement. Arabic, French and Spanish are the other major UN working languages, and proficiency in any of these opens doors to positions in regions where these languages are spoken. In your application, list all languages with honest self-assessments of your proficiency level, and if you have formal certifications (such as DALF for French or DELE for Spanish), include them. For Pakistani candidates who have studied or worked in multilingual environments, this is an opportunity to stand out.

How do I show the UN competency framework in my application?

The application itself requires careful attention to the UN specific competencies framework. UN agencies assess candidates against core competencies that include communication, planning and organising, teamwork, client orientation and conceptual clarity. When writing your application, structure your examples around these competencies and use the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) to describe your experience. Avoid vague claims about being a team player or a good communicator: provide specific examples that demonstrate these qualities in action. The framework is usually printed in the vacancy, so read it line by line and mirror its terminology in your own document.

Which examples are strongest for a UN internship application?

For Pakistani applicants, examples drawn from academic projects, community work, NGO internships or government interactions are all valid, as long as they show the competencies the UN values. Choose examples that have a beginning, a decision you made and an outcome you can name. Proofread your application carefully, as errors in a UN application are noticed and can undermine an otherwise strong candidacy. If possible, have a mentor or colleague review your application before submission, ideally someone who has either read many applications or worked inside an international organisation.

UN Volunteers Programme as an Alternative Entry Point

What is the United Nations Volunteers programme?

The United Nations Volunteers (UNV) programme is one of the most practical entry points into the UN system, and it is particularly well-suited to Pakistani candidates who want to gain UN experience before or instead of an internship. UNV places volunteers in UN agencies worldwide, with assignments ranging from two months to two years, and provides a monthly living allowance, health insurance and a travel grant. For candidates comparing routes into the UN, UNV is often overlooked because it is not called an internship, yet it carries a package, a duration and a level of responsibility that most internships cannot match.

Who can apply to the UNV programme?

The programme is open to candidates aged 25 and older with a bachelor degree and at least two years of professional experience, though younger candidates with relevant skills may also be considered. The experience requirement is the real filter: it means the route suits someone who has already worked, even in short-term or project-based roles, rather than a final-year student with no professional track record. Younger applicants can still succeed, but they will usually need unusually strong language skills, a technical specialisation or substantial volunteer or research experience to offset the missing years.

What fields and locations does UNV cover?

UNV assignments are available in a wide range of fields, including governance, education, health, environment, human rights and emergency response. Many assignments are based in field offices, which means you could be posted within Pakistan, in a neighbouring country or in another region entirely. Field-based UNV assignments are particularly valuable for Pakistani candidates, as they provide hands-on experience in the operational environments where UN agencies do their most visible work. The monthly stipend varies by location and is intended to cover basic living costs, not to provide a salary in the conventional sense, but for many Pakistani candidates the financial support makes volunteering sustainable in a way that unpaid internships are not.

How does the UNV application process work?

The application process for UNV involves creating an online profile, indicating your fields of interest and preferred locations and applying to specific assignments as they are posted. Successful candidates are matched to assignments based on their skills, experience and the needs of the UN agency requesting a volunteer. Because the same profile can be considered for many assignments, the accuracy of your profile matters more than the number of applications you submit, and it is worth revising it after any new qualification or posting.

How does UNV compare with a standard internship?

The advantages of the UNV route over a traditional internship include the financial support, the professional-level responsibility and the duration of the assignment. While internships typically involve supporting staff with tasks, UNV assignments often involve independent responsibility for specific outputs, such as writing reports, coordinating activities or managing small projects. This level of responsibility is more valuable for your professional development and for your CV, as it demonstrates that you can operate effectively within the UN system. Many UNV alumni have gone on to secure regular UN positions, as the volunteer experience provides both the skills and the network needed to compete for professional roles.

What should Pakistani candidates prepare before applying to UNV?

For Pakistani candidates considering the UNV route, there are a few practical considerations. The programme requires a commitment to the full duration of the assigned placement, so you should be prepared to relocate if necessary and to commit to the timeline. The application process can be slow, with several months between submission and placement, so patience is important. You will need to provide academic transcripts, references and a motivation statement, and some assignments require additional documents such as a valid passport and medical clearance. The UNV experience is most valuable when it aligns with your longer-term career goals, so choose assignments that build on your existing skills and introduce you to the areas of UN work you want to pursue. Pakistani candidates who have prior experience in development, governance or humanitarian work are particularly competitive for UNV assignments, as the programme values candidates who can contribute immediately.

Building a Strong UN Internship Application: Documents and Strategy

What are the three foundations of a strong UN internship application?

A strong UN internship application is built on three foundations: a compelling motivation letter, a well-structured CV and evidence that you understand the UN work and the specific agency mandate. These three components are assessed together, and a weakness in one is usually enough to sink an application no matter how strong the other two are. The most common failure pattern is a polished CV paired with a generic letter, which reads as a mismatch to any panel that has seen a few hundred files.

How should the motivation letter be structured?

The motivation letter should be tailored to the specific internship you are applying for, not a generic letter sent to every vacancy. Start by explaining why you are interested in that particular agency and that particular department, referencing recent reports, initiatives or campaigns that the agency has led. Then describe the specific skills and experience you bring, linking them directly to the internship description. Close by explaining what you hope to gain from the experience and how it will contribute to your career goals. Keep the letter to one page, use clear and concise language and avoid repetition.

What CV format does the UN expect?

The UN uses a specific CV format for some applications, and even when a custom format is not required, following the UN conventions helps your application pass initial screening. The UN CV format emphasises education, work experience, languages and skills in that order, with each entry including the title of the position or programme, the organisation, the dates of attendance or employment and a brief description of your responsibilities and achievements. Use action verbs to describe your experience: coordinated, analysed, facilitated, produced, managed. Quantify your achievements wherever possible: the number of people reached, the budget managed, the report produced. For Pakistani applicants, experience with government agencies, NGOs, research institutions and community organisations is all relevant, as long as it demonstrates competencies the UN values.

Which supporting documents should I attach?

Supporting documents strengthen your application significantly. Academic transcripts, certificates of completion for relevant training programmes, writing samples and publications all provide evidence of your capabilities. If you have research experience, including a writing sample such as a research paper, policy brief or analysis report allows the hiring panel to assess your analytical and communication skills directly. Put the most relevant document first, name files clearly, and keep a master folder of current certificates so that you can assemble a compliant set within hours rather than days when a vacancy closes.

How do references strengthen a UN internship application?

References are important: choose referees who know your work well and can speak specifically about your skills and achievements. A reference from a UN staff member, if you have one, carries additional weight, but a strong reference from a university professor or professional supervisor is equally valid. Ensure your referees are aware that they may be contacted and that they have agreed to provide a reference for you. Brief them with the vacancy title and the two or three competencies you most need them to speak to, because a generic reference wastes the one opportunity to influence the panel.

Can networking actually help me get UN internships?

Strategic networking is an often-overlooked element of the UN internship application process. Attending UN events, webinars and conferences, joining UN-related professional groups on LinkedIn and reaching out to current or former interns for advice can provide insights that improve your application. If you can identify and contact a staff member in the department you are applying to, a brief and professional email expressing your interest in the internship and asking for any guidance on the application process can make your application more visible. This approach must be handled with care: be respectful of the person's time, do not ask for a favour and keep the email short and focused. For Pakistani candidates, the UN presence in Pakistan means that there are often local events, workshops and networking opportunities that you can attend to build connections with UN staff in your area.

Common Mistakes in UN Internship Applications and Interview Preparation

What is the biggest mistake in a UN internship application?

The most common mistake in UN internship applications is submitting a generic application that does not address the specific role or agency. UN hiring panels review hundreds of applications for each internship, and applications that could have been written for any vacancy are immediately distinguishable from those that demonstrate genuine knowledge of the agency work. If your letter would still read well if the agency name were swapped out, it is not finished.

How do I show that I have researched the agency?

Before you apply, read the agency's recent strategic plan, annual report or key publications, and reference these in your motivation letter. Explain how your skills align with the specific tasks listed in the internship description, not just the agency's general mission. This level of specificity shows the panel that you have invested time in understanding the role and that you are applying thoughtfully rather than casting a wide net. One precise reference to a current programme does more work than a paragraph of general admiration, and it is the fastest way to be moved from the rejected pile to the read pile.

Why do applications fail to demonstrate the UN core competencies?

A second frequent error is failing to demonstrate the UN core competencies in your application. The UN assesses candidates against competencies such as communication, teamwork, planning and organising, professionalism and client orientation. Your application should provide concrete examples of each competency, drawn from your academic, professional or volunteer experience. If the internship description mentions specific competencies, address each one explicitly in your application. For Pakistani applicants, examples from community engagement, academic group projects, NGO work or government internships are all valid, as long as they show the competency in action. Avoid generic claims: instead of saying you are a good communicator, describe a specific situation where your communication skills produced a measurable outcome.

What should I expect in a UN internship interview?

Interview preparation for UN internships follows a different pattern than for private sector positions. UN interviews typically involve behavioural questions that probe your competencies, situational questions that test your judgement and technical questions related to the specific role. Prepare by reviewing the competency framework for the agency you are interviewing with, practising STAR-format answers to common behavioural questions and researching the specific issues the department works on. For Pakistani candidates, be prepared to discuss how your experience relates to the UN work in Pakistan, including development challenges, humanitarian responses and governance issues. The panel may also ask about your ability to work in multicultural teams, your experience with UN systems or tools and your availability for the full internship period.

Should I send a follow-up email after the interview?

Yes, it is a small but important step. After a UN interview, send a brief thank-you email to the panel or the contact person, reiterating your interest in the role and summarising one or two key points from the interview that you want to reinforce. This is not a common practice in all cultures, but it is appreciated in the UN context and can leave a positive impression. Keep it to a short paragraph, address it to the named contact, and proofread it, because the email itself becomes an additional sample of your written English.

What should I do if I am not selected?

If you are not selected, request feedback if possible: some agencies provide feedback to unsuccessful candidates, and the insights you gain can improve future applications. The UN recruitment process is lengthy, and it is common to apply multiple times before securing a position. Treat each application as a learning experience, refine your approach based on the feedback you receive and persist. Many successful UN professionals applied for internships and entry-level positions multiple times before securing their first role, and the persistence and self-improvement they demonstrated along the way are themselves evidence of the competencies the UN values.

Writing a UN Internship Motivation Letter That Stands Out

Why is the motivation letter the most important document?

The motivation letter is the most important document in a UN internship application, and it is where you distinguish yourself from the hundreds of other candidates who meet the basic eligibility requirements. A strong UN motivation letter does three things: it demonstrates genuine knowledge of the agency's work, it connects your specific skills and experience to the internship description and it explains how the experience will contribute to your career goals. Panels read it before the CV, and they read it quickly, so the first two lines have to earn the rest of the page.

How long should a UN internship motivation letter be?

The letter should be one page maximum, written in clear and concise English, and structured around the specific competencies and responsibilities listed in the internship advertisement. Avoid generic statements about wanting to work for the United Nations or about being passionate about international development: these are sentiments that every applicant expresses, and they do not differentiate you. A one-page limit is not a formality either; many systems reject over-length attachments outright, so check the stated limit and comply exactly.

How should I open the letter?

Start your letter by explaining why you are interested in that particular agency and that particular department. Reference specific reports, campaigns, initiatives or publications that the agency has produced, and explain how they connect to your academic or professional interests. This demonstrates that you have researched the agency and that your interest is specific rather than generic. Then describe the skills and experience you bring, linking each one directly to a requirement or responsibility listed in the internship description. Use concrete examples: instead of saying you have strong analytical skills, describe a specific analysis you conducted and the outcome it produced. Instead of saying you are a good communicator, describe a specific instance where your communication skills produced a measurable result. The third paragraph should explain how the internship will contribute to your career goals, showing that you have thought about how this experience fits into a longer professional trajectory.

How do you weave the UN competencies into the letter?

The UN specific competencies should be woven throughout your letter, not listed as a separate section. The core competencies that UN agencies assess include communication, teamwork, planning and organising, professionalism and client orientation. For each competency, provide one or two specific examples from your academic, professional or volunteer experience. If the internship description mentions specific competencies, address each one explicitly. Weaving them in rather than boxing them in keeps the letter readable, and it also gives the panel evidence rather than assertion.

How should you use the STAR method?

The STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) provides a useful structure for these examples, ensuring that you provide enough context for the reader to understand the situation, the action you took and the outcome you achieved. Keep each example to two or three sentences so that three or four fit on a single page. For Pakistani applicants, examples from academic research, community engagement, NGO work and government interactions are all valid, as long as they demonstrate the competencies in action.

How should I proofread and submit the letter?

Proofreading and presentation matter more in a UN application than in many other contexts. The UN receives applications from candidates around the world, and the quality of your English, including grammar, spelling and punctuation, is assessed as part of the evaluation. Errors in a motivation letter suggest carelessness or insufficient English proficiency, both of which can undermine an otherwise strong application. After you have drafted your letter, read it aloud to check for flow and clarity, ask a native English speaker to review it for grammar and idiomatic accuracy and check that it addresses every point listed in the internship description. Submit the letter in the format specified by the agency, usually as a PDF attachment, and ensure that your name and the internship reference number are clearly stated on the document.

UN Internship Work Environment and What to Expect on the Job

What is the UN work environment like?

Understanding the UN work environment before you begin your internship helps you adapt more quickly and make a stronger impression during your placement. The UN work culture varies between agencies and between headquarters and field offices, but several characteristics are broadly consistent. The UN operates in a multilingual, multicultural environment where English is the primary working language, but you will interact with colleagues who speak many different languages and come from diverse professional backgrounds. Coming from a country where workplaces are more hierarchical, the most common adjustment is learning to read the room in a culture that rewards restraint and courtesy in internal communication.

How does decision-making work at the UN?

Decision-making in the UN is often consensus-based and bureaucratic, which means that processes can be slower than in the private sector, but it also means that every voice is heard and every perspective is considered. Understanding this culture of deliberation, and adapting your working style to it, is an important part of a successful internship. Interns who get frustrated by the pace often make the mistake of trying to shortcut it; interns who succeed learn to document their reasoning so that consensus is faster to reach.

What tasks will I be assigned as an intern?

As an intern, you can expect to be assigned specific tasks and projects that contribute to the work of your team. These tasks may include research, data analysis, report writing, event coordination, translation, database management or communication support, depending on the department and the internship description. Most interns are surprised by how much of the work is written output, so get comfortable with briefing notes, meeting summaries and data tables early, and ask to see examples of formats the team already uses rather than inventing your own.

Why does initiative matter during a UN internship?

The most successful interns are those who take initiative: rather than waiting to be told what to do, they identify needs, propose solutions and volunteer for additional responsibilities. This proactive approach is valued in the UN system and can lead to additional responsibilities, stronger references and potential job offers. At the same time, respect the boundaries of your role: as an intern, you are there to learn and to contribute, not to make decisions or to bypass established processes. The balance is offering more, not claiming more.

How important is teamwork in a UN office?

The UN work environment includes a strong emphasis on teamwork and collaboration. You will work with colleagues from different units, departments and even agencies, and the ability to work effectively in a team is one of the core competencies that the UN assesses. Demonstrate your teamwork skills by being responsive to emails, meeting deadlines, sharing information with colleagues and contributing constructively to group discussions. The UN also values a culture of continuous learning: attend training sessions, workshops and seminars that are offered to staff and interns, and use them to build skills that are relevant to your career goals. Many UN offices have internal learning platforms that provide access to online courses, webinars and professional development resources, and taking advantage of these during your internship adds value to your experience beyond the tasks you are assigned.

What values should interns demonstrate at work?

For Pakistani interns, the UN work environment may feel different from the professional cultures you are accustomed to. The UN places a strong emphasis on gender equality, diversity and inclusion, and these values are reflected in everyday workplace interactions, hiring practices and programme design. Demonstrating awareness of and commitment to these values in your work and your interactions is important. The UN also has strict rules on impartiality, neutrality and conflict of interest, and as an intern you are expected to comply with these rules. Understanding the UN code of conduct, the standards of behaviour and the reporting mechanisms before you begin your internship prevents misunderstandings and ensures that you represent yourself and your country professionally throughout your placement.

UN Internship Platforms and Where to Find Current Openings

Where is the central UN careers portal?

Finding current UN internship openings requires knowing where to look, as opportunities are posted across multiple platforms depending on the agency. The UN careers portal (careers.un.org) is the central hub for vacancies across the UN system, and it lists internships alongside regular positions. However, many agencies also post internships on their own dedicated platforms, so the central portal is a starting point rather than a complete answer. Check it weekly, and use its alerts where available, because a large share of Secretariat-level internship adverts appear only there.

Which agency-specific platforms list UN internships?

UNICEF uses its own careers site, as does UNDP, UNHCR and WHO. The World Bank posts internship opportunities through its dedicated internship page, and the IMF uses its own recruitment system. Checking these platforms regularly, setting up job alerts and following the relevant agencies on professional networking sites ensures that you do not miss opportunities. Budget thirty minutes a week for this cycle, because the postings that matter for you are rarely clustered; they arrive steadily throughout the year.

Can you find UN internships on social media?

Social media is an underutilised resource for finding UN internship opportunities. Many UN agencies and departments use Twitter, LinkedIn and Facebook to announce internship openings, particularly for shorter-term or field-based positions that may not be advertised for long. Following the social media accounts of agencies you are interested in, as well as the accounts of specific departments or regional offices, can alert you to opportunities that you might otherwise miss. LinkedIn is particularly useful, as many UN staff members share internship opportunities in their professional networks, and engaging with UN-related content on the platform increases your visibility to UN recruiters.

When should I apply for UN internships?

The timing of your application matters. Many UN internships follow a seasonal recruitment cycle, with summer internships advertised between January and March and winter or spring internships advertised between August and October. However, some agencies recruit on a rolling basis, and field-based internships may be advertised at any time depending on operational needs. Applying early in the recruitment window, rather than waiting until the deadline, gives you an advantage, as many agencies begin reviewing applications as soon as they are received. Setting up a calendar reminder for the recruitment cycles of your target agencies ensures that you apply at the optimal time.

How can the UN presence in Pakistan help my search?

For Pakistani candidates, the UN in Pakistan maintains a local presence that can facilitate your search. The UN country team in Pakistan coordinates across agencies, and some agencies advertise local internship opportunities through their Pakistan offices. Contacting the human resources or admin section of UN agencies in Pakistan, expressing your interest and asking about upcoming internship opportunities can open doors that are not publicly advertised. Attending UN events, workshops and seminars in Pakistan also provides opportunities to meet staff members who can advise you on the application process and alert you to relevant vacancies. Building these local connections, combined with regular monitoring of the global platforms, creates a comprehensive search strategy that maximises your chances of finding and securing a UN internship.

Frequently asked questions

Are UN internships paid in 2026?+

Mostly yes at the big agencies. UNICEF pays around $1,000 per month plus a travel contribution, UNDP and UNHCR pay interns who are not otherwise sponsored, and WHO and FAO have paid schemes. Parts of the UN Secretariat still advertise unpaid roles, so check each vacancy.

Who is eligible for a UN internship?+

Students enrolled in an undergraduate or graduate degree program, or recent graduates (typically within two years), of any nationality. You usually need to be at least 18 and fluent in at least one UN working language, most often English.

How long do UN internships last?+

Most run two to six months. UNICEF internships run six to 26 weeks, and some agencies allow longer periods for academic credit or national service arrangements.

Where do I apply for UN internships?+

You apply separately to each agency: UNICEF on unicef.org/careers, UNDP on jobs.undp.org, UNHCR on unhcr.org/careers, WHO on its careers portal, and many bodies also advertise on careers.un.org.

Does a UN internship lead to a UN job?+

Not automatically. It is a strong credential and networking opportunity, but entry-level UN positions are extremely competitive. Treat it as one step in a longer career plan rather than a guaranteed doorway.
